Abstract
Experienced and less experienced soccer players were compared in terms of their
gaze behavior (number of fixations, fixation duration, number of fixation regions, and
distribution of fixations across specific regions) during frontal 1 vs. 1 defensive situations. Twenty-four men (eight experienced soccer players, eight less experienced
players and eight non-players) watched 20 video clips. Gaze behavior was registered
with an Eye Tracking System. The video scenes were analyzed frame-by-frame.
Significant main effect of the group (experience) was observed for the number of
fixation regions. Experienced soccer players had a lower number of fixation regions
than the non-soccer players. Moreover, the former group presented with significantly
larger percentage of fixations in the ball/foot region. These findings suggest that
experienced players may use a more efficient search strategy than novices, involving
fixation on a lesser number of areas in specific locations.
Keywords
Visual behaviors, soccer players, visual search strategy

Introduction
Visual perception is a key factor inﬂuencing the eﬃciency of motor activities in
many team sports. To obtain information from dynamically changing surroundings and to make quick decisions based on the analysis of many factors (such as
location of the ball, partners/opponents, used tactics, and physical performance), team sport athletes need to present with high levels of visual and perceptual skills (Williams, 2000). Research on visual behavior can contribute to
individualization and optimization of training methods in ball games, in the
case of which recognizing the opponent’s behavior plays a key role. This
refers both to individual sports associated with permanent involvement in 1
vs. 1 situations, such as tennis (Ward, Williams, & Bennett, 2002), table tennis
(Piras, Raﬃ, Lanzoni, Persiani, & Squatrito, 2015; Ripoll, 1989), and badminton
(Alder, Ford, Causer, & Williams, 2014), and to team sports, e.g. cricket (Land
& McLeod, 2000), baseball (Kato & Fukuda, 2002) and penalties in soccer (Kim
& Lee, 2006; Van der Kamp, 2011) or hockey (Williams, Ward, & Chapman,
2003).
Recently, attempts were made to assess the underlying mechanisms of visual
perception and cognitive skills, and to identify the parameters that contribute to
variance in soccer players’ expertise (Savelsbergh, Van der Kamp, Williams, &
Ward, 2005). Previous studies dealing with the problem in question centered on
a relationship between the visual skills of soccer players and their age, sport
skills (Du Toit, Kruger, Chamane, Campher, & Craﬀord, 2009; Ward &
Williams, 2003), and position in the ﬁeld (striker, midﬁelder, defender, goalkeeper) (Du Toit et al., 2009).

strategies utilized during a defensive play (11 vs. 11, 1 vs. 1) seemed to be
modulated by the player’s skills. Experienced players used more ﬁxations of
shorter duration than the inexperienced ones; interestingly, however, this did
not refer to 3 vs. 3 simulations. A 1 vs. 1 situation is an elementary component of
both attack and defense (Harrison, 2003). Eﬀective 1 vs. 1 play may give an
advantage on a given part of the pitch, such as overcoming a solid defensive
block, ﬁnal pass, or a shot at goal (Harrison, 2003).
A few interesting and well-designed studies (Williams, 2000; Williams &
Davids, 1998; Williams et al., 1994) demonstrated that visual search behavior
of soccer players is adjusted to situation during the game (oﬀensive vs. defensive), individual experience, and number of involved players. Moreover, visual
behavior of players involved in high-pressure defense (in the opponents’ half of
the ﬁeld) seems to diﬀer from that during low-pressure defense (in their own half
of the ﬁeld) (Roca, Ford, McRobert, & Williams, 2013). During high-pressure
defense, highly skilled players presented with shorter duration of ﬁxations and
larger number of ﬁxation locations than during low-pressure defense. In less
skilled players, however, the only diﬀerence in visual search behavior during
high- and low-pressure defense pertained to the number of ﬁxations, whereas
the number of ﬁxation locations and mean duration of ﬁxations were essentially
the same (Roca et al., 2013). Furthermore, McRobert, Ward, Eccles, and
Williams (2011) found that cricket batters reduce their mean ﬁxation time whenever situational information from the same bowler is available, and therefore
they rely less on body information.
Visual control strategies seem to depend on the motor task performed. Long
ﬁxation duration is a key factor during stationary tasks, such as playing billiards
(Williams, Singer, & Frehlich, 2002), golf putting (Vickers, 1992), dart throwing
(Vickers, Rodrigues, & Edworthy, 2000), and free throws in basketball (Vickers,
1996; Wilson, Vine, & Wood, 2009). However, in dynamic situations, late pickup of optical information becomes critical for the successful guidance of movements. Goal-directed actions in sport have been shown to be modulated online
to facilitate acquisition of appropriate perceptual information (e.g. Caraugh &
Janelle, 2002; De Oliveira, Oudejans, & Beek, 2006). These ﬁnding is consistent

with the theoretical Gibson’s (1986) ecological approach to perception and
action, according to which players who rely on information arising from their
opponent’s posture anticipate the eﬀect of action. In the present study, we used
an ecological approach in which visual attention is considered as the control of

Eﬀective 1 vs. 1 defensive play is a vital component of defense and as such
should be constantly developed. To be optimally prepared in terms of defensive
technique and tactics, players need to develop their visual search acuity to correctly predict their opponent’s intentions. However, to the best of our knowledge, only few previous studies analyzed a relationship between sport
experience of players and their visual behavior in 1 vs. 1 defensive situations
(Nagano et al., 2004; Williams & Davids, 1998). Some evidence (Williams &
Davids, 1998) suggests that in 1 vs. 1 simulations, experienced players more
frequently alternate their ﬁxations between the ball and the hip region, whereas
less skilled athletes seem to ﬁxate mostly on the ball. This implies that it is the
hip region which is particularly important in anticipating opponents’ movements, probably due to its proximity to the player’s center of gravity. Also a
review of visual search strategies used in 1 vs. 1 defensive situations (Nagano
et al., 2004) suggests that soccer players usually focus on their opponent’s knees
and hips. However, according to sports coaching literature (e.g. Harrison, 2003;
Luongo, 2000), in a 1 vs. 1 situation, defenders should concentrate on the ball,
rather than on the dribbling player’s body. Also the analyses of other defensive
situations, such as goalkeeper’s behavior during penalty kicks (Piras, 2010) or
detection of oﬀside position (Maruenda, 2004), revealed that each gaze should
both begin and end on the ball interest areas.
Inconclusive results of previous studies and scarcity of available data justify
further research on the problem in question. Establishing the most eﬀective
patterns of visual behavior during defense is vitally important from the sport
practice perspective. It is still unclear if players use various visual strategies in 1
vs. 1 situations, and if their visual habits diﬀer depending on the level of sport
experience. Moreover, the appropriateness of visual search strategies acquired
by players during training and competitive experience needs to be veriﬁed
against actual trends in teaching and development of defensive skills in soccer.
Consequently, the principal aim of this study was to compare the gaze control
of soccer players with various levels of expertise in 1 vs. 1 defensive situations.
We combined video-based analysis and eye-movement recording to determine
the skill-based diﬀerences in gaze behavior during 1 vs. 1 defensive soccer tasks.
Based on available evidence that experienced players display a greater taskspeciﬁc cognitive knowledge than the inexperienced ones (e.g. Ward &
Williams, 2003; Williams & Davids, 1998; Williams, Davids, Burwitz, &
Williams, 1993; Williams et al., 1994), we hypothesized that gaze behavior strategies will depend on sport expertise level. We expected that experienced players

Method
Participants
The study included 24 participants, among them are 8 experienced male soccer
players: 4 defenders and 4 midﬁelders (M age ¼ 22.2, SD ¼ 3.5), 8 less experienced male players: 4 defenders and 4 midﬁelders (M age ¼ 23.5, SD ¼ 4.1), and
8 non-players (M age ¼ 23.2, SD ¼ 4.0). Experienced players had been playing
soccer for 12.4 years (SD ¼ 1.5) on average, which corresponded to the mean
number of 612 (SD ¼ 80.3) competitive matches. All participants from this

matches, mostly in the Polish fourth and ﬁfth leagues. All participants from this
group were less experienced soccer players. The non-players group consisted of
the University of Szczecin students who did not take part in any form of systematic sport training.
Prior to the experiment, all participants were familiarized with the testing
protocol and gave their written informed consent to participate in the study.
None of the subjects declared any problems with the eyesight at the time of
testing. The protocol of the study was approved by the Local Bioethics
Committee at the Regional Medical Chamber in Szczecin.
Test video clip
Participants were presented a video clip with 20 oﬀensive soccer sequences and
asked to imagine themselves as a central defender in a 1 vs. 1 defensive situation.
Each sequence presented an attacking player dribbling toward the receding
camera and making (or not) diﬀerent feints before passing on the left or right
side. Six team players participated in the sequences to provide diﬀerent frontal 1
vs. 1 feints and movements for each scene. The dribbling scenes were recorded
on a regular soccer ﬁeld in a central sector in front of the penalty box. The
simulation was ﬁlmed at an eye-level (1.7 m) from a receding defensive player’s
point of view. A Sony GoPro camera (HERO 3 White Edition) worn by the
cameraman was used to record the scenes (mp4 format, 1080p, 30 fps,
1920  1080). This position provided the most representative visual display of
a defender in a 1 vs. 1 defensive situation. The dribblers were displayed on the
screen in a natural size.

Device
Gaze behavior was recorded with a wireless SensoMotoric Instruments (SMI
ETG 2w, Germany) transmitter, using an Eye Tracking System mobile binocular
consisting of glasses to track the eye movements and a controller to store the
video recording. The eye tracker consisted of three cameras built into the glasses:
binocular infrared LEDs responding to eye movement tracking, an infrared
camera to record the movements of each eye and a high-deﬁnition (HD)
camera (1280  960, 25fps) to record the visual scene. Eye movements during
a given gaze behavior were recorded using iViewETG version 2.2 software at
60 Hz. The data from the eye tracker were recorded with Samsung Galaxy S4
smartphone placed in a small backpack worn by the examined participant. A
simple point-eye calibration was conducted to verify the point-of-gaze before
testing of each study participant. The appropriateness of the calibration procedure was veriﬁed prior to each trial to provide adequate accuracy of the system.
The data were analyzed frame-by-frame using SMI BeGaze 3.5.101 software
installed on a PC Dell Precision T1700, Intel Xeon CPU E3-1225 v3
@3.2 GHz, 8 RAM memory. An oﬀset correction by reference point was activated to control the recording. Three search rate measures were analyzed: mean
ﬁxation duration per sequence, mean number of visual ﬁxations per sequence,
and mean number of ﬁxation regions per sequence.
Procedure
Prior to the presentation of the test clips, calibration procedure of the eye-tracking
device was conducted. Then, the participants had in ﬁve familiarization trials to
become accustomed to the device (especially the glasses). The data collected at this
stage were not included in the analysis. During the test, participants stood 4 m
from a large screen (3.5  3.5 m) on which the dribbling sequences were projected
using a BENQ PU 9730 projector located behind and above. Since previous
studies demonstrated that search strategies may be inﬂuenced by external instructions (Yarbus, 1967), the participants were only asked to watch the sequences as if
they were actually taking part in the game. They were allowed to remain in an
unrestrained position; although they were not speciﬁcally instructed to respond to
the opponent’s movements, they could move to the expected direction of action,
since the wireless Eye Tracking System does not constrain movement in any way
(Figure 1(a)). Prior to the presentation of each clip, the participant was asked to
gaze at a marker positioned in the center of the screen in order to control the
calibration status (Figure 1(b)). The recorded gaze activity clips, each approximately 5 s in duration, were collected for further analysis. The whole testing procedure, including instruction, calibration, and watching 20 oﬀensive sequences,
took about 4 minutes per each examined person. The oﬀensive players shown in
the video clips were not known to the participants.

Analysis
Each participant (eight experienced players, eight less experienced players, and
eight non-players) watched 20 video clips. All 480 video scenes were analyzed
frame-by-frame and assessed. The number, duration, and regions of all gaze
ﬁxations were determined. Also the percentage viewing time of the display
areas was calculated for each sequence. Seven regions of ﬁxation were identiﬁed
on the display to categorize the gaze position (Figure 2): (1) zone in front of the
ball/foot, (2) ball/foot, (3) knee/shin, (4) thigh/hip, (5) trunk/arm, (6) head, and
(7) zone beyond the player and ball. According to other authors (Johansson,
Westling, Ba¨ ckstro¨ m, & Flanagan, 2001), gaze ﬁxation is deﬁned as the period
between the end of one saccade and the onset of another saccade.
The results were analyzed with STATISTICA software (v 10.0). Descriptive
statistics (means  standard deviations) and the distribution of all ﬁxations
across seven speciﬁc regions were analyzed. One-way analysis of variance
(ANOVA) with group factor and Kruskal–Wallis H test for non-parametric
statistics were used. Signiﬁcant main eﬀects were examined using Bonferroni
and Kruskal–Wallis post hoc tests, respectively. Eﬀect sizes were reported as
eta squared (Z2) values. Statistical signiﬁcance alpha level was set at p < .05.

First, we determined quantitative characteristics of visual search behaviors for
each study group, namely the number of ﬁxations, ﬁxation duration, and
number of ﬁxation regions. Then, we analyzed the distribution of ﬁxations
across seven speciﬁc regions: (1) in front of the ball/foot, (2) ball/foot, (3)
knee/shin, (4) thigh /hip, (5) trunk/arm, (6) head, and (7) beyond the player
and ball.
We hypothesized that experienced players demonstrate more eﬀective gaze
behavior strategy involving more ﬁxations of shorter duration to fewer areas at
speciﬁc locations within the visual ﬁeld than their less experienced peers.
Surprisingly, this hypothesis was not fully conﬁrmed by the study ﬁndings,
since the analyzed groups did not diﬀer signiﬁcantly in the number of ﬁxations
and mean ﬁxation duration. Consequently, the ﬁndings remain in opposition to
the results of previous studies (Williams, 2000; Williams & Davids, 1998;
Williams et al., 1994) in which experienced players were shown to use more
ﬁxations of shorter duration than their inexperienced peers, both in 1 vs. 1
and 11 vs. 11 situations. Interestingly, however, no signiﬁcant intergroup diﬀerences in ﬁxation number, ﬁxation duration, and number of ﬁxation locations
were found if players with various levels of experience were subjected to 3 vs. 3
simulations (Williams & Davids, 1998). Both ﬁxation frequency and ﬁxation
duration are considered to reﬂect information-processing demand during a
given task (Williams, Davids, & Williams, 1999). The participants of our
study watched 20 video sequences with diﬀerent oﬀensive actions performed
by unknown dribbling players. Previous experiments demonstrated that duration of the ﬁxation may be shorter if a situational information from the
same player was earlier available (McRobert et al., 2011) or under high pressure
(Roca et al., 2013), which was not used in our study. Perhaps, the lack of
intergroup diﬀerences was somehow related to the study procedure.
Nevertheless, we found a signiﬁcant intergroup diﬀerence in the number of
ﬁxation regions. Experienced soccer players ﬁxated on fewer regions (p < .05)
than non-players, which implies that the former group used a more eﬀective gaze
strategy. These ﬁndings are consistent with the results of previous studies showing that due to their greater experience, elite players can use more eﬀective and
eﬃcient tactical solutions than their less skilled peers (Farrow & Reid, 2012;
Milazzo et al., 2016; Ward & Williams, 2003; Williams & Davids, 1998; Williams
& Eliot, 1999; Williams et al., 1993, 1994). Williams and Davids (1998) observed
that elite soccer players were faster and more accurate in recognizing and recalling patterns of play in defensive simulations (i.e. 11 vs. 11, 3 vs. 3, 1 vs. 1).
Furthermore, they were better in anticipating their opponents’ actions based on
contextual information (e.g. acquisition of postural information, conﬁguration
of the game), which was associated with the use of an optimal gaze strategy.
These elaborated cognitive structures direct visual search toward task-speciﬁc
information (Roca et al., 2013). According to Ericsson and Kintsch (1995),
experts can faster access and retrieve model situations stored in their long10

term memory structures. As a result, expert’s action is characterized by lower
number of choices, and therefore, both selection and execution of the response
occurred more rapidly (Thomas, French, & Humphries, 1986). In dynamic situations, such as defense, experts are more likely to identify and to refer to the
information sources that are relevant for their actions, while abandoning those
that are currently irrelevant (e.g. Abernethy, 1996; Williams et al., 1999).
In the present study, experienced soccer players were characterized by signiﬁcantly greater (p< .05) percentage of ﬁxations in the ball/foot region than nonplayers. This observation is inconsistent with the results of previous studies
analyzing visual search strategies of soccer players in 1 vs. 1 situations.
According to Williams and Davids (1998), experienced players more often ﬁxate
on the hip region during 1 vs. 1 simulations, which suggests that this area is particularly important for predicting their opponent’s movements. Moreover, experienced players were more prone to alternate their ﬁxations between the ball and the
hip region (see also Williams, 2000). According to Nagano et al. (2004), elite players
ﬁxate more frequently on the knee and hip regions than novices. This clearly shows
that defensive visual behavior depends on the situation on the pitch and character
of the action. From a practical perspective, a defensive action should be aimed at
regaining possession of the ball, preventing the opponent from advancing the ball,
and reaching the goal. Defenders need to adopt their postures and movements to
actively inFuence the opponent’s actions. Importantly, playing against dribbling
players, the defender should keep eyes on the ball at all times (Harrison, 2003;
Luongo, 2000). According to Piras, Lobietti, et al. (2014), the defender in ball
sport anchors the fovea close to the ball areas to be able to use the parafovea
and visual periphery to pick up relevant information.
Soccer is an acyclic sport, which requires the use of speciﬁc skills in various
situations. During the game, the soccer player changes direction of movement
every 2–4 seconds, which corresponds to a total of 1200–1400 changes per match
(Bangsbo, 1992). Such rapid activity necessitates visual control of the ball. In a
study of 4 vs. 4 situations (Savelsbergh et al., 2010) comparing the number of
interceptions and assuming the correct position after the interception, the best
results were obtained by the players who observed the ball and its immediate
surroundings, whereas those who observed the player receiving the ball and the
trunk and hands of the player passing the ball, scored worst. Williams (2000)
observed that players (both experienced and novice) maintained ﬁxations on the
player in possession of the ball or on the ball itself during 3 vs. 3 simulations,
with relatively few foveal ﬁxations on the other defenders or attackers. The
current ﬁndings support the suggestions that in learning 1 vs. 1 strategies, defensive players should concentrate on the ball and lower body of the dribbling
player.
Some limitations of the present study need to be mentioned. The study groups
were relatively small and included solely males. The participants watched 20
video scenes taken from preset locations and distances under experimental

In conclusion, the ﬁndings suggest that experienced soccer players use a more
eﬃcient search strategy than novices, involving ﬁxation on lesser number of
areas at speciﬁc locations. However, the question of optimal visual behavior
still remains open and needs to be addressed during the course of future
research. Nevertheless, the study provided information for both soccer training
practice and science. Since the ﬁxation location is assumed to reﬂect the important cues used in decision-making in motor behavior (Williams et al., 1999), we
believe that practical implications of both current experiment and future studies
will serve as guidelines to improve the training process of future elite athletes.
