Aiming to Deceive: Examining the Role of the Quiet
Eye During Deceptive Aiming Actions
Greg Wood,1 Samuel J. Vine,2 Johnny Parr,3 and Mark R. Wilson2
1Manchester Metropolitan University; 2University of Exeter; 3Liverpool Hope University
In three experiments, we explored the use of deceptive gaze in soccer penalty takers using eye-tracking
equipment. In Experiment 1, players competed against a goalkeeper while taking unconstrained shots. Results
indicated that when players used deception (looking to the opposite side to which they shot), they extended the
duration of their ﬁnal aiming (quiet eye) ﬁxation and maintained shooting accuracy. In Experiment 2, with no
goalkeeper present, players still used extended quiet-eye durations when using a deceptive strategy, but this
time, their accuracy suffered. In Experiment 3, we manipulated the goalkeeper’s location while controlling for
the use of peripheral vision and memory of goal size. Results indicated that increased quiet-eye durations were
required when using deceptive aiming, and that accuracy was inﬂuenced by the position of the goalkeeper. We
conclude that during deceptive aiming, soccer players maintain accuracy by covertly processing information
related to the goalkeeper’s location.
Keywords: deception, gaze, head fakes, penalty kicks, peripheral vision, soccer
The ability to predict the mental state and behavioral
intentions of others from their gaze direction has been
fundamental to our evolutionary success (Emery, 2000).
Accordingly, humans have developed a predisposition to
focus on the gaze direction of others when attempting to
interpret and anticipate their future actions (Blakemore,
Winston, & Frith, 2004). For example, Nummenmaa,
Hyönä, and Hietanen (2009) have shown that pedestrians use eye movements to indicate their intended
direction of travel and that approaching pedestrians
use this information to avoid collisions. However, the
predisposition to focus on the gaze of others can be
exploited through the use of deceptive gaze behaviors
that aim to disguise the deceiver’s future intentions
(Emery, 2000). Such deceptive behaviors are readily
observable
in
sport
(Güldenpenning,
Kunde,
&
Weigelt, 2017).
In a sporting context, these deceptive behaviors are
often referred to as “head fakes” (e.g., basketball;
Kunde, Skirde, & Weigelt, 2011) and are characterized
by performers faking gaze in one direction while shooting or passing to another. At the heart of their use is a
deliberate attempt to misdirect the attention of an opponent and negatively inﬂuence their responsive actions to
create a performance advantage. Despite their frequency
in sport, there have been few empirical studies that have
explored the cognitive factors behind their use (Kunde
et al., 2011). Those that have done so have focused on
expert and novice differences in the ability to detect
deceptive actions across a range of sports, including
handball (Ca˜nal-Bruland & Schmidt, 2009; Ca˜nalBruland, van der Kamp, & van Kesteren, 2010), rugby
(Jackson, Warren, & Abernethy, 2006), basketball
(Sebanz & Shiffrar, 2009), and soccer goalkeeping
(Dicks, Uehara, & Lima, 2011; Smeeton & Williams,
2012; Tomeo, Cesari, Aglioti, & Urgesi, 2012). The
consensus from these studies suggests that experts are
better able to detect deceptive behaviors, and this advantage seems to be attributable to their superior experience
in both perceiving and performing the observed actions
(Canal-Bruland et al., 2010). Although the ability of the
observer to detect deceptive actions has obvious and
important implications for performance in sport, there
are consequences of employing such behaviors from the
deceiver’s perspective.
Evidence for such implications comes from both
motor control research suggesting that visually guided
motor performance is optimized when the gaze system
provides advanced target information to the motor system (Land, 2009) and from cognitive psychology
research suggesting that deceptive behaviors are inherently more cognitively complex (Zuckerman, DePaulo,
& Rosenthal, 1981). Therefore, employing head fakes—
which disassociate gaze from aiming intention—should
have a detrimental impact on performance, particularly
in aiming tasks where the processing of target-related

aiming intention impairs performance in simple visuomotor tasks, such as pointing (Neggers & Bekkering,
2001), and in more dynamic tasks, such as driving
(Wilson, Chattington, & Marple-Horvat, 2008). Therefore, any beneﬁt of employing head fakes arising from
increasing the complexity of the observer’s decisionmaking process must be weighed up against the
increased difﬁculty of performing the skill while dissociating gaze and intention.
In sport, this coordination of gaze and motor
systems has received much research attention in the
exploration of key perceptual-cognitive variables that
underpin expert performance (see Mann, Williams,
Ward, & Janelle, 2007 for a review). One such measure,
the quiet eye (QE; Vickers, 1996)—deﬁned as the
duration of the ﬁnal ﬁxation toward a relevant target
before the initiation of a critical movement—has been
proposed to provide a period of cognitive preprogramming of movement parameters (force, direction, and
velocity) immediately before an aiming skill is performed (Vickers, 1996, 2007). As task complexity
and preprogramming demands increase, so does the
QE period (Klostermann, Kredel, & Hossner, 2013;
Sun, Zhang, Vine, & Wilson, 2016; Williams, Singer,
& Frehlich, 2002; Wood, Vine, & Wilson, 2013). It is
therefore probable that deceptive visuomotor actions
will also require such extensions of cognitive preprogramming to compensate for the dissociation between
gaze and aiming intention and the resultant increase in
task demands that arise.
Furthermore, this dissociation is likely to have
implications for the overt and covert attentional processes used during QE durations. Unlike typical aiming
skills where the target is ﬁxated during (e.g., darts) or
before (e.g., golf putting) the critical movement using
overt attentional processes (via foveal vision; Vickers,
2007), deceptive aiming actions are likely to rely on
covert attentional processes (via peripheral vision) to
extract target-related information necessary to parameterize the shot and ensure accuracy. Interestingly, covert
attentional processes have also been shown to increase
QE aiming durations (Querfurth, Schücker, de Lussanet,
& Zentgraf, 2016) possibly due to the inhibition of notto-be parameterized movement variants (Klostermann,
Kredal, & Hossner, 2014). Taken together, this may
suggest a degree of ﬂexibility regarding the attentional
processes underpinning the QE and the type of information that can be used during QE durations to guide
accurate aiming.
One sporting task where deceptive eye movements
have been recently shown to be prevalent is the soccer
penalty kick. Early research (Kuhn, 1988) identiﬁed two
aiming strategies: keeper dependent (KD), where the
kicker focuses on the movements of the goalkeeper and
responds by shooting in the opposite direction to which
he or she dives, and keeper independent (KI), where the
kicker aligns gaze with aiming intention and looks where
he or she intends to shoot. Wood and Wilson (2010b)
uncovered a third strategy, which they labeled the
opposite independent strategy, characterized by players
looking to the opposite side of the goal to which they
shoot, a behavior that is often colloquially referred to in
soccer as “giving him or her the eyes.” At the heart of
this strategy is the deliberate attempt to deceive or
mislead the goalkeeper to disguise the kicker’s true
aiming intention (Wood & Wilson, 2010b).
An intriguing ﬁnding from this study was that
players were just as accurate when employing head
fakes as they were when using the KI strategy (looking
where they intended to shoot). This is surprising given
the consensus of experimental research that has explored
the visual aiming strategies of soccer penalty takers,
which suggests that shooting accuracy is signiﬁcantly
impaired if players decouple gaze and aiming intention while shooting, for example, inducing aiming ﬁxations to the goalkeeper causes shots to be more centrally
distributed (Binsch, Oudejans, Bakker, & Savelsbergh,
2010; van der Kamp, 2011; Wilson, Wood, & Vine,
2009; Wood & Wilson, 2010a, 2010b, 2011, 2012).
Therefore, how do players maintain shooting accuracy
despite dissociating gaze from aiming intention when
employing head fakes? What compensatory visual or
attentional processes are involved in protecting performance? and Why is this decoupling strategy not as
debilitating to performance as the KD approach?
In the following three experiments, we aim to
provide an examination of the attentional and/or performance costs behind deception in soccer penalty shooting. This represents an intriguing shift in research focus
from the perceiver to the deceiver and may offer an
insight into the compensatory processes that underpin
head fakes in aiming-based tasks. In Experiment 1, we
extend the work of Wood and Wilson (2010b) by
examining the gaze behavior of penalty takers and the
effects of these on the anticipatory performance of the
goalkeeper. We hypothesized that when players used a
deceptive strategy, they would be able to maintain
accuracy as long as they lengthened their QE aiming
ﬁxations (to the opposite side of the goal) to compensate
for increased task demands (as Klostermann et al., 2013;
Williams et al., 2002). We further hypothesized that the
deceptive nature of the strategy would negatively affect
the anticipatory performance of the goalkeeper, causing
him or her to dive to the wrong side on more occasions
than when using the KI strategy (as Dicks et al., 2011).

Methods

one experienced goalkeeper (age = 31 years; experience
= 24 years) gave written consent to partake in the study
after procedures were approved by the ethics committee
at the University of Exeter.1

Apparatus.
A standard soccer ball (size 5) was shot
from the standard penalty kick distance (11 m) toward
a full-sized soccer goal (7.32 m wide × 2.44 m high) that
was marked out on an adjacent wall. To allow the
goalkeeper to dive freely and to reduce the potential
for injury, gym mats (32-mm thickness) were used to
cover the full width of the goal. Participants were ﬁtted
with an Mobile Eye Tracker (Applied Science Laboratories, Bedford, MA), which measures eyeline of gaze
at 25 Hz, with respect to eye and scene cameras,
mounted on a pair of glasses. The system incorporates
a recording device (a modiﬁed digital videocassette
recorder), worn in a pouch around the waist, and a
laptop (Dell Inspiron 6400, TX) installed with “EyeVision” recording software. A circular cursor, representing 1° of visual angle with a 4.5-mm lens, indicating
the location of gaze in a video image of the scene (with
spatial accuracy of ±0.5° visual angle and 0.1° precision) is recorded for ofﬂine analysis.
Procedure.
To familiarize participants to the testing
environment and apparatus, each participant attended
individually and took 10 practice penalty kicks with no
goalkeeper present. Participants were then calibrated to
the eye tracker using locations on the goal, took a single
block of 10 penalty kicks against the goalkeeper and
were told that they should try to score as many kicks as
possible, similar to real competition. This procedure was
repeated over 3 consecutive weeks resulting in an individual sample of 30 kicks per participant and an overall
potential sample of 300 kicks. To minimize errors in data
collection, calibration was checked after every kick.
Previous research (Wood & Wilson, 2010b) has shown
that by allowing penalty takers to take a series of kicks,
they naturally alternate the shooting strategies they use,
and this allowed us to produce a mix sample of kicks
without giving any instruction to the players. During
testing, the goalkeeper was asked to attempt to save as
many shots as possible and was given a minimum of
10-min rest between participants to prevent fatigue.
Shooting strategies.
Each shot taken was categorized
into one of the three strategies outlined in previous
research (e.g., Wood & Wilson, 2010b), based on the
location of the ﬁnal (QE) ﬁxation prior to the initiation of
the run-up. Speciﬁcally, KD, ﬁnal ﬁxation on the goalkeeper; KI, ﬁnal ﬁxation on the target area to which the
ball was kicked; and the deceptive kicking strategy
(previously referred to as the opposite independent strategy; Wood & Wilson, 2010b), ﬁnal ﬁxation to one side of
the goal with a resulting shot to the opposite side.
Measures.
Quiet eye: Each half of the goal was divided
into six zones of 61 cm, and these were used to map the
location of the QE ﬁxation from each post (0 cm) to the
center of the goal (183 cm). Therefore, lower numbers
represent a distal aiming strategy (i.e., further away from
the goalkeeper). QE aiming ﬁxations were determined
via frame-by-frame analysis of the eye tracker video ﬁle
using Quiet Eye Solutions analysis software (www.
quieteyesolutions.com) and were deﬁned as the last
ﬁxation on the goal that started prior to, and extended
beyond, the initiation of the run-up (as Wood & Wilson,
2011). This deﬁnition of the QE therefore reﬂects the
ﬁnal ﬁxation of the aiming phase that often extends into
the early part of the execution phase (run-up). A ﬁxation
was classiﬁed as three or more consecutive frames
(≥120 ms) in which the cursor stayed in the same
location (Vickers, 1996). The QE was therefore deﬁned
in terms of its location (distance from the post) and
duration (in milliseconds). Interrater reliability of 85
shots revealed an agreement of 98%.
Performance: Shots hit closer to the post and further
from the goalkeeper are more likely to be successful
(Bar-Eli & Azar, 2009; Kerwin & Bray, 2006), we
measured shooting accuracy by measuring the distance
in centimeters from shot placement (the center of the
ball) to the post. Therefore, lower values reﬂected shots
that were hit closer to the post and further from the
goalkeeper. Shots that missed the goal were not given an
accuracy score and those that were saved were given an
estimated score relating to where they would have hit the
goal (Wood & Wilson, 2011). The location of a shot was
determined via frame-by-frame analysis of the eye
tracker video ﬁle using Quiet Eye Solutions analysis
software with a precision of 5 cm (approximately one
quarter the diameter of the ball) in the same manner as
the location of the QE data. Estimates of shooting
accuracy from a sample of 72 shots revealed an interrater
reliability of 96%. The number of goals, saves, and
misses were recorded for each strategy, as was the
number of times that the goalkeeper moved or dived
to the opposite side to which the players shot the ball.
These tallies were converted to percentages (of the total
number of shots taken using the strategy) for subsequent
comparisons.
Data analysis.
A one-way repeated-measures analysis
of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences
in the location and duration of QE, and the shooting
accuracy for each of the three shooting strategies was
identiﬁed by Wood and Wilson (2010b). Signiﬁcant
effects were followed up with Bonferroni-corrected
pairwise comparisons, and effect sizes were reported
using eta-squared statistics (η2). To assess the effectiveness of each strategy, the outcome of the shot (goal, miss,
and save) and the direction that the goalkeeper dived
(correct/incorrect side to the eventual shot) were analyzed using hierarchical log-linear regression, and signiﬁcant interactions were followed up using chi-squared
tests at each level of the variable. Effect sizes were
reported using Cramer’s V statistics with 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5
representing small, medium, and larger effect sizes,
respectively (Cohen, 1988).

Discussion
In this ﬁrst experiment, we investigated the gaze
strategies of soccer players as they attempted to score
penalties against a goalkeeper in an unconstrained environment. Speciﬁcally, we hoped to examine if deceptive
gaze behavior was effective in deceiving the goalkeeper
and if compensatory extensions to preprogramming
visual attention protected against any accuracy impairment caused by the dissociation between gaze and
aiming intention. The results of this ﬁrst experiment
partially supported our hypotheses, with players exhibiting signiﬁcantly longer QE durations when employing
the deceptive strategy compared with when they aligned
gaze with aiming intention (KI strategy).
One potential caveat to this interpretation is our
assumption that the extended QE durations seen in
deceptive kicks are indicative of supplementary and
compensatory preprogramming. An alternative explanation for these extended QE durations could be that
players are deliberately attempting to exaggerate their
gaze behavior (and deceptive intention) to “sell” the
head fake to the goalkeeper. In fact, exaggerated gaze
behaviors are synonymous with deception (e.g., lie
detection; Mann, Vrij, Leal, Granhag, Warmelink, &
Forrester, 2012) and deceptive actions (e.g., magic;
Rieiro, Martinez-Conde, & Macknik, 2013), and it
has previously been shown that soccer penalty takers
do use exaggerated body cues when attempting to fool
the goalkeeper (Smeeton & Williams, 2012). An “exaggeration” explanation, however, does not explain how
players maintained their shooting accuracy despite the
dissociation of gaze from aiming intention that is synonymous with poor performance in this task (Binsch
et al., 2010; van der Kamp, 2011; Wilson et al., 2009;
2010a, Wood & Wilson, 2010b, 2011, 2012). A further
potential explanation is that players used information

A further interesting ﬁnding from Experiment 1 was
that there was no signiﬁcant difference between QE
durations when players used a deceptive strategy compared with when they used a KD strategy (Table 1), yet
the accuracy scores between these strategies differed
signiﬁcantly (Table 2). Although both strategies purposefully dissociate gaze from aiming intention prior to
shooting, the effect that this had on performance suggests
that there are fundamentally different attentional processes being used to guide the eventual kick. Speciﬁcally,
we propose that the deceptive strategy is an aiming
strategy where auxiliary target-related information, extracted through covert attentional processes, is being
processed to guide accurate shooting. On the other
hand, the KD strategy is an overt attentional strategy
that is reﬂective of an anticipatory, temporally constrained behavior that is linked to the movements of
the goalkeeper (Van der Kamp, 2011). By ﬁxating on the
goalkeeper, it may be possible to detect movement cues
regarding which way he or she might dive and therefore
reduce the demands for accurate aiming (shooting into
the now open goal on the other side). However, previous
research has suggested that the goalkeeper-dependent
strategy is associated with a loss of self-reported control
(Wood & Wilson, 2012) and can be problematic if the
goalkeeper delays moving (van der Kamp, 2006).
Finally, when players utilized deceptive gaze, they
signiﬁcantly disrupted the anticipatory performance of
the goalkeeper, with him diving to the opposite direction
to which the ball was shot on signiﬁcantly more occasions (54%) compared with when players shot with
nondeceptive KD (30%) and KI (34%) strategies.
Although the effectiveness of deceptive behaviors
for negatively biasing goalkeeper anticipation has
been reported in previous studies (e.g., Smeeton &
Williams, 2012), this study highlights further beneﬁts
of using deception when taking soccer penalties. Not
only players are more likely to deceive the goalkeeper,
who tends to ﬁxate the penalty taker’s head during this
aiming period (see Dicks, Button, & Davids, 2010), but
they can be just as accurate in their shooting by exaggerating this long ﬁxation to the “wrong” side.
In summary, in this ﬁrst experiment, we showed
that the maintenance of shooting accuracy seen when
using the deceptive strategy seemed to be underpinned
by extended QE durations that provide covert supplementary preprogramming time and resources as a way of
compensating for the decoupling of gaze and aiming
intention. In Experiment 2, we hoped to solidify these
conclusions by removing the need for deception, and by
removing the goalkeeper, to control for exaggeration
effects of increased QE duration.
Experiment 2
Methods
Participants.
In total, 25 right-footed male university
soccer players (Mage = 20.7 years, SD = 1.69), who were
not involved in Experiment 1, volunteered to take part.
All participants gave written consent to partake in the
study after procedures were approved by the ethics
committee of Liverpool Hope University.
Apparatus.
The same goal dimensions were marked
out on a plain wall as in Experiment 1. Participants were
ﬁtted with a Mobile Eye-5 Eye Tracker (Applied Science
Laboratories), which measures eyeline of gaze at 30 Hz.
Procedure.
Participants attended the lab individually
and were ﬁrst given 10 practice kicks before being
calibrated to the eye tracker using nine calibration points
located on the goal. The KI and deceptive kicking
strategies were then explained to the participants, and
they were told that they would be required to take kicks,
while alternating these strategies. Participants always
looked to the right-hand side bottom corner of the goal
(from their perspective) but alternated where they shot to
depending on the strategy employed. They then took ﬁve
practice shots using each strategy. Each participant then
took 30 kicks (two counterbalanced blocks of 15 kicks)
using alternate kicking strategies. This produced a total
sample of 750 kicks (375 for each strategy). After each
shot, the experimenter reminded the kicker of the next
strategy to be used. During the experiment, participants’
eye movements were monitored on a laptop to ensure
that they adhered to the gaze instructions and only looked
where directed prior to shooting. When they did not
adhere to these instructions, they were told that the kick
was invalid, and they were required to retake the shot.
Measures and Data Analysis.
Shooting accuracy was
measured using Kinovea video analysis software (http://
www.kinovea.org/), and interrater reliability scores,
based on a sample of 50 shots, was 94%. Using a scaling
factor that was visible in the scene view of the eye tracker
(i.e., the height of the post), we measured the distance (in
centimeters) from the center of the ball when it hit the
wall and the post. QE durations were measured in
exactly the same manner as Experiment 1. Gaze data
for two participants were lost due to corrupt video ﬁles.
Interrater reliability scores for the location and duration
data on a sample of 50 shots were 98% and 93%,
respectively. Paired sample t tests were used to explore
differences in QE locations, QE durations, and shooting
accuracy across KI and deceptive kicking strategies.
Effects sizes were reported using Cohen’s d statistic
where 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 represented small, medium, and
large effect sizes, respectively (Cohen, 1988).

Discussion
This second experiment was designed to explore the
possibility that the extensions of QE durations seen in
Experiment 1 were attributable to the preprogramming
of more complex shot information due to the deliberate
decoupling of gaze and aiming intention, rather than
merely trying to exaggerate the “head fake” to the
goalkeeper. Our data, at least partially, support the
former rather than latter explanation. With no goalkeeper
present, there was no longer any need for the players to
attempt to be deceptive and exaggerate their gaze, yet
they still extended their QE durations. From this, we
conclude that the increased complexity of the shotimposed supplementary processing demands on the
players resulting in extensions to QE aiming durations
(Klostermann et al., 2013; Williams et al., 2002).
However, in contrast to Experiment 1, these extensions to QE aiming durations did not help kickers to
maintain shooting accuracy when using deceptive kicks,
as shots taken with this strategy became signiﬁcantly
centralized (∼23 cm) and therefore less accurate compared with KI shots. One explanation for this reduction
in accuracy may be that eliminating prekick scanning
behavior meant that players did not have the necessary
information to guide their shot. However, as relativity,
few shots were taken in Experiment 1 using this behavior, it is unlikely that this can explain the effect.
A further explanation is that some important information, which players depended on to help guide their
shot in Experiment 1, was missing in Experiment 2: the
goalkeeper (Masters, van der Kamp, & Jackson, 2007).
Consequently, players could have resorted to using
peripheral vision and/or memory of the goal dimensions
to try to maintain shooting accuracy to the opposite side
of the goal. Interestingly, these explanations may explain
why shots became less accurate. First, targets focused on
in the periphery look smaller than targets focused on in
the fovea (Newsome, 1972). Therefore, if the players
resorted to using peripheral vision to guide their shot, the
goal would have looked smaller than it actually was,
which could impair shooting performance (Wood,
Jordet, & Wilson, 2015). Similarly, using an internal
representation of the dimensions of the goal to guide
their shot is inefﬁcient as previous research has suggested that “stored target information serving memoryguided action is susceptible to a compression of visual
space in memory such that the egocentric distance of a
remembered target is underestimated” (Heath & Binsted,
2007, p. 169). For the ﬁnal experiment, we devised a
penalty-kicking task that would allow an exploration of
the importance of the spatial location of the goalkeeper
during deceptive penalty shots while reducing the contribution of information in peripheral vision and
memory.
Experiment 3
Research in penalty taking has highlighted that penalty
takers are very sensitive to the spatial relationship
between the goalkeeper’s position and the goal posts
that constrain the target area (Masters et al., 2007).
Indeed, these authors demonstrated that when a goalkeeper creates even 0.5% differences in space in the goal
by standing marginally off-center, penalty takers can
reliably discriminate which side has more space at above
chance levels, despite reporting that his or her decisions
are guessed (see also Noël, van der Kamp, & Memmert,
2015). It is therefore likely that the goalkeeper’s position
provides information critical for the processing of spatial
parameters important for aiming. Therefore, we suggest
that to simplify the computation problem in aiming with
a deceptive gaze strategy, soccer players apply a mirroring algorithm, effectively converting the kicking angle
for the side they ﬁxate (made by the triangle between
the ball, the post, and the goalkeeper), to the opposite
(un-ﬁxated) side (see Figure 1).
The aim of this third experiment was to explore the
contribution that spatial information provided by the
goalkeeper’s location provides toward the maintenance
of shooting accuracy when gaze and aiming intention are
decoupled while attempting to eliminate goal-related
information from peripheral vision and memory. We
again hypothesize that when players decouple gaze
from aiming intention, they will employ compensatory
extensions in QE durations (as in Experiments 1 and 2).
We suggest that the difference in performance between
Experiments 1 and 2 is due to the removal of information
from the relative space between goalkeeper and post
from one side of the goal, which is utilized in the
formulation of a shot to the opposite side of the goal.

Apparatus.
Participants wore the same eye tracker as
in Experiment 2 and shot to two randomized goals that
were projected (Hitachi XGA projector, CP-X275;
Tokyo, Japan) onto a blank wall from a distance of
6 m. Goal and goalkeeper projections were used to
(a) allow for the alternation of goal size and goalkeeper
location in an efﬁcient manner, (b) allow for the elimination of target-related information from peripheral
vision without constraining the vision of the kicker,
and (c) allow for the maintenance of the spatial relationship between the goalkeeper and goal while eliminating
the negative effect his or her mere presence can have
(Navarro, van der Kamp, Ranvaud, & Savelsbergh,
2013; Wood & Wilson 2010b; Wilson et al., 2009).
Due to the need to project the goal, the distance of the
penalty shot was reduced, and therefore, we decided to
use a modiﬁed (indoor) penalty kick with a restricted
(one-step) run-up (as used by Wood & Wilson, 2010b).
By controlling these aspects of the experimental design,
we can be more conﬁdent that any effects observed from
the manipulation of goalkeeper position are attributable
to the spatial manipulation alone.
Each projected goal measured 2 m in height but
differed in width (4 vs. 3.6 m; see Figure 2). These
differing sizes were incorporated to reduce the impact of
memory of goal size in the aiming task and minimize any
learning effects from repeatedly shooting to the same
goal.3 To minimize the use of peripheral vision, only the
right half of each goal was shown. All presented goals
also had a projected image of a goalkeeper (height =
1.80 m) that was situated in two counterbalanced locations (directly in the center of the goal “center” and then
0.5 m to the right “off-center”). For each goal, a colored
target (30 cm × 30 cm) was visible in the bottom righthand corner. A green target signaled to the player that he
or she was to shoot with the KI strategy (look where
he or she was shooting), and a red target signaled to
the player to shoot with a deceptive strategy (look at the
target but attempt to hit the same location on the opposite
side of the goal). Players shot using an indoor Mitre
Cyclone football (Manchester, UK) of standard inﬂation. Shot location was recorded using a Panasonic SDRS26 (60 Hz; Osaka, Japan) camcorder that was located
1 m to the right of the penalty spot.

examples of each goal with differing goalkeeper positions and both colored targets. Players were told that they

whether that was the post in the direction that they were
shooting (KI strategy) or the estimated post location in
the opposite corner of the goal (deceptive strategy). It
was explained to participants that before each shot, they
were required to turn around with their back facing the
goal. The researcher would then give a verbal indication

Discussion
The aim of this ﬁnal experiment was to explore the
contribution that angular information pertaining to the
goalkeeper’s location makes to the maintenance of
shooting accuracy when players disassociate gaze
from aiming intention during head fakes. As predicted
and in line with the two previous experiments, a deceptive shooting strategy induced signiﬁcantly longer QE
aiming durations to compensate for the decoupling of
gaze from aiming intention (Figure 3). Furthermore,
when we manipulated the spatial location of the goalkeeper during deceptive shots, shooting accuracy centralized signiﬁcantly (Table 4). Therefore, when the
visual angle between goalkeeper and post was made
more acute, resultant shooting angle also became more
acute, and shots became centralized. However, in contrast to Experiment 1, this time we found that the
deceptive strategy was not as accurate as the KI strategy
when the goalkeeper was located in the center of the
goal, despite longer aiming durations.
There are a number of differences between the two
experiments that might explain why we failed to replicate the maintenance of accuracy from Experiment 1.
First, the KI condition in Experiment 3 was effectively a
simple aiming task; with no need to beat the goalkeeper,
players were free to allocate all of their attentional
resources to the preprogramming of target-related information necessary for accurate shooting. In Experiment 1,
this was not as straightforward, and the goalkeeper
would likely receive covert attention in all attempts
(KI, KD, and deceptive). The KI data from Experiment
3 underline the accuracy that players are capable of if
they can learn to adapt to, or ignore, the presence of the
goalkeeper. It also suggests that training these attentional
abilities offers scope for practical interventions for penalty takers (e.g., Wood et al., 2015).
Second, in this experiment, the choice of strategy
was driven by the experimental manipulation rather
than an active decision from the participant (cf. Experiment
1).
Therefore,
there
are
likely
additional
attentional
demands
in
inhibiting
the
proponent
response to shoot to the target and to inhibit gaze
from being directed to the opposite side of the goal
to the abstract target (similar to attentional effects seen
in Stroop aiming tasks; see Wood, Vine, & Wilson,
2016). Although these differences make performance
comparisons across experiments problematic, they do
not undermine the central ﬁndings regarding our
ﬁrst hypothesis: Deceptive gaze increased task complexity and induced compensatory extensions in QE
durations.

General Discussion
The results of all three experiments showed that when
players use deceptive gaze, they engage in the supplementary processing of other target-related information
(evidenced through extensions in QE durations) to
maintain performance. Speciﬁcally, the location of the
goalkeeper and the relative geometric information
provide a particularly important source of information
that players can use during deceptive kicks. Although
these three experiments were focused on the task of
penalty kicks, the ﬁndings have wider implications for
(a) understanding the attentional and performance costs
of employing deceptive gaze strategies in general, (b) the
mechanisms behind the QE phenomenon in aiming
tasks, and (c) the utility of angular information and
peripheral vision in the control of skilled action.
First, despite the widespread use of deceptive
gaze in sport, little empirical evidence exists to support
the utility of such behaviors, and no studies have
explored the attentional cost behind their use. What is
clear from this research is that decoupling gaze from
aiming intention increases the complexity of the aiming
action, increases the demands of the task, and requires
supplementary preprogramming of other, more complex, target-related information (e.g., goalkeeper location) in an attempt to safeguard against performance
decrements. When this important information is missing
(Experiment 2) or manipulated (Experiment 3), then
shooting accuracy suffers despite extended QE durations.
However, if a goalkeeper makes use of this deceptive gaze information to help base his or her decision on
which side to dive (Dicks et al., 2010), then this might be
a useful strategy for players to use, as there is no longer a
need for a high level of accuracy. Research exploring the
effectiveness of gaze misdirection in magicians does
provide a cautionary note however (Cui, Otero-Millan,
Macknik, King, Martinez-Conde, 2011; Rieiro et al.,
2013). Although magicians believe that gaze misdirection is one of the most powerful tools in their repertoire,
conjuring tricks are just as effective when the magician’s
gaze is occluded (Rieiro et al., 2013). Therefore, the
compulsion to employ head fakes probably stems from
an overinﬂated view of the importance of gaze in
deceiving the observer. If direction of gaze has little
inﬂuence on the decision making of goalkeepers, then
players might ﬁnd it useful to combine these strategies
by looking where they intend to shoot while also employing exaggerated and deceptive body movements to
the opposite direction. Future research should seek to
understand the role of player’s gaze in guiding the
decision making of goalkeepers.
Second, our results support the information processing account for how long QE durations support for
aiming performance. In this explanation, the QE is
proposed to be related to the length of time required
for the preprogramming of target-related information and
the ﬁne-tuning of movement responses (Vickers, 1996,
2007). Recent research has found support for this
proposal by correlating longer QE durations with more
difﬁcult task demands (e.g., Klostermann et al., 2013;
Williams et al., 2002), with neural measures of preprogramming (e.g., Bereitschaftspotential; Mann, Coombes,
Mousseau, & Janelle, 2011) and by utilizing illusions to
bias preplanning (Wood et al., 2013). In Experiment 1,
players revealed signiﬁcantly longer QE durations when
adopting a deceptive strategy, and in Experiments 2 and
3, players utilized signiﬁcantly longer QE durations when
the task was more difﬁcult (shooting to a mirror position
as opposed to what they ﬁxated on). One issue with trying
to interpret the compensatory nature of this extended QE
duration is that while longer QE durations are generally
associated with better performance, they are also associated with increased task difﬁculty, conditions when
performance is likely to be poorer (see WaltersSymons et al., 2017, for further discussion). As such,
we are unable to dissociate the combined and opposite
effects of manipulation and performance outcome on QE
with this design.
Third, although the QE is deﬁned in terms of point
of gaze, there has been little interest in exploring exactly
what type of information is being processed during QE
durations (Williams, 2016). Our results suggest that in
the context of deceptive aiming strategies, the QE is used
for the covert extraction and processing of target-related
information related to the spatial positioning of the
goalkeeper. Overall, although our ﬁndings support the
assumption that the QE is a period reﬂecting the extraction and preprogramming of target-related information,
it seems that there is a degree of plasticity regarding the
type of information that can be used to support successful aiming actions, even within the same task.
In conclusion, the results of these experiments
suggest that there is an attentional cost of using deceptive
gaze strategies in visuomotor tasks, which require covert
compensatory adaptations to attentional control. As far
as we are aware, this is the ﬁrst study that has explored
the cost of deceptive head fakes and the effectiveness of
deceptive gaze from the deceiver’s perspective. As such,
many questions remain to be answered surrounding the
utility of such behaviors and how attentional adaptations
compensate for the increased complexity that is inherent
with their use. Hopefully, this can be a fruitful line of
research in the near future.
