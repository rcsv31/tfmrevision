Abstract
In many team sports, in which environmental change is constant, athletes selectively
allocate attention between the approaching ball and other players, and constantly
consistently making decisions regarding whom to pass the ball to. Few previous
studies on decision-making in team sports such as soccer have included the ball
reception phase. This study examined players’ visual search strategies during pass
decisions. Using five-on-four soccer-specific film simulations from previously
recorded real scenes, high-level players (HLPs) and middle-level players (MLPs)
reacted to life-sized soccer scenes. We measured their visual search strategies in
decision-making tasks involving ball reception and pass execution and collected their
verbal reports. We employed a novel system wherein the ball is ejected toward
participants according to the video clips in order to maintain perception–action

Introduction
Passing is one of the main skills in team sports such as soccer (Mohammed,
Shaﬁzadeh, & Platt, 2014; Rampinini, Impellizzeri, Castagna, Coutts, & Wisløff,
2009); it requires players to receive and pass a moving ball to targeted locations
or players (G. J. P. Savelsbergh, Onrust, Rouwenhorst, & Van der Kamp, 2006).
In such skillful play, it is important to efﬁciently and consistently select the right
action at the right moment during a game (Baker, Coˆ te, & Abernethy, 2003;
Caljouw, Van der Kamp, & Savelsbergh, 2004a, 2004b). Such skillful performance requires not only accurate execution of motor behavior but also perceptual–cognitive skills (G. J. P. Savelsbergh, Van der Kamp, Williams, & Ward,
2005; G. J. P. Savelsbergh, Williams, Van der Kamp, & Ward, 2002; Williams,
Davids, & Williams, 1999). For example, expert players have superior anticipation skills compared with novices in terms of making accurate predictions from
the opponent’s body motion and playing situation (G. J. P. Savelsbergh et al.,
2002; Williams & Davids, 1998; Williams, Davids, Burwitz, & Williams, 1994).
However, past studies have generally relied on data acquired through paperand-pen responses, joystick controls, and verbal responses (Abernethy &
Russell, 1987; G. J. P. Savelsbergh et al., 2005; Williams & Burwitz, 1993).
Such practices present problems with ecological validity, as they separate perception from action in execution, while these task elements are normally combined in the real environment (Farrow & Abernethy, 2003). From an ecological
perspective, Gibson (1979) asserted that there is a cyclical interdependence
between perception and action, and this has been supported by previous studies
in sports (Farrow & Abernethy, 2003; D. L. Mann, Abernethy, & Farrow,
2010). For example, D. L. Mann et al. (2010) examined the accuracy of cricket

batters’ predictions under reaction situations with different degrees of coupling
between perception and exercise. They reported that the difference in prediction
accuracy between expert and novice cricket players increased in situations close
to the actual exercise environment. In meta-analyses of studies on perceptual–
cognitive skills in sports, researchers have found that expertise is most evident in
tests that take place within environments that are representative of the actual
performance environment, as opposed to conventional laboratory-based tests
(D. T. Y. Mann, Williams, Ward, & Janelle, 2007; Travassos et al., 2013). These
ﬁndings show the importance of using action tasks close to actual play in order
to accurately describe experts’ perceptual–cognitive skills. Therefore, as Dicks,
Button, and Davids (2010) indicated, to clarify the accuracy of decision-making
and the execution of exercise based on visual information, it is better to use a
task that combines perceptual–cognitive and motor skills, and it is necessary to
consider the cognitive processes that enable excellent performance (McRobert,
Williams, Ward, & Eccles, 2009).
Studies have also been conducted on the perceptual–cognitive process underlying performance in soccer players, with a focus on the linkage between perception and action. For example, W. Helsen and Pauwels (1993) examined the
visual search strategy of soccer players during attacking scenarios in which
decision-making is required. They found that experts looked longer at the position of defenders and space, whereas novices tended to search for information
from sources such as attackers, goals, and ball. Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, and
Philippaerts (2007) investigated differences in visual search strategies during
decision-making among youth soccer players. Their participants were required
to view a test ﬁlm and to make a choice between pass to other team players, shoot
to goal, and dribble as soon as possible after watching the video. Subsequently,
based on the guidelines prescribed by seven youth soccer coaches, participants’
decision-making skills were evaluated. Those with superior skills spent a longer
time paying attention to the player with the ball and frequently moved their gaze
from that player to other places, as compared to those with inferior skills. These
studies reveal the features of visual search strategy among expert soccer players
that support these players’ excellent decision-making through ﬁlm-based tests in
experimental environments.
However, previous research has not clariﬁed how attention is distributed
among players and the ball while players receive the ball, as seen in actual
soccer games. Since pass actions involve making decisions about which teammate to pass the ball to, players must have a well-balanced distribution of
attention between the approaching ball and the surrounding players (Oppici,
Panchuk, Serpiello, & Farrow, 2017). Nevertheless, there is little research on
what sort of visual search strategy is used by a soccer player to control and make
decisions regarding the approaching ball. One such study was by Oppici et al.
(2017), who examined how futsal and soccer players directed their attention
during the reception phase from when the ball leaves a teammate’s foot until

relationship between decision-making and visual search strategies in team

Eye movement measures can be used to determine the position and trajectory
of a ﬁxation point, and it is possible to analyze information such as the attention
target, number of ﬁxation points, and ﬁxation duration (Williams, Janelle, &
Davids, 2004). In addition, as gaze shift and attention shift are closely correlated
(Henderson, 2003), the gaze pattern can provide information on individual
attentional control (Panchuk, Vine, & Vickers, 2015). However, prior work
suggests that the point of ﬁxation does not necessarily coincide with focal attention (Huys & Beek, 2002; Tenenbaum, 2003), making this measure a poor indicator for determining the meaning and context of information extracted during
tactical decision-making (Roca, Ford, McRobert, & Williams, 2011). Verbal
reports may help provide such meaning and context (Afonso, Garganta,
McRobert, Williams, & Mesquita, 2012). As such, eye movements and verbal
reports might have a complementary relationship supporting both weak elements (Williams & Ericsson, 2005). Therefore, through the integration of
these methods, it is possible to more accurately examine visual search strategies
utilized during tactical decision-making from the viewpoint of gaze and
attention.
In this study, in order to clarify the characteristics of players’ visual search
strategies when making passing decisions, we analyzed eye movement data and
verbal reports while soccer players one-touch passed the ball in a ﬁve-on-four
attacking scene. We hypothesized that players’ visual search strategies for
accessing visual information would differ according to their skills or proﬁciencies and differences in phase in pass execution.
Method
Participants
Participants were 18 high-level soccer players (HLPs) and 18 middle-level soccer
players (MLPs). The mean age of the HLP group was 19.7 (SD ¼ 1.1) years, with
a mean competition experience of 13.1 (SD ¼ 1.7) years. The HLP group played
at the top level in the Japanese university football league and had top-level
competitive abilities domestically. The mean age of the MLP group was 20.1

ﬁlms of play patterns were taken from an elevated perspective (approximately
three meters) of the center circle area.
The test ﬁlm was created in cooperation with different soccer players who
were not included among the study participants. For this purpose, we provided
detailed instructions to eight adult players with more than 10 years of playing
and top-level competition experience at the university level in Japan, and we
conducted practice sessions on how to receive the ball (i.e., action in the off-theball phase) over a 2-week period. Actions such as passing the ball to teammates
or advancing toward the soccer goal while in possession of the ball were not
included. Next, we ﬁlmed ﬁve-on-four attacking play scenes in which an offensive player tried to receive a pass from a central midﬁelder in the pitch center.
The central midﬁelder was not visible because play was ﬁlmed from that position. For the test video used in the decision-making task, the video material was
edited at the point where the central midﬁelder passed the ball using Adobe
Premiere Pro CS4 software (Adobe Systems Incorporated, San Jose, CA). In
total, 15 ﬁlm clips were generated. The length of the test ﬁlm was approximately
ﬁve seconds. In each sequence, the offensive players took off from a defensive
player’s mark and tried to receive a pass from the player located in a central
position. We occluded the ﬁlm sequences at the point just before the central
midﬁelder passed the ball to prevent participants viewing the ﬁlm from receiving
any feedback in relation to the decision that was made during the actual ﬁlming
session. Test sequences included a standard countdown from ﬁve to one prior to
each ﬁlm’s presentation.

Apparatus
We used a compact, lightweight eye movement measurement system (EMR-8b,
Nac Image Technology, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) to record gaze data. This is a videobased monocular system that measures the line of gaze with respect to a headmounted scene camera. This system utilizes the pupil and the corneal reﬂex
method; the eye movements were measured at a sampling rate of 30 Hz with
the right eye monocular (Afonso et al., 2012; Nagano, Kato, & Fukuda, 2006;
Oppici et al., 2017), with a resolution of 0.1 both horizontally and vertically.
The eye movement data were recorded by a digital video camera (iVIS HV 30,
Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and superimposed as a cursor on the videotaped
image to show the direction of participants’ gaze.
Procedure
The test ﬁlms were projected onto a large screen (7.3 m  2.9 m) located ﬁve
meters from the participant. We presented a near-life-size image that subtended
a visual angle of around 72 in the horizontal direction and 55 in the vertical
direction. The screen image subtended a visual angle of approximately 8, and
the result of calculation by the trigonometric function showed an error level
within 1 of the actual images. Thus, they strictly simulated a live situation
(G. J. P. Savelsbergh et al., 2002). We asked participants to watch the video
from the perspective of an offensive player playing in the pitch center.
Participants were required to watch the test ﬁlm and decide which teammate
should receive the pass at the moment of occlusion. To increase the reality of the
experimental setting, we asked participants to make a one-touch pass of balls
ejected from an ejection machine (three rotor machine SSK-03, Sunaga Inc.,
Tochigi, Japan) on the participant’s diagonal front left toward the chosen teammate. Moreover, to ensure that participants carried out the intended pass, we
required them to verbalize their intended pass response immediately after each
trial. Here, we asked participants to state, in as much detail as possible, to whom
the ball was intended and how they perceived the play situation. Verbal reports
were recorded using an Integrated circuit recorder (ICD-BX 332, Sony Inc.,
Tokyo, Japan), which were converted to a computer audio ﬁle (.wav) and transcribed before analysis.
After explaining the experimental procedure, to ensure familiarity with the
experiment, participants performed a practice trial using 10 ﬁlms that were
different from the ones used in the test trial before completing the actual
decision-making test. The practice trial was conducted in the same way as the
decision-making test, and participants were equipped with an eye movement
measurement system on their head. We calibrated the eye movement measurement system using a conventional 9-point reference grid according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Participants performed a pass with one touch of the

Dependent Variables
Decision-making test
Decision-making accuracy. Based on Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, Mazyn, et al.
(2007) and Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, and Philippaerts (2007), four soccer
coaches ﬁrst evaluated ideal decisions (i.e., an appropriate pass leading to an
immediate goal-scoring opportunity) and priorities for each ﬁlm clip individually and then made a ﬁnal decision after discussing the different evaluations.
Evaluation criteria for each ﬁlm clip were as follows: (a) four points ¼ an appropriate pass leading to an immediate goal-scoring opportunity, (b) three
points ¼ not a directly goal-oriented pass but a pass indirectly leading to a
goal-scoring opportunity, (c) two points ¼ continuation of ball possession but
not leading to a goal-scoring opportunity, and (d) one point ¼ a wrong decision
leading to a disadvantageous situation (e.g., loss of ball possession). Although
they were not blind evaluators, the concordance rate among the coaches was
86.7%. For those with differing evaluations, we made the ﬁnal decision after
consultation. Decision-making accuracy was calculated as the value obtained by
dividing the score during the 30 trials by the total trial score (30 trials  4
points ¼ 120 points) and multiplying by 100. This was based on verbal reports
obtained from participants.
Decision-making replication. Based on the recognition task method (Williams,
Davids, Burwitz, & Williams, 1993), we evaluated whether consistent decisionmaking was made during the 15 different test videos (presented twice at
random). The reproducibility of decision-making was calculated by dividing
the number of answers in which the same decision was made twice by the test
video number (15) and multiplying by 100. This was based on verbal reports
obtained from participants. Group differences in decision-making were analyzed
using an independent t test.
Visual search data. We collected eye movement data from the start of the presentation of the ﬁlm clip to when the participant kicked the ball using eye movement data analysis software (EMR-dFactory, Nac Image Technology, Inc.,
Tokyo, Japan).
Search rate. Search rate included the mean number of visual ﬁxations per
trial, the mean ﬁxation duration per ﬁxation (in milliseconds), and the mean
number of ﬁxation locations per trial (Afonso et al., 2012; Roca et al., 2011).
A ﬁxation was deﬁned as when the line of gaze remained for 120 ms or more on
a certain visual target (Roca et al., 2011; G. J. P. Savelsbergh et al., 2002;
Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, Mazyn, et al., 2007; Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, &
Philippaerts, 2007; Williams & Davids, 1998). Differences between groups
across each of these three measures were analyzed using independent t tests.

An OS was deﬁned as the space in front of the defensive player or the defense

spaces were deﬁned as CS. B–NS was deﬁned as the ball or the space between

analysis, the ﬁlms were selected with consideration given to avoiding category

any situation.
In accordance with Oppici et al. (2017), this study was divided into two
phases to identify when and what the participant was watching from video
presentation to pass performance. We determined Phase 1 as the time from
the beginning of the ﬁlm clip until the ball was ejected. We determined Phase
2 as the time from ball ejection until the participant kicked the ball with one
touch. Differences between groups in terms of ﬁlm viewing time due to the
ball ejection procedure were not observed during Phase 1 (mean [M] ¼ 3.87,
SD ¼ 0.14 seconds vs. M ¼ 3.87, SD ¼ 0.13 seconds) or Phase 2 (M ¼ 1.78,
SD ¼ 0.12 seconds vs. M ¼ 1.76, SD ¼ 0.10 seconds).
The reliability of eye movement data has been demonstrated using intra- and
interobserver agreement methods (Roca et al., 2011). Therefore, in this study,
the reliability analysis of eye movement data was carried out using the same
methods (for more details, see Thomas, Nelson, & Silverman, 2005). Just over
20% of all eye movement data were reanalyzed to provide the agreement ﬁgures
using the procedures recommended by Thomas et al. (2005). As a result,
the intra- and interobserver reliability of eye movement data was 93.3% and
90.5%, respectively, conﬁrming the reliability of the data (Roca et al., 2011;
G. J. Savelsbergh, Haans, Kooijman, & van Kampen, 2010; G. J. P. Savelsbergh
et al., 2006; Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, Mazyn, et al., 2007). All data (expressed
as a percentage) were subjected to an arcsine transformation (G. J. P.
Savelsbergh et al., 2002) to guarantee the homogeneity of the variance and
were analyzed using a three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in which

Verbal reports. Participants’ verbal reports were encoded using the method of
McPherson (1999a, 1999b, 2000). This adapted protocol includes goal concepts
referring to the purpose of the selected action, concepts referring to the action
selected during a speciﬁc situation, and concepts referring to the conditions
under which the action occurred (Afonso et al., 2012; McPherson, 2000). For
this study, the task was to make the decision and then to make an actual onetouch pass (with shooting and dribbling restrictions). Therefore, statements
related to goal and action concepts were not made in the same way as in previous studies (Afonso et al., 2012).
We further investigated what kind of condition concepts were verbally
reported by participants to examine their visual search strategies from the viewpoint of pattern recognition. McPherson (1999a, 1999b, 2000) used a method
that involved creating concepts (e.g., participants’ position and opponent players’ position) from participants’ verbal reports, and it was developed to investigate how these concepts were organized (i.e., how often two or more concepts
are generated as a phrase). In this study, we adapted McPherson’s (1999a,
1999b, 2000) method and examined how each concept was organized. Based
on participants’ verbal reports, we created eight conditions: teammate (TM),
opponent player (OP), space (S), teammate–opponent player (TM–OP), teammate–space (TM–S), opponent player–space (OP–S), teammate–opponent
player–space (TM–OP–S), and others (O). For example, in the case of the statement, “the right forward player was pulling away,” and similar statements
invoking a teammate’s movement, we encoded TM. In addition, in the case of
the statement, “the attacker on the (left) side pulled down and the defender was
marked by the player,” both a teammate and an opponent player were verbally
reported in one phrase, and we encoded the statement as TM–OP. We evaluated
the reliability of the verbal report data using the same method as was used to
examine the eye movement data. We found intra- and interobserver reliability of
the verbal report data to be 95.4% and 94.2%, respectively (Afonso et al., 2012;
Roca, Ford, McRobert, & Williams, 2013; Roca et al., 2011).

rate in the visual search data using t tests. As mentioned earlier, all data
(expressed as a percentage) were subjected to an arcsine transformation (G. J.
P. Savelsbergh et al., 2002) to guarantee the homogeneity of variance, and we
analyzed statistically signiﬁcant differences using a three-way ANOVA in which
ﬁxation location and phase were within-participants factors and group (HLPs
and MLPs) was the between-participants factor. We used the Bonferroni

While no player skill differences were observed in the number of ﬁxations or
ﬁxation times, HLPs had a signiﬁcantly greater number of ﬁxation locations
than MLPs. This ﬁnding indicates that HLPs did not differ from MLPs by
ﬁxating repeatedly on any particular location, but, rather, viewed more visual
information sources in a limited time. Therefore, the HLP group’s visual search
range appeared to be greater than that of the MLP group. These results are
consistent with previous studies (Afonso et al., 2012; Roca et al., 2011; Williams
et al., 1994) and appear to highlight a characteristic difference in soccer play
between the HLP and MLP groups.
When comparing percent viewing times in Phases 1 and 2, regardless of
proﬁciency, participants tended to spend more time ﬁxating on the free attacker
(A), defender (D), attacker closely marked by a defender (A/D), and OS and CS
during Phase 1. Furthermore, participants tended to spend more time ﬁxating
on the best choice player (BCP) and B–NS during Phase 2. These results indicate
that participants developed phase-speciﬁc strategies to collect the information
they needed to make an accurate decision. There are many players in soccer
(creating many potential information sources), and it is difﬁcult to predict the
behavior of the ball (Oppici et al., 2017). Past research has shown that soccer
players pay close attention to surrounding players, while their teammates are in
possession of the ball, and they switch attention, mainly to the ball, during the
ball reception phase (Oppici et al., 2017). However, an important new ﬁnding of
our study is that soccer players shift their gaze according to the situation (each
phase of play). Soccer players utilize tactics-related strategies to direct their line
of gaze to the surrounding environment, including teammates, opponents, and
spaces, to make appropriate decisions during the phase when a teammate
possesses the ball; and they employ skill-related strategies to direct their line
of gaze to the ball and the teammate who will pass, in order to accurately pass
when the approaching ball must be passed immediately. They seem to adopt a
goal-oriented strategy to differentially control visual search patterns according
to play circumstances.
Previous studies have found that soccer players frequently gaze at surrounding players and the ball when their teammates had the ball or when they were
receiving the ball (Oppici et al., 2017). However, no prior research has examined
how players’ gaze points may differ in accordance with different player skill
levels. In our study, HLPs, compared with MLPs, demonstrated a relatively
higher percentage of viewing time on the A, D, and BCP versus OS. In contrast,
MLPs, compared with HLPs, exhibited a higher percentage viewing time on the
A/D and OS. Previous researchers (W. F. Helsen & Starkes, 1999; Vaeyens,
Lenoir, Williams, & Philippaerts, 2007) reported that expert players gaze at
the defender and the ball holder, consistent with some of our results.
However, W. F. Helsen and Starkes (1999) found that unskilled players look
at the attacker longer, while Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, and Philippaerts (2007)
reported that players good at decision-making look closely at the ball holder

among the attackers. Thus, consistent results have not always been obtained.
Our different ﬁndings may have been affected by the fact that we dealt only with
pass selection scenes in the ball reception phase of soccer, while other research
targeted a wide variety of attacking scenes. This suggests that attackers (A and
BCP) and defenders (D) are more important information sources than space
(OS) in our research because these information sources support more precise
decision-making within attacking scenes (i.e., pass-selection scenes) in which
time and space restrictions are severe. Therefore, our HLP group’s visual
search strategy to shift their gaze toward these objects was intended to accurately and quickly collect needed information.
The HLP group, relative to the MLP group, made more statements concerning TM–OP and TM–OP–S, whereas the MLP group, relative to HLPs, generated more statements concerning TM and TM–S. We found remarkable
qualitative differences in pattern cognition in that HLPs tended to capture the
situation as a larger pattern and pay more attention to information concerning
OP than did MLPs. Conversely, MLPs, who did not primarily gather OP information but focused instead on TM and S, tended to treat these visual targets as
individual informational elements rather than as part of a larger pattern.
McPherson (1999a, 1999b) examined thought content of tennis players of different skill levels and revealed that skilled players explained in detail the situation of the opponent in comparison with the unskilled players. This ﬁnding is
similar to our results. Being able to accurately recognize game situations is
important for making decisions within the game (Williams, 2009). In a study
that examined the relationship between decision-making and attention, lowskilled players were found to spend more time pondering the situation in
order to perform better, while those with prominent skills, such as HLPs,
made quicker decisions to achieve better performance (Beilock, Bertenthal,
McCoy, & Carr, 2004; Johnson & Raab, 2003). HLPs have a more elaborate
soccer-speciﬁc knowledge base (procedural knowledge), enabling them to capture a game situation more accurately (Williams & Davids, 1995; Williams et al.,
1993). Thus, we suggest that HLPs who perform highly automated actions can
grasp information regarding teammates, opponents, and spaces as a single pattern, rather than as a complex array of separate pieces of information, allowing
them to grasp the situation in detail. Their knowledge base helps them visualize
and utilize this global pattern comprehension. They can intuitively detect potential faults in an opponent’s defensive system and rapidly assess available space
to make a decision that is based on the opponent’s movements and positional
relationships. This process ensures optimal preparation for play development
and accurate decision-making. Our ﬁndings provide new insights into when and
how soccer players allocate their attention, assisting coaching, and training
methods in practical situations. These results support Oppici et al.’s (2017)
emphasis on the ball reception phase, which should be used in future research
regarding decision-making in team sports.

Among the limitations of this study was that, even though we designed a task
that included the ball reception phase, we still relied on a simulated experiment
for ﬁve-on-four play that is not identical to an actual (11 vs. 11) game scenario.
In addition, although the differences in player positions is known to affect performance (Tedesqui & Orlick, 2015), our study did not consider player position.
Therefore, future studies should assess attention and decision-making in a more
practical (in-situ) context, consider the player’s position, and include choices
relevant to game behaviors other than in passing (e.g., dribbling and shooting).
The results of this study provide two implications for coaching. First, in a
team sport, before receiving the ball, it is important to pay attention to the
opponents; while receiving the ball, it is better to pay attention to the teammates
who take a pass (when they must concurrently perform ball reception and
engage in decision-making about the allocation of attention). Second, when
judging whether or to whom a player should pass, prolonged contemplation
does not necessarily contribute to improved decision-making (Johnson &
Raab, 2003). On the contrary, making decisions quickly and intuitively may
lead a player to a higher performance. An expert’s procedural skills beneﬁt
from learning to play under time restrictions rather than the attention to
performance (Beilock et al., 2004), meaning that setting time limits in training
may help suppress excessive conscious attention and improve decision-making.
