Evidence of different underlying processes in pattern
recall and decision-making
Adam D. Gorman1,2,3, Bruce Abernethy2,4, and Damian Farrow1,5
1Movement Science–Skill Acquisition, Australian Institute of Sport, Canberra, ACT, Australia

The visual search characteristics of expert and novice basketball players were recorded during pattern
recall and decision-making tasks to determine whether the two tasks shared common visual–perceptual
processing strategies. The order in which participants entered the pattern elements in the recall task was
also analysed to further examine the nature of the visual–perceptual strategies and the relative emphasis
placed upon particular pattern features. The experts demonstrated superior performance across the
recall and decision-making tasks [see also Gorman, A. D., Abernethy, B., & Farrow, D. (2012).
Classical pattern recall tests and the prospective nature of expert performance. The Quarterly Journal
of Experimental Psychology, 65, 1151–1160; Gorman, A. D., Abernethy, B., & Farrow, D. (2013a).
Is the relationship between pattern recall and decision-making inﬂuenced by anticipatory recall? The
Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 66, 2219–2236)] but a number of signiﬁcant differences
in the visual search data highlighted disparities in the processing strategies, suggesting that recall
skill may utilize different underlying visual–perceptual processes than those required for accurate
decision-making performance in the natural setting. Performance on the recall task was characterized
by a proximal-to-distal order of entry of the pattern elements with participants tending to enter the
players located closest to the ball carrier earlier than those located more distal to the ball carrier. The
results provide further evidence of the underlying perceptual processes employed by experts when
extracting visual information from complex and dynamic patterns.
Keywords: Pattern recall; Decision-making; Memory; Visual search; Expertise.
The capability of expert performers to accurately
recall the locations of speciﬁc features from a
brieﬂy presented visual image is a ubiquitous
ﬁnding in numerous empirical investigations and
across a diverse range of domains (e.g., Adelson,
1981; Borgeaud & Abernethy, 1987; Gilhooly,
Wood, Kinnear, & Green, 1988). Many of the earliest recall studies compared expert and lesser
skilled chess players in an attempt to identify the
underlying mechanisms contributing to the expert
advantage (e.g., Chase & Simon, 1973; de Groot,
1965; see also Gobet, 1998). The results revealed,

While the results from recall tasks have helped
to advance theoretical explanations of expertise
(e.g., Ericsson & Kintsch, 1995; Gobet & Simon,
1996), the capability to accurately recall the
locations of elements from a visual display is generally not a core requirement of actual performance,

the recall paradigm is the extent to which recall performance is actually related to performance in the
natural setting (Ericsson, Patel, & Kintsch, 2000;
Ericsson & Smith, 1991; Ward, Williams, &
Hancock, 2006; Williams & Ericsson, 2005). It
has been argued that memory-based tasks such as
pattern recall may not necessarily provide a true
indication of the underpinning perceptual–cognitive processing strategies employed by experts compared to tasks such as anticipation and decisionmaking that require a more realistic response
(Ward et al., 2006; Williams & Ericsson, 2005;

underlying processing strategies of experts is of
both theoretical and practical signiﬁcance (see
Ericsson et al., 2000; Ericsson & Smith, 1991;
Ward et al., 2006; Williams & Ericsson, 2005).
The primary aim of the present study was to
examine the nature of the relationship between
pattern recall and decision-making using the
team-sport of basketball as the medium. The
dynamic, complex, and domain-speciﬁc interactions that occur between players in a typical
team-sport environment provide ideal scenarios
for investigating the pattern perception qualities
of expert performers (Starkes & Deakin, 1984;
Williams, Hodges, North, & Barton, 2006). The
intention was to compare the visual search behaviours of expert and novice basketball players
across tests of recall and decision-making to determine whether the two tasks share common search
characteristics. Similarities in gaze behaviours,
such as the number of visual ﬁxations and the percentage of time spent viewing certain display features, would provide evidence to suggest that a
recall task involves visual–perceptual processing
requirements similar to those used during a more
representative
task
such
as
decision-making
(North et al., 2009). Such results would suggest
that pattern recall may be one of the possible underpinning processes contributing to perceptual expertise (Farrow, McCrae, Gross, & Abernethy, 2010;
North et al., 2009; Williams & Davids, 1995).
Additional experimental manipulations included
variations in the type of display (i.e., static images
versus moving images) and measures to examine
the apparent importance of the various elements
within the patterns (e.g., attackers and defenders).
It has been suggested that the underlying functional role behind the accurate recall of structured
playing sequences may be related to anticipation
and
decision-making
(Farrow
et
al.,
2010;
Gorman, Abernethy, & Farrow, 2013a; Gorman
& Farrow, 2009; Starkes, Allard, Lindley, &
O’Reilly,
1994;
Williams
&
Davids,
1995).
Experienced performers may use the locations of
players in a typical playing sequence to anticipate
the likely subsequent states of the pattern, thereby
assisting in the rapid formulation of an appropriate
decision in response to the evolving pattern structures (Didierjean & Marmèche, 2005; Gorman,
Abernethy, & Farrow, 2011, 2012, 2013a; Starkes
et al., 1994). However, despite the intuitive appeal
of such notions, research examining the extent of
the relationship between pattern recall and anticipation suggests that the links between the two
tasks may in fact be quite limited (Ericsson et al.,
2000; Ericsson & Smith, 1991; Williams &
Ericsson, 2005; see also Farrow et al., 2010;
Gorman et al., 2013a; Williams & Davids, 1995).

For example, an investigation by Ward and
Williams (2003) into the acquisition of perceptual–cognitive skill across the developmental spectrum of elite and subelite soccer players showed
that representative tasks (i.e., anticipation and the
capability to accurately identify key players in a
playing sequence) were stronger predictors of skill
level than performance on a recall task. Similarly,
Williams and Ericsson (2005) argued that although
Williams and Davids (1995) found that performance on a soccer anticipation test was closely
related to performance on a pattern recall task,
only a small amount of the overall variance in anticipatory skill was explained by recall. Research into
chess expertise has also shown that recall skill is generally not a good predictor of other performance
variables such as chess ranking (Ericsson et al.,
2000; Pfau & Murphy, 1988; see also Holding &
Reynolds, 1982). The general consensus of the
results tends to argue against the utility of recall
skill as a key contributing factor to expert performance in the natural setting (Ericsson & Smith,
1991; Ericsson et al., 2000; Ward et al., 2006;
Williams & Ericsson, 2005; see also Farrow et al.,
2010). However, the absence of measures to
examine the underlying processing strategies has
meant that these assumptions are largely based
upon behavioural outcomes with few attempts to
isolate the key differences in perceptual processes
employed across the different experimental tasks
(McRobert, Williams, Ward, & Eccles, 2009;
North et al., 2011; North et al., 2009; Williams &
Ericsson, 2005).
One means of examining the underlying nature
of the visual–perceptual processing strategies is to
examine differences in visual search characteristics
(Williams
&
Ericsson,
2005).
For
example,
North et al. (2009) recorded the eye movements
of skilled and less skilled soccer players as they
completed a recognition task and an anticipation
task. The anticipation task required participants
to predict the ﬁnal location of a soccer kick executed by a player in a temporally occluded video
action sequence, whereas in the recognition task,
participants were asked to identify between previously viewed action sequences (shown in the
earlier anticipation test) and entirely new action
sequences. The results from the outcome measures
revealed an expert superiority across both tasks,
consistent with previous studies (e.g., Farrow
et al., 2010; Williams & Davids, 1995), but the
results from the visual search data revealed both
similarities and differences in the processing strategies employed by participants. For the experts,
there
were
no
signiﬁcant
differences
in
the
number of locations ﬁxated and the number of ﬁxation transitions across the two tasks, but both skill
groups exhibited more ﬁxations in the anticipation
test and signiﬁcantly longer ﬁxations in the recognition test. Differences also emerged for the
amount of time spent viewing speciﬁc display features with more time directed towards the location
of the ball and less time directed towards the
attacking team in the recognition test than in the
anticipation test. As concluded by the authors,
the visual search data appeared to suggest that
while the perceptual processes employed during
the recognition of a domain-speciﬁc stimulus
pattern are likely to be comparable to some
degree to those used during anticipation, the differences exhibited in visual search behaviours suggest
that recognition skill is likely to explain only a
portion of the overall processes required for successful anticipatory performance in the natural
setting (see also Farrow et al., 2010; Gorman
et al., 2013a; North et al., 2011).
The study by North et al. (2009) provided an
important ﬁrst step towards further identifying
the underlying nature of the visual–perceptual processes that are critical for expert pattern perception
(see also North et al., 2011; Roca, Ford, McRobert,
&
Williams,
2011).
However,
comparisons
between memory-based tasks (e.g., pattern recognition and pattern recall) and more representative
tasks (e.g., anticipation
and
decision-making)
using process tracing measures such as visual
search behaviours have not been extensively examined (but for an exception, see North et al., 2009;
see also North et al., 2011), and to date, no
studies have included a comparison of experts and
novices
in
a
dynamic
and
time-constrained
domain across recall and decision-making tasks.

Method
Participants
A total of 32 male participants were allocated into
either an expert (n = 16) or a novice group (n =
16). The participants were the same as those
described by Gorman et al. (2012, 2013a). The
experts were current or former basketball players
who had played at a regional and/or national level
of competition. Their average age was 20.06 years
(SD = 5.99), and they had an average of 11.84
years of playing experience (SD = 5.11). The
novices reported only limited amounts of playing
experience (M = 0.41 years, SD = 1.14 years) in
lower level competitions (M age = 24.19 years;
SD = 3.54 years). Informed written consent was
obtained from all participants, and the experiment
received institutional ethical clearance.

previously
(see

were

Procedure
Participants sat in a chair positioned 4.15 m from a
large screen (1.43 m tall × 1.92 m wide). The eye
tracking system was ﬁtted to the participant’s
head and calibrated using a 9-point reference
grid, which was displayed on the same screen as
that used for testing. The experiment involved a
pattern recall task and a decision-making task,
and both tasks were completed using static video
and moving video displays. The tasks and display
types were counterbalanced across participants,
but the static and moving displays were completed
one after another for a given task type so that all
recall trials, and all decision-making trials, were
completed as two consecutive blocks (i.e., moving
decision-making
followed
by
static
decisionmaking, or static decision-making followed by
moving decision-making; moving recall followed
by static recall, or static recall followed by moving
recall). Each test clip was displayed for 7 s using a
custom-built computer program (AIS React). To
avoid
confounding
the
interpretation
of
the
results
with
a

trade-off
(as
described by Abernethy, Baker, & Côté, 2005),
there were no instructions to emphasize the speed
of response in any of the experimental tasks (see
also Gorman et al., 2011, 2012, 2013a, 2013b).

the image disappeared from view. At this point,

They were also asked to indicate the location of
the ball carrier by using a left mouse click to
select the appropriate attacking player on screen.
All icons could be adjusted or deleted up until the
moment when the participant elected to move to
the next trial by clicking a button on screen labelled

Data analysis—Pattern recall and decision-making
Pattern recall task. Using the same procedure as that
described by Gorman et al. (2012, 2013a, 2013b),
the coordinate locations of the icons entered by
the participants for a given pattern structure were
directly compared to the actual coordinate locations
using a custom-built computer program (AIS
React). The program used a least squares approach
to calculate the distance between each of the
entered coordinates and each of the actual coordinates to essentially create a matrix showing all possible combinations of distances. The formula used to
calculate the distances was: √[(x1 – x2)2 + (y1 –
y2)2]. The smallest combined total of distances for
the ﬁve attacking elements and the ﬁve defensive
elements were calculated separately and were then
averaged to provide a mean recall error score for
attack and another mean recall error score for
defence for a given test trial. The scores for each
participant were averaged across all trials. The coordinates could range between values of zero and one,
and so the recall error scores were quite small in
absolute terms.
The recall data were also analysed using an
“order of entry” measurement technique, which
was designed to shed more light on the nature of
the perceptual processing strategies employed by
experts and novices (for a similar method using
chess patterns, see Gobet & Simon, 1996). Each
of the attacking and defensive players in the test
clips were given a speciﬁc label based upon their
perceived proximity to the ball carrier (see also
Ryu et al., 2013, 2014). The ball carrier was labelled
as A1; the next closest attacking player to the ball
carrier was labelled as A2; the second closest attacking player was labelled as A3; the third closest
player was A4; and the player farthest from the
ball carrier was A5. The defensive players were
labelled based upon the attacking player they were
guarding. That is, D1 was the player defending
the ball carrier; D2 was the player defending A2;
D3 was defending A3; D4 was defending A4;
and D5 was defending A5. The numerical labels
were determined by viewing perspective where
players who appeared to be closer to the ball
carrier were allocated smaller numerical values
(i.e., A2, A3) than those who appeared to be
further away. The AIS React computer program
recorded the order in which participants entered
each pattern icon for every test clip with the static
and moving displays examined separately. These
data were summed across trials to provide overall
frequencies for the number of occasions that participants entered each pattern icon (A1 through
to D5) across each of the possible entry orders
(entered ﬁrst through to entered 10th). For
example, as shown in Table 1, the experts entered
the ball carrier (A1) as their ﬁrst pattern element
51% of the time for the static video condition.
A small number of trials (less than 2%) were
removed from the analyses of the recall data due
to participants (a) erroneously adding or omitting
pattern elements (identiﬁed by a post hoc analysis
of participant responses) or (b) mistakenly selecting an attacking icon to drag onto screen when
the intention was to select a defending icon (or
vice versa). The latter errors were identiﬁed
during each participant’s test session by observing
the participant’s placement of the icons and/or
by listening to the participant’s remarks. For
example, whenever a participant placed an icon
off to one side (e.g., off the court playing area)
before later placing it in its ﬁnal location, a note
was made on the test sheet to remove that trial
from the order of entry data because there was
some doubt as to whether the participant had
intentionally selected the icon in the order that
was shown. Similarly, whenever a participant
made an obviously identiﬁable remark, either to
himself or to the experimenter, that he had unintentionally selected the wrong icon, a note was
made on the trial sheet to remove the trial from
the order of entry data.

Discussion
The primary aim of the present study was to examine
the nature of the relationship between pattern recall
and decision-making in a dynamic and complex
team-sport environment by using an analysis of
visual search behaviours to determine whether the
two tasks shared common visual–perceptual processing strategies. It was predicted that if recall and
decision-making utilized similar processes, the
visual search characteristics should be similar, with
few, if any, signiﬁcant differences across the two
tasks (North et al., 2009; see also Farrow et al.,
2010; Williams & Davids, 1995). The results from
an extensive analysis of visual search behaviours
revealed that the two tasks tended to exhibit more
differences in the visual–perceptual processing strategies than similarities. Participants exhibited signiﬁcantly more ﬁxations of shorter duration when
attempting to recall the locations of the players
within the stimulus patterns than when they were
required to decide upon the best possible response
for the ball carrier. These results were also reﬂected
in the percentage of time that participants spent
viewing speciﬁc pattern features. During the
decision-making
tests,
participants

One of the unexpected ﬁndings to emerge from
the experiment was the relative absence of differences between the two skill groups for the visual
search data. While the expert superiority was

greater duration of time devoted to D1 (the player
defending the ball carrier) by the novices than by
the experts may have been due to a variety of
factors, such as the novices’ lack of understanding
of the importance of this player to the overall
pattern structure (e.g., Abernethy et al., 2005;
Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, & Philippaerts, 2007),
or simply the fact that the novices were attracted to
the animated movements of D1 as he attempted to
prevent the ball carrier from executing a shot, pass,
or dribble manoeuvre (see Memmert & Furley,
2007). Although the absence of expert–novice
differences in visual search are somewhat inconsistent with the majority of research in the area (e.g.,
Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, Mazyn, et al., 2007;
Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, & Philippaerts, 2007),
similarities in gaze behaviours for expert and lesser
skilled individuals have been reported previously
for other sporting tasks (e.g., Abernethy, 1990;
Abernethy & Russell, 1987; Savelsbergh et al.,
2006; Williams & Davids, 1998; but see also
Abernethy, 1988). The basic conclusion to be
drawn from such ﬁndings is that the expert
advantage is
not necessarily
dependent upon
enhanced visual search strategies, but rather, the
experts appear to be better able to extract and interpret meaning from the information upon which they
are ﬁxating (Abernethy, 1988, 1990; Abernethy &
Russell, 1987; Ryu et al., 2013; Williams &
Davids, 1998; Williams et al., 2004). This capability
may stem from the experts’ task-speciﬁc knowledge
and their enhanced understanding of the typical
action–effect principles that exist within structured
conﬁgurations of pattern elements (Aglioti, Cesari,
Romani, & Urgesi, 2008; Ericsson & Kintsch,
1995; Ericsson et al., 2000; Gobet & Simon, 1996;
see also Jordan & Hunsinger, 2008; Jordan &
Knoblich, 2004). A related explanation is that the
experts may possess an advanced capability to
extract information from outside the central area of
visual ﬁxation by making greater use of peripheral
vision
(Abernethy,
1988;
Ryu
et
al.,
2013;
Williams & Davids, 1998; Williams et al., 2004).

displays produced a number of signiﬁcant differences, demonstrating that the presence or absence
of movement in the images can have a marked inﬂuence on the nature of the results (see also Gorman

increased number of ﬁxations of shorter duration
in the static displays suggests that participants
attempted to implement a more comprehensive
examination of the patterns when the locations of
the players in the images remained unchanged.

monitoring players in distal locations in the static
displays, presumably because the stationary nature
of the images meant that information could be
gleaned from the peripherally located players

around the ball carrier (see also Helsen & Starkes,
1999). In contrast, during observation of the

locations meant that participants were more inclined

the analysis of gaze strategies demonstrates that
recall and decision-making tasks are likely to
involve different underlying processes, indicating
that recall skill may be only one of many processes
that contribute to an expert’s capability to select
appropriate motor responses in the normal competitive

closest to the ball carrier, as well as the ball carrier
himself,
with the
locations
of the
attacking
players generally appearing to be more important
than the locations of the defensive team (see

et al., 2006; Williams & Davids, 1998). Finally,
the presence or absence of movement within the
visual display appears to exert a signiﬁcant inﬂuence
on the nature of the processing strategies and possibly also on the way in which the images are
encoded into memory (see DeLucia & Maldia,
2006; Gorman et al., 2011, 2012, 2013a; Helsen
& Starkes, 1999; Kourtzi & Nakayama, 2002).
