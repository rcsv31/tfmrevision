ABSTRACT
Decision-making in team sports necessitates monitoring multiple performers located at different distances 
(i.e., viewing eccentricities) from a critical information source. The processing of peripheral information is 
generally impaired under anxiety and when responding to stimuli located at larger eccentricities. These 
hypotheses have not been sufficiently tested in dynamic performance environments. We examined how 
pressure and eccentricities affect decision-making and visual behaviour in 4v4 basketball defensive scenar­
ios using a head mounted display. Experienced players monitored plays from the first-person perspective 
(centre position) and made defensive steps towards opponents threatening the basket from different 
eccentricities under low- and high-pressure. To tax working memory, participants simultaneously per­
formed a backward counting task. Players responded slower and with lower accuracy to opponents at 
larger eccentricities. Players mostly fixated on the ball-carrier, but over 50% of fixations were located on 
peripheral players, indicating that information in the periphery must be frequently updated with foveal 
vision (i.e., pivot strategy). When pressured, participants increased mental effort and improved counting 
performance; however, gaze behaviour and decision-making were relatively unaffected. Findings suggest 
that basketball players respond more quickly to opponents positioned at lower compared to higher 
eccentricities at the cost of impaired responses to opponents in the periphery.

Introduction
In team sports, athletes must rapidly process visual information 
from a variety of sources (e.g., multiple opponents) to make effec­
tive decisions (Roca et al., 2018; Vaeyens et al., 2007). Athletes rely 
heavily on both peripheral and central vision to monitor the 
actions of opponents and teammates in relation to the ball and 
the goal/target (Jordet et al., 2020; Vansteenkiste et al., 2014; Vater 
et al., 2020). This ability to keep track of multiple players and stimuli 
depends on “viewing eccentricities”, which refers to the viewing 
angle between the point of fixation and a relevant location in the 
visual periphery. Perceptual capabilities, as well as motor 
responses, are impaired if viewing eccentricities are too large 
(Strasburger et al., 2011; Svärd et al., 2021; Vater et al., 2017b). 
For instance, greater viewing eccentricities result in poorer detec­
tion of movement-related changes when simultaneously monitor­
ing multiple objects (Vater et al., 2017a, 2017b). However, the role 
of viewing eccentricities in sport performance has yet to be directly 
investigated. In the present study, we examine whether viewing 
eccentricities affect decision-making accuracy and response time 
in complex basketball scenarios using simulated reality.
In competitive performance environments, visual adaptations 
vary depending on the task constraints and the processing 
demands imposed by any number of critical stimuli and perceptual 
cues (Vater et al., 2016, 2020; Williams & Davids, 1998). Team sport 
settings can feature a multitude of competing and synergistic task 
demands – from tracking multiple opponents to planning 
movement responses as part of top-down tactical and strategic 
planning. To optimally extract relevant information from multiple 
sources, skilled athletes in dynamic, open-skill sport domains often 
employ more fixations of shorter duration (compared to novices) 
by frequently shifting gaze between pre-determined critical loca­
tions (Roca et al., 2018; Vater et al., 2020). This strategy contrasts 
with closed-skilled sports (e.g., self-paced aiming tasks) where 
athletes often employ fewer visual fixations of longer duration 
(Causer et al., 2010; Janelle et al., 2000; Vine et al., 2011). For 
example, skilled soccer players demonstrate more fixations of 
shorter duration than novices (Roca et al., 2011, 2018; Vaeyens 
et al., 2007), while simultaneously employing higher scanning 
behaviours, such as a greater number of head-eye movements 
away from, and back towards, the ball-carrier (Aksum et al., 2021; 
Jordet et al., 2020; Phatak & Gruber, 2019). A higher search rate 
allows athletes to continuously track multiple opponents and 
enables more informed decision-making according to frequent 
momentary changes in the performance environment (Gibson, 
1979; McGuckian et al., 2019). Peripheral vision facilitates the 
determination of optimal visual fixation locations, as motion pro­
cessed in the periphery can guide subsequent visual saccades. 
Athletes can maximize the use of peripheral vision by fixating on 
locations between important cues toextract information from their 
periphery (“gaze anchor”; Hausegger et al., 2019) and/or to move 
fixations back and forth between locations (“visual pivot”; Vater 
et al., 2019, 2020). Thus, in dynamic team sport settings, a strategic

utilization of peripheral vision is essential for tracking important

While few researchers have investigated the impact of view­
ing eccentricities on information extraction in sport, studies 
involving automobile driving have shown that targets in the 
periphery are generally processed less efficiently when located 
at greater viewing eccentricities (Berg et al., 2007; Svärd et al., 
2021; Yu et al., 2010; for a systematic review, see; Vater et al., 
2022). For example, drivers take longer to brake in response to 
targets located at greater viewing eccentricities (Lamble et al., 
1999; Strasburger et al., 2011; Svärd et al., 2021). Svärd et al. 
(2021) demonstrated that when drivers in a simulation perform 
a concurrent task requiring visual gaze off the road towards 
a secondary target, greater eccentricities are associated with 
more frequent glances back towards the road due to uncer­
tainty about potential hazards. Similarly, in studies involving 
multiple object tracking, individuals are worse at detecting 
changes in the physical form of peripheral objects at greater 
eccentricities (Henderson & Hollingworth, 1999; Rosenholtz, 
2016). Visual resolution gradually declines from foveal to per­
ipheral areas of the retina due to the distribution of photore­
ceptor cells (Caves et al., 2018), making the detection of fine 
details in a scene more difficult using peripheral vision (Levi 
et al., 1984, 1985; Loschky et al., 2005). Furthermore, peripheral 
vision is more scotopic (i.e., low light detection), sensitive to 
large object detection, and specialized to detect changes in 
motion (Rosenholtz, 2016; Wilkinson et al., 2020). Thus, in 
dynamic sporting contexts, athletes may be able to generally 
detect when opponents are moving around a key information 
source (e.g., the ball-carrier in ball-sports), but when informa­
tion is located at greater viewing eccentricities (and possibly 
requires higher visual acuity to detect important details), spe­
cific aspects of information extraction may be impaired as 
reflected by slower and less effective behavioural responses.
The visual system can be further impacted in dynamic 
sport settings when anxiety is induced through performance 
pressure (Alder et al., 2018; Fawver & Janelle, 2019; Williams 
& Elliott, 1999). For example, competitive anxiety in dynamic 
performance environments (e.g., driving) has been shown to 
narrow the visual field, which impairs the detection of 
objects using peripheral vision (Janelle et al., 1992; Murray 
& Janelle, 2003). Additionally, researchers have shown that 
anxiety or arousal can enhance the detection of target stimuli 
in foveal and peripheral visual fields in less-dynamic perfor­
mance environments (e.g., static balance at height) due to 
lowered perceptual thresholds (Kim et al., 2017; Vermehren & 
Carpenter, 2020). Anxiety is thought to enhance bottom-up 
stimulus processing to rapidly detect threats in the environ­
ment (Wilson, 2008), although top-down attentional pro­
cesses (e.g., expectations, goals) can also guide perceptions 
towards threats (Sussman et al., 2016. Scientists who have 
assessed the effects of anxiety on attention and visual search 
behaviour in sport have primarily used less-dynamic perfor­
mance contexts (e.g., 1 versus 1 scenarios) or self-paced 
motor tasks (e.g., free throw shooting). Within these domains, 
experts typically exhibit fewer fixations of longer duration 
(i.e., a visual search pattern interpreted as being more effi­
cient and effective for response time and motor accuracy; 
Giancamilli et al., 2022; Harle & Vickers, 2001; Williams & 
Elliott, 1999). However, under anxiety, individuals display 
a greater number of fixations of shorter duration, which has 
been found in shooting sports (Behan & Wilson, 2008; Causer 
et al., 2011; Shah et al., 2020), golf putting (Moore et al., 
2013; Vine & Wilson, 2011), basketball free-throws (Wilson, 
Vine, et al. 2009), and martial arts (Williams & Elliott, 1999). In 
more dynamic performance environments (e.g., team sports), 
greater top-down visual attentional control is critical to suc­
cessfully integrate technical skills within tactical frameworks 
(e.g., anticipate passes, positions, and movements of sur­
rounding stimuli, or other players). However, few researchers 
have examined how anxiety impacts visual behaviour in set­
tings where several opponents or stimuli must be tracked at 
different viewing eccentricities (Vater et al., 2016).
Moment-to-moment changes in the uncertainty of potential 
outcomes during dynamic sport contexts can lead to perfor­
mance anxiety. Processing Efficiency Theory (PET; Calvo & 
Eysenck, 1992), and its successor Attentional Control Theory 
(ACT; Eysenck et al., 2007), assert that anxiety taxes limited 
working memory resources primarily through worrisome 
thoughts, which impacts processing efficiency more than per­
formance effectiveness since performance can be maintained 
through increasing mental effort (Calvo & Eysenck, 1992). 
Increased mental effort and working memory load brought 
on by anxiety affects visual behaviour depending on task con­
straints. For instance, anxious drivers display a greater number 
of fixations to peripheral regions to notice targets that they 
would otherwise pick up with peripheral vision (Janelle et al., 
1992; Murray & Janelle, 2003; Wilson, Smith, et al. 2007). 
However, during more dynamic visuomotor tasks that require 
action planning (e.g., walking over complex terrain, rock- 
climbing), anxious individuals have been shown to employ 
fewer visual fixations, suggesting they are preoccupied with 
task-irrelevant information at the cost of adaptive preparatory 
planning for future actions (see Young & Ellmers, 2022). In both 
cases, evidence suggests that anxiety changes gaze patterns, 
accompanied by increases in mental effort or declines in per­
formance. That is, individuals typically either have more 
exhaustive visual search patterns to extract peripheral informa­
tion due to a narrowed attentional field and perceptual- 
cognitive bias to threat, or simply adopt a more conservative 
and resource-saving strategy of spending more time extracting 
information from central fixations at the cost of poorer proces­
sing of peripheral stimuli (e.g., combat sports; Fawver & Janelle, 
2019; Williams & Elliott, 1999; Young & Ellmers, 2022). Within 
the context of viewing eccentricities in dynamic team sports, 
anxiety elicited by performance pressure might impair informa­
tion extraction at greater viewing eccentricities due to 
a narrowed field of peripheral attention, requiring more mental 
effort (i.e., fixations to peripheral regions) to track peripheral 
stimuli and maintain performance, although these effects have 
yet to be empirically evaluated.
In settings with multiple task-demands, anxiety can cause 
attentional resources to be devoted towards task features 
representing threats to personal or team success (i.e., fea­
tures which in turn generate or sustain feelings of anxiety) at 
the cost of higher-order (e.g., top-down) or holistic informa­
tion processing (Eysenck & Derakshan, 2011). Consequently, 
researchers have implemented dual-task paradigms to

under anxiety-inducing conditions in dual-task paradigms; 
however, task performance tradeoffs may be evidenced by 
increases in mental effort to maintain primary task perfor­
mance at the cost of decreased secondary task performance 
(Alder et al., 2018; Ducrocq et al., 2017; Wilson, Chattlngton, 
et al. 2007). Alder et al. (2018) demonstrated this task trade- 
off effect in badminton players who were instructed to antici­
pate shot direction while responding to the frequency of an 
auditory tone (secondary task). Under anxiety-inducing con­
ditions, novices demonstrated poorer performance on the 
secondary auditory task, presumably because anxious ath­
letes prioritized attention towards the primary anticipation 
task (Alder et al., 2018). Therefore, by using dual-task para­
digms, it is possible to examine if performance deterioration 
elicited by pressure and attention on secondary tasks is 
accompanied by distinct changes in visual behavior.
We examined changes in decision-making accuracy and 
peripheral information extraction at different target eccentrici­
ties in an applied team sport setting (basketball) under low- 
and high-pressure conditions meant to induce feelings of anxi­
ety. Notably, central and peripheral visual functions have pre­
viously been investigated by Ryu et al. (2013, 2015) using 2D- 
basketball decision-making scenarios where either central or 
peripheral vision was blurred or occluded. Their findings gen­
erally showed that restricting central and peripheral visual 
information changes gaze strategies to compensate for missing 
visual information (Ryu et al., 2015). When peripheral vision was 
removed, athletes compensated for their inability to process 
information with peripheral vision by reducing their mean 
saccadic amplitude, reflecting a narrower (less-expansive) pat­
tern of visual search (Ryu et al., 2013, p. 605). As opposed to 
manipulating access to peripheral vision, we manipulated the 
viewing eccentricities of players cutting to the basket relative 
to the ball-carrier to use a more applied, dynamic basketball 
scenario requiring more ecologically valid motor responses. 
Consequently, we implemented a first-person simulated reality 
environment (head-mounted display) to present 360-degree 
videos of basketball 4v4 defensive scenarios in which players 
had to accurately anticipate opponents threatening to score 
while opponents were passing, moving, and cutting to the 
basket. The approach allowed participants to freely look around 
the environment and make explicit movement responses to 
defend against opponents (i.e., stepping to a defensive position 
with hands raised to defend without committing a foul). 
Previously, researchers have indicated that immersive simu­
lated performance environments mirror the demands of real 
game scenarios more closely than traditional video-based dis­
plays (Mann et al., 2010; Neumann et al., 2017). Given that the 
influence of anxiety on visual attention and performance is 
attributed to increases in working memory load (Calvo & 
Eysenck, 1992; Derakshan & Eysenck, 2009; Smith et al., 2001), 
we utilized a cognitively demanding secondary task to examine 
how individuals prioritize cognitive resources during dual task 
performance.
We made a priori predictions related to gaze eccentricity and 
how the location of critical information impacted on decision- 
making accuracy across low- and high-pressure conditions. We 
expected that players would have slower response times and 
lower response accuracy when opponents located at greater 
visual eccentricities from the ball-carrier were making the final 
offensive movement to the basket (Svärd et al., 2021). 
Furthermore, we predicted that more time spent fixating near 
the ball-carrier would be associated with increased response 
times, because less peripheral information would be accessible 
to track further opponents’ movements towards the basket 
(Janelle et al., 1992; Williams & Elliott, 1999). We expected 
participants in the high-pressure conditions would report 
increased anxiety and mental effort devoted to the task. 
Furthermore, we expected pressure to cause participants to 
fixate more towards opponents at further eccentricities to 
track peripheral stimuli, because anxiety tends to narrow the 
attentional field (i.e., an impaired ability to make use of periph­
eral vision), which must be compensated by fixations to the 
periphery (Derakshan & Eysenck, 2009; Murray & Janelle, 2003). 
Finally, we hypothesized that performance on the secondary 
task would be impaired only during the high-pressure condi­
tion, since anxiety would limit the working memory resources 
available to dedicate to both primary and secondary task per­
formance (i.e., task trade-off effect).
Materials and methods
Participants
A total of 16 male basketball players (M age = 21.3 ± 3.2 
years; M experience = 13.7 ± 4.3 years) who all reported play­
ing in competitive basketball leagues during high school 
(i.e., ~14–18 years old) were recruited from the local univer­
sity population. We conducted a post-hoc sensitivity analy­
sis in G*Power using the “Repeated measures, within 
factors” function, with α set at .05, statistical power set at 
80%, and a correlation of .60 among repeated measures (i.e., 
low- and high-pressure conditions). The sensitivity analysis 
revealed that our sample size was sufficient to detect med­
ium-to-large effect sizes (f = .34, d = .67). All participants 
except for one reported having normal or corrected-to- 
normal vision during the experiment (one participant wore 
glasses but could not use them while wearing the HMD). 
Five participants did not have competitive experience play­
ing the center position. Due to technical issues, visual data 
were only available for 140 out of a possible 160 trials per 
participant. All procedures were approved by the lead insti­
tution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB_00116943), and par­
ticipants signed an informed consent prior to beginning any 
study activities.

One-sided defensive basketball virtual-reality (VR) scenarios 
were constructed using Experiment Center 2.4 from 
Sensorimotor Instruments (SMI; Gaze Intelligence; Paris, 
France), and the VR environment was configured using 
SteamVR 3.2 (Washington, USA). An HTC Vive head mounted 
display (HMD) and system as used to create a movement

Test film
Basketball scenarios were recorded using a GoPro Omni 
(Woodman Labs, Inc., San Mateo, USA) with six synchronized 
GoPro Hero 4 black producing a spherical video array. The 
Omni was mounted onto a tripod and placed directly under 
the basket at a height of 1.60 m (approximate height of a 1.85 
m player when in a defense position with bended knees). The 
synchronization was achieved by an auditory signal (one player 
clapped his hands) that was recorded by each of the cameras.
Altogether, 8 experienced basketball players (4 defence, 4 
offence) were instructed to play different attacking sequences 
including a variety of tactical elements (e.g., pick and roll, give 
and go, backdoor cuts). In each situation, one offensive player 
would cut to the basket. Players were instructed to play 
a different number of passes (four to ten passes) in each scene 
before a player cut to the basket to vary the length of the situa­
tions. Moreover, the player cutting to the basket, and his relative 
position to the ball-carrier varied across trials to manipulate visual 
eccentricity. The aim was to create a variety of realistic situations 
with different viewing eccentricities between the player with the 
ball and the player cutting to the basket (eccentricity 1: player with 
ball cuts to the basket; eccentricity 4: player who is furthest from 
the player with the ball cuts to the basket, see Figure 1). Two 
research assistants were instructed to count the number of passes 
and mark the eccentricities. The players continued to play until at 
least five scenes for each combination of pass count (6) and 
eccentricity (4) were attained (in total 120 scenes). The recorded 
videos were imported and stitched together with Autopano 
(GoPro Kolor, 2016, San Mateo, USA). Adobe Premiere Pro (2016, 
San José, USA) was used to adjust the colour and lighting settings 
and later rendered into 120 single scenes. A set of 80 valid trials 
were selected by an independent basketball expert, who rated the 
correct responses (step to right, center, left, or no step; see below) 
for each trial. In total, there were twenty trials that required no 
response, fourteen at eccentricity 1, twelve at eccentricity 2, twenty 
at eccentricity 3, and fourteen at eccentricity 4.
Experimental tasks
Primary task
During the primary task, from a standing position, participants 
viewed the sequences described above in which offensive players 
passed and moved with the ball, and their goal as the defensive 
center was to determine the final offensive movement to the 
basket and defend by making a decisive step towards an opponent 
driving towards, or receiving a pass near, the basket with their 
hands raised (i.e., the prototypical defensive position to block an 
opponent or receive a charge). Participants were instructed to 
keep their feet still until a player threatened the basket. On some 
trials, no players cut to the basket, and participants were instructed 
not to move during those trials. There were four possible move­
ment responses for participants: (1) step to the right; (2) step 
towards the center; (3) step to the left; or (4) do not step at all. 
An audible beep signalled the start of each trial so experimenters 
could record performance measures (i.e., response time) post hoc 
for each trial using external camera footage. Response time was 
not recorded for trials where no response was required, but false 
steps were recorded for response accuracy. After participants 
stepped towards the player each trial, the test film continued 
until the offensive player shot the ball at which point the film 
froze and continued to the next trial.
Secondary task
In addition to the primary task, participants concurrently per­
formed a secondary backwards counting task, which involved 
counting backwards by a pre-determined interval by 3’s from 
a given number. The starting number for the secondary task

Performance pressure manipulation
After piloting various manipulations that could create the 
sufficient performance pressure to induce anxiety, we deter­
mined that non-contingent performance feedback (Vater 
et al., 2016) and monetary incentive (Wickens et al., 1993) 
were the most effective methods for the current experimental 
task and participant sample. We henceforth refer to the con­
ditions as low-pressure (LP) or high-pressure (HP). The dura­
tion and physical nature of the experiment (i.e., considerable 
mental and physical demand) raised concerns about counter­
balancing HP before LP, because some participants may have 
been less motivated to exert maximal effort during the sub­
sequent LP condition with no prize for performance after an 
effortful HP condition. While learning effects could occur 
across conditions, we prioritized eliminating potential 
decreases in effort and confounding motivation effects from 
having the HP condition first (Beilock & Carr, 2001; Wang 
et al., 2004). Therefore, the LP condition always preceded 
the HP condition.
Before the LP condition, participants were instructed that 
the experimental paradigm would potentially be used to 
develop talent identification tools at the collegiate basketball 
level and that participants must perform well for that to hap­
pen. Participants were instructed that the money they would 
receive for participation was primarily to motivate them to try 
their best. We informed participants that performance was 
based equally on their stepping accuracy, response time, and 
counting performance. After each block, non-contingent per­
formance feedback was given instructing participants they 
performed well regardless of their actual performance.
Before the HP condition, participants were instructed they 
would be competing for a $100 prize that would be distributed 
to the top few performers during the upcoming blocks of trials. 
Critically, we told participants that competition for the monetary 
prize required performance on both the primary and secondary 
task that was superior relative to other participants as well as 
relative to their previous performance on the LP condition. Thus, 
participants were under the guise that they had to perform at, or 
above, previous performance levels during which they received 
positive non-contingent performance feedback. After each block 
in the HP condition, however, experimenters gave non-contingent 
performance feedback indicating participants were close to or 
below their previous performance level.
Measures
Decision-making performance
Response time and response (stepping) accuracy were used as 
measures of performance on the primary task. Video recordings 
of participants were used to measure response time when 
stepping to offensive players cutting to the basket. The average 
of the two raters was calculated as response time (IRR = 0.90). 
Trials with response times greater than 2-seconds (0.98% of 
trials) were removed and catch trials with no attacker cutting to 
the basket were not included in the analyses. Stepping direc­
tion for response accuracy was determined through video 
recordings and validated by two experimenters for each trial.
Working memory performance
Backwards counting was used to assess working memory. 
Backwards counting has been shown to occupy working mem­
ory and executive function (Allen et al., 2006; van den Hout 
et al., 2010). Counting performance was measured as the num­
ber of correct numbers named during the trial. If a participant 
named an incorrect number, subsequent numbers were 
marked as correct if they were accurately named from the 
incorrect number.
Mental effort
The Rating Scale of Mental Effort (RSME; Zijlstra, 1993) was 
used to measure mental effort during the preceding block. 
The RSME comprises a vertical scale from 0 to 150, with nine 
points describing the amount of effort an individual expended 
(e.g., 50 = very little effort; 150 = maximal effort). The RSME 
has been proven to be a reliable and valid method of asses­
sing mental effort in previous literature (Sartang et al., 2016; 
Veltman & Gaillard, 1996).
Cognitive anxiety
The Mental Readiness Form-Likert (MRF-L; Krane, 1994) was 
used to assess state anxiety after each block. The MRF-L 
includes a 1–11 Likert scale, and participants rated the thoughts 
about the previous trials from calm (1) to worried (11). The MRF 
has been shown to be a valid and reliable tool for assessing 
state anxiety (Duncan et al., 2017; Krane, 1994).
Heart rate
A Polarflow watch (Polar M430, PolarFlow; 1 Hz) was used to 
collect heart rate (HR) data. The Polarflow watch has been 
shown to be a reliable instrument for collecting HR and energy 
expenditure data at a variety of exercise intensity levels 
(Henriksen et al., 2019; Tabor et al., 2019).
Gaze measures
The HTC Vive HMD featured a built-in eye-tracker from 
Sensorimotoric Instruments (SMI, Germany) that captures data 
from both eyes at 250 Hz with an average spatial accuracy of 
0.2° (Lohr et al., 2019). We used the x-y- coordinates of that data 
and the fixation detection algorithm of the manufacturer for 
the analyses of dependent measures. To synchronize the eye- 
tracker with the videos which played back at 30 Hz, we sampled 
down the eye-tracking data to 30 Hz to synchronize gaze with 
the videos. Gaze data were reduced to 6 measures by averaging 
the following variables within trials in each condition for each

Procedures
Participants provided written informed consent and filled out 
necessary questionnaires after being instructed about the nat­
ure of the task. Participants were fitted with a wearable heart 
rate monitor and wore the VR headset, then completed 6 
familiarization trials to practice the correct movement direction 
in responses to typical scenes. During practice trials on the 
primary task, performance feedback was provided. Next, parti­
cipants practiced the secondary task in isolation from the bas­
ketball scenes. During practice trials of the secondary task, 
participants counted backwards from 3 by a number between 
30 and 99 dictated by the experimenter. There were 6 practice 
trials of 10 seconds for the backward counting task. Finally, the 
basketball scene and counting task were practiced together for 
12 familiarization trials. After practice, participants completed 8 
blocks of 10 trials under LP and HP conditions (i.e., low pressure, 
high pressure) for a total of 16 blocks. Before each block, each 
participant’s gaze was calibrated, and the experimenter gave 
the participant a number to count backwards from on every 
trial. Following each block, mental effort was assessed using the 
RSME (Zijlstra, 1993) and cognitive anxiety was evaluated using 
the MRF-2 (Krane, 1994), both of which were visually displayed 
to participants within the HMD. Participant responses for these 
self-report assessments were obtained through verbal self- 
report. Participants finished the LP condition trial blocks, took 
a short break, and then completed the HP condition blocks. 
Upon completion of the experiment, participants were 
debriefed about the manipulation, and they received $20 for 
completing the experiment.

Discussion
We examined how basketball players visually track and 
respond to opponents cutting towards the basket from dif­
ferent viewing eccentricities under LP and HP conditions. We 
hypothesized slower response times and lower response 
accuracies would be evident at greater viewing eccentricities, 
and that higher levels of performance pressure would exacer­
bate these effects. Also, we hypothesized that participants 
would demonstrate a greater number of visual fixations away 
from the ball-carrier in the HP condition since anxiety nar­
rows the peripheral visual field, thereby interfering with 
information extraction when not fixating on the primary 
stimulus. Partially supporting our hypothesis, participants 
displayed slower response times and lower response accura­
cies when reacting to opponents at the largest viewing 
eccentricity. While claims about attentional allocation based 
on performance measures cannot be made with certainty, it 
is possible that players were prioritizing attention towards 
lower eccentricities due to the perceived higher situational 
probability that these opponents would attack (i.e., based on 
heuristics). In contrast to our predictions, this eccentricity 
effect was not altered by added performance pressure, nor 
was primary task performance. Furthermore, performance 
pressure did not cause players to demonstrate more fixations 
away from the ball-carrier, which may be due to the unique 
constraints and demands of our basketball task (e.g., first- 
person view in virtual reality, multiple opponents moving 
and crossing paths). Findings demonstrate how basketball 
players respond to attacking opponents is sensitive to the 
relative distance of the peripheral stimulus (i.e., off-ball oppo­
nent) to the central stimulus (i.e., ball-carrier) in conditions of 
varying performance pressure.
During sport tasks involving tracking multiple opponents 
simultaneously, effective gaze behavior generally necessitates 
the interactive use of foveal and peripheral vision to extract 
relevant information (Roca et al., 2018; Vandendriessche et al., 
2012; Vater et al., 2020). Opponents positioned at greater view­
ing eccentricities pose a challenge for observers because players 
must either use peripheral vision to extract important informa­
tion or direct gaze further away from the ball-carrier to accurately 
process the opponent’s movement, potentially missing out on 
vital lower-level (e.g., postural) or higher-level (e.g., tactical) 
information in the foveal region. Our findings indicate that 
players mainly look at the player in possession of the ball (over

Findings from the eccentricity analyses are in line with those 
from studies involving driving, where the ability to use periph­

a review, see Vater et al., 2022). Drivers glance back towards 
the road more often when attending to objects at higher 
eccentricities from the road (Svärd et al., 2021). Additionally, 
drivers have slower braking response times for road hazards 
when attending to objects at greater eccentricities, presumably 
because peripheral vision is not well-suited to extract impor­
tant information on the road when eccentricities are too great 
(Berg et al., 2007; Lamble et al., 1999; Svärd et al., 2021).

sensitivity (i.e., higher accuracy and faster responses) to oppo­
nents closer to the ball-carrier – likely the most important 
information source for task performance since. Besides scenar­

come from the player in possession. Published reports suggest 
that players can use advanced kinematic information from the 
ball-carrier to anticipate where the next pass will end up 
(Smeeton et al., 2019). For instance, the ball-carrier may glance 
or orient their body towards a future pass location, which is 
a smaller detail that foveal vision would be best suited to detect 
(Carrasco, 2011; Land, 2006). Thus, maintaining foveal vision 
closer to the ball-carrier should be advantageous in most sce­
narios. However, this visual strategy might not be optimal for 
defending against opponents at greater eccentricities, as 
players may be more susceptible to deception from the ball- 
carrier. For successful anticipation of opponents at greater 
eccentricities, players may need to rely more so on situational

An alternative reason for impaired response accuracies and 
response times at larger eccentricities could be due to the 
“crowding phenomenon”, which refers to impaired object 
recognition when the stimulus is surrounded closely by other 
targets and distractors (Levi, 2008; Ringer et al., 2021; 
Strasburger, 2020; Vater et al., 2017b). Since there is an increas­
ing number of teammates and opponents between the ball- 
carrier and player cutting towards the basket from eccentricity 
1 to 4, crowding rather than viewing eccentricity could have 
reduced the ability to make use of peripheral vision and avoid 
response time and accuracy impairments. The crowding phe­
nomena may explain why response times were faster at eccen­
tricity 1 and 3 compared to eccentricity 4 but did not differ 
between eccentricity 2 and 4. It could have been more difficult 
to perceive positional changes of players closest to the ball- 
carrier (eccentricity 2) compared to players further away (eccen­
tricity 3) because further players at eccentricity 3 are not as 
crowded near the ball-carrier. Moreover, crowding could 
explain the high number of fixations to peripheral players, 
which are useful to update visual information with foveal rather 
than peripheral vision. However, impaired performance at 
eccentricity 4 implies that there is a critical threshold for the 
distance at which players can reliably be detected. Thus, players 
may be most sensitive to movement changes at a relatively 
fixed radius around the ball-carrier, and opponents at 
eccentricity 4 may have generally fallen outside this area of 
attentional focus resulting in worse performance when these 
further opponents cut to the basket.
As mentioned previously, it is relevant to consider that 
experienced players in our study were using situational prob­
abilities, and players at eccentricity 1 and eccentricity 3 may be 
the most likely to threaten the basket in typical game scenarios. 
In our study, there were more trials where players at eccentri­
city 3 received a pass, so participants may have updated their 
situational probabilities as the experiment continued (Thomas 
et al., 2022). Notably though, response time was faster at 
eccentricity 3 compared to eccentricity 4 while accuracy did 
not differ between these eccentricities, suggesting that players 
may have been more sensitive to eccentricity 3 but could not 
pick up reliable information for superior performance. Further 
research on game intelligence and contextual priors would be 
necessary to disentangle these results.
Previous reports have demonstrated that anxiety can narrow 
peripheral attention, reducing the ability of athletes to extract 
information via peripheral vision (Janelle et al., 1992; Murray & 
Janelle, 2003; Williams & Elliott, 1999). We expected that added 
performance pressure would result in more frequent fixations 
towards further eccentricities as indicated by time spent fixat­
ing on the ball-carrier, viewing eccentricity off the ball, percen­
tage of fixations to peripheral locations, and gaze distance off 
the ball. However, pressure generally did not influence any of 
these gaze measures or performance on the primary task.

According to ACT, anxiety increases working memory 
demands, which causes individuals to prioritize limited atten­
tional resources on the primary task at the expense of per­
formance on any secondary tasks (Alder et al., 2018; 
Derakshan & Eysenck, 2009; Wilson, Chattlngton, et al. 
2009). Yet, participants in the current study reported greater 
mental effort and demonstrated improved secondary task 
(counting) performance during the HP condition, while pri­
mary task performance (response time and accuracy) was not 
affected by performance pressure. Participants were 
instructed that both primary and secondary task performance 
were equally weighted in terms of performance, so a logical 
conclusion is that the level of anxiety experienced was not 
sufficient to negatively impact working memory resources to 
a point where task trade-offs would occur. Importantly 
though, improved secondary task performance did not 
come at the cost of poorer primary task performance. 
Working memory demands under pressure likely did not 
exceed a critical capacity to impair performance on either 
the primary or secondary task; that is, the basketball decision- 
making task may have been simple enough to reserve extra 
cognitive resources (mental effort) for better counting perfor­
mance in the HP condition. Alternatively, it is possible that 
feelings of anxiety were not perceived as debilitative to per­
formance, and instead broadly encouraged enhanced vigi­
lance towards both tasks (Akinola et al., 2016; Beltzer et al., 
2014; Mellalieu et al., 2006).
Our findings highlight the potential of using HMD’s with 
360° video stimuli to test and train perceptual-cognitive skills 
in high level performers, as well as the challenges scientists face 
when attempting to induce anxiety through pressure within 
non-competitive laboratory settings. Therefore, some limita­
tions are worth acknowledging when interpreting the data. 
First, given participant immersion in the simulated environ­
ment and separation from the experimenter, the false feedback 
we provided may not have been as effective as demonstrated 
from previously collected pilot data or past literature (Oh et al., 
2019). More work with simulated reality environments is neces­
sary to determine the optimal approach for inducing psycho­
physiological responses to increased task demands and 
pressure. Second, to ensure participants had adequate motiva­
tion and effort during the LP condition, we did not counter­
balance HP and LP conditions. Such an approach has been 
taken by researchers examining anxiety and choking under 
pressure to preserve motivation during LP conditions (Beilock 
& Carr, 2001; Mesagno & Mullane-Grant, 2010; Wang et al., 
2004). Furthermore, although our decision not to counterba­
lance might have elicited learning effects for the second con­
dition (high-pressure), performance decrements should be 
harder to induce under HP since participants are more prac­
ticed, which would make any observed behavioral or perfor­
mance changes due to pressure more meaningful. Finally, the 
perspective of the center position was used when developing 
film scenarios for the experimental task to ensure maximum 
viewing distance consistent to parameters of the simulated 
environment. While all participants had some playing experi­
ence at the centre position, some were more experienced in 
adjacent small-forward or power-forward positions. Therefore, 
findings may have been clearer if a more homogenous sample 
was recruited with respect to playing position experience.
In conclusion, findings suggest that basketball players have 
higher expectancies of opponents attacking the basket at lower 
eccentricities during dynamic decision-making scenarios. This 
prioritization of information near the ball-carrier appears to 
come at the cost of impaired performance when reacting to 
opponents at further viewing eccentricities. Participants 
viewed the player in ball possession over 60% of the time, yet 
more than 50% of their total fixations landed on peripheral 
players (i.e., players without the ball). This latter strategy sug­
gests that experienced basketball athletes prefer a risk profile 
associated with more effective extraction of critical momentary 
information from the ball-carrier at the cost of slower responses 
to opponents at further eccentricities, albeit while still allocat­
ing some attention peripherally to “hedge their bets”. Null 
effects from the pressure manipulation on gaze behaviour 
and task performance suggest a need for large sample sizes, 
feedback tied to real-time performance, and utilization of mea­
sures sensitive to the idiosyncratic effects of anxiety among 
athletes. In future, researchers should explore whether situa­
tional probabilities buffer performers from impaired peripheral 
perception strategies with stimuli at even greater eccentricities.

Response Accuracy. A significant main effect of Eccentricity was documented for response accuracy on the primary task, F(3, 56) = 25.88, p < .001, η2 = 
.477. Post-hoc follow up tests indicated that response accuracy was significantly higher at eccentricity 1 compared to
eccentricity 2 (p = .008, r = .802), eccentricity 3 (p = .034, r = .699), and eccentricity 4 (p < .001, r = .879), and significantly lower at eccentricity 4 compared

.291). No significant main effect for Condition, F(1, 44) = .39, p = .538, η2 = .006, or interactions between Condition and Eccentricity were revealed, F(3, 44) = 
.68, p = .568, η2 = .030.
Secondary Task Performance. Counting performance was significantly better in the HP compared to LP condition (p < .001, r = .840), showing that 
participants named more numbers on average per trial under added performance pressure.
Gaze Measures. Comparisons of visual search behavior between low- and high-pressure conditions revealed that less time was spent fixating on the 
ball-carrier in the high-pressure condition than the low-pressure condition (p = .044, r = .504). No significant differences were evident between conditions

gaze eccentricity off the ball (p = .665, r = .131), and gaze distance from the ball (p = .074, r = .452). Additionally, no significant effects emerged between 
gaze distance from the ball-carrier and response time (p = .079, τ = .333).
