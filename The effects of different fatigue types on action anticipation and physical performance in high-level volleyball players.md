Abstract 
12 
This study examined the effects of different fatigue types on action anticipation and physical performance 
13 
in high-level volleyball players. Twenty-four participants underwent four counterbalanced conditions: 
14 
60-min of cycling at 60% peak power output, 60-min Stroop task, 60-min cycling at 60% peak power 
15 
output with Stroop task and 60-min neutral documentary to induce physical fatigue (PF), mental fatigue 
16 
(MF), dual fatigue (DF) and control group (CG), respectively. Action anticipation (anticipation test and 
17 
visual search test) and physical performance (countermovement jump, T-test, and spike test) were 
18 
conducted at baseline, immediately after (Post1), and 40-min after fatigue intervention (Post2). DF and 
19 
PF led to significantly lower jump height, T-test completion time, and spiking speed at Post1 and Post2 
20 
compared with CG and MF. Compared with PF, DF led to a significant decline in jumping, agility, and 
21 
spiking performance at Post1 and decreased jumping performance at Post2. MF significantly decreased 
22 
reaction time in the anticipation test at Post1 compared with CG. Reaction times in the visual search test 
23 
were significantly lower with DF and MF at Post2 compared with CG. PF led to a greater decline in 
24 
physical performance and this was exacerbated in DF, yet anticipation performance remained lesser 
25 
impact.

33

39 
understanding of how fatigue affects action anticipation and physical performance is crucial for devising 
40

41 
Fatigue is a multifaceted symptom that affects various aspects of the body, encompassing 
42 
cardiorespiratory, neuromuscular, physiological, and cognitive functions (Bestwick-Stevenson et al., 2022; 
43 
Knicker, Renshaw, Oldham, et al., 2011; Pageaux & Lepers, 2018). It can be classified into different types, 
44 
each with unique characteristics. Physical fatigue (PF) is a physiological phenomenon marked by 
45 
decreased neural excitability in the motor cortex, leading to an inability to sustain the necessary force 
46 
output following prolonged physical activities (Thomas et al., 2017). PF diminishes neuromuscular 
47 
capacity, primarily affecting strength, speed, and endurance (Lin et al., 2021; Marcora et al., 2008; 
48 
Skurvydas et al., 2011). Additionally, PF has been associated with impaired anticipation and 
49 
decision-making abilities, possibly associated with reduced activation in the prefrontal cortex (D. B. 
50 
Alder et al., 2019; Parkin et al., 2017). Mental fatigue (MF) is a psychological state induced by prolonged 
51 
cognitive activity or high cognitive demand, resulting in a feeling of exhaustion or weariness (Marcora et 
52 
al., 2009). MF affects the activation of the prefrontal cortex, reducing attentional resources and, 
53 
consequently, affecting cognitive function, technical abilities, and tactical execution (Filipas et al., 2021; 
54 
Kosack et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2016). Numerous studies have demonstrated the detrimental effects of 
55 
MF on endurance (Holgado et al., 2021; Penna et al., 2018), training volume (Queiros et al., 2021) and 
56 
balance (Noe et al., 2021). However, its influence on neuromuscular performance, such as explosive 
57 
power and maximal strength, appears to be negligible (Duncan et al., 2015; Pageaux et al., 2015).

Many sport-related and non-sport-related situations impose high cognitive demands, which can lead 
59 
to MF (Thompson et al., 2021). Extended training sessions or competitions are more likely to lead to the 
60 
accumulation of physical and mental loads. There are common neuromodulatory pathways between PF 
61 
and MF (Mehta & Parasuraman, 2014). The combination of PF and MF, known as dual fatigue (DF), can 
62 
exacerbate the inhibition of prefrontal cortex activity and result in more severe impairments compared 
63 
with the negative effects of single type of fatigue (D. Alder et al., 2021; Dailey et al., 2015; Kelly et al., 
64 
2012). Barzegarpoor et al. reported that, in male recreational cyclists, DF negatively affected endurance 
65 
more than PF did, with no significant differences observed in heart rate or maximum voluntary 
66 
contraction (Barzegarpoor et al., 2020). The impairment caused by DF appears to be primarily 
67 
psychologically rather than physiologically mediated. However, Rubio-Morales et al. found that, among 
68 
physically active university students, only MF resulted in significant impairments in reaction time 
69 
following exposure to DF, MF, and PF (Rubio-Morales et al., 2022). A plausible explanation for these 
70 
inconsistent results is that fatigue may manifest differently among athletes participating in various sports 
71 
disciplines and at different levels. 
72 
Open-skill sports demand that athletes respond to unpredictable environments, demanding enhanced 
73 
visual attention, precision in execution, and decision-making abilities (Bishop et al., 2013; Kim et al., 
74 
2008; Nakamoto & Mori, 2008). However, the effects of different fatigue types on high-level athletes 
75 
may have different characteristics (Coyne et al., 2021). Parkin et al. reported that PF can enhance rather 
76 
than impair decision-making performance in top athletes (6 Olympic medalists) (Parkin et al., 2017). 
77 
High-level athletes demonstrate higher resistance to fatigue (Kosack et al., 2020), and they consistently 
78 
exhibit superior physical and cognitive performance compared to novice and younger individuals (De 
79 
Waelle et al., 2021; Piras et al., 2014; Trecroci et al., 2021). Understanding the effects of different fatigue 
80 
types on action anticipation and physical performance can help in designing targeted training 
81 
interventions for high-level volleyball players. However, no existing research has delved into the distinct 
82 
effects of different types of fatigue on action anticipation and physical performance in high-level 
83 
volleyball players.

85 
action anticipation and physical performance among high-level volleyball players. As noted by Thomas et

Physical performance 
118 
Agility was assessed using the modified T-test (SASSI et al., 2009), involving a combination of 
119 
forward sprint, lateral slides, and a backward run. Participants initiated the test with a forward sprint 
120 
covering a distance of 5 m, where they touched a central cone with their right hand. While maintaining a 
121 
forward orientation and avoiding crossing their feet, they moved 2.5 m to the left, touching a cone with 
122 
their left hand. Following this, they swiftly slid 5 m to the right, ensuring contact with a cone with their 
123 
right hand. Subsequently, they reversed direction, gliding back to the left to touch the central cone. The 
124 
test concluded with participants sprinting backward to the starting line as rapidly as possible. Each 
125 
participant performed two attempts, with a brief 1–2 min rest between them. The time was measured 
126 
using timing gates (Wireless Timing Systems, Shiniangong, China). The reliability of the modified T-test 
127 
was assessed using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC; ICC = 0.899). 
128 
Jumping performance was assessed using the countermovement jump (CMJ). During this assessment, 
129 
participants started from an upright standing position with hands on their hips and jumped as high as 
130 
possible. This involved descending to their usual squat depth, followed by an explosive jump to attain 
131 
maximum height. The landing was executed in an athletic position. Each participant completed three 
132 
maximal attempts, with a 30-s interval between each repetition. The CMJ measurements were captured 
133 
using an optical jump system (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy). The CMJ test showed an ICC of 0.982. 
134 
The spike test (ST) was performed according to a previously described protocol (Valades, 2009). In 
135 
brief, players were tasked with hitting a ball with maximal force toward a target zone (1.5 × 1.5 m). 
136 
Participants were required to self-toss the ball and execute the spike without jumping or lifting their feet 
137 
off the ground. A researcher stood to the side to monitor the performance closely. If any aspect of the 
138 
execution was incorrect, the test was repeated. A Bushnell Radar Gun (Bushnell Outdoor Products, 
139 
Kansas, USA) was positioned to measure the velocity of the ball within the target zone. Rigorous 
140 
monitoring of ball pressure, radar orientation, and calibration was undertaken for each test. Each player 
141 
was granted three trials, and the average speed was recorded. The ST showed an ICC of 0.967.

In the anticipation test (AT) (De Waelle et al., 2021), videos were captured at the center of the court, 
145 
positioned at a height of 1.30 m above the ground, using a high-definition digital camera (Sony A7M4; 
146 
Sony, Tokyo, Japan) with a frame rate of 50 frames/s. The video clips illustrated a situation in which a 
147 
free ball was expertly distributed to the setter’s position. Afterward, the setter transferred the ball to one 
148 
of three conditions: (1) a long backward pass to the outside spiker, (2) a brief forward pass to the central 
149 
spiker, or (3) an extended forward pass to the other outer spiker (right side). As the play neared the set-up 
150 
phase, the video was occluded (i.e., a black screen was presented), referred to as “viewing conditions”. 
151 
Specifically, the videos were intentionally occluded at the moment the ball was received by the setter. 
152 
Participants were instructed to respond promptly and with precision, identifying which of the three 
153 
passing options the setter would choose by pressing the corresponding key on the keyboard. To ensure 
154 
that participants understood the test, they received a comprehensive explanation, which included a 
155 
familiarization clip and practice trials. During the test, a total of 100 video clips were presented, each 
156 
lasting about 2–4 s. These clips were displayed in a randomized order for each participant to mitigate any 
157 
potential order effects. Participants were granted a 5-s window within which to respond. Response times 
158 
(ATRT) and accuracy (ATACC) were recorded as critical performance measures. The ICC showed 
159 
reliable results, with mean values of 0.861 for ACCRT and 0.893 for ATACC. 
160 
In the visual search test (SVT) (Parkin & Walsh, 2017), participants were required to conduct a 
161 
visual search within an array to determine the presence or absence of a specific target image—in this case, 
162 
a red square. The visual array included three different types of distractors: green squares, green circles, 
163 
and red circles. The groups were divided into 3, 6, and 9 items according to the number of displayed 
164 
items. Two different trial types were included: 1) the target was present, and the display item featured a 
165 
red square; or 2) the target was not present, and the display item did not contain a red square. Throughout 
166 
the test, a total of 156 clips were shown across two blocks. Participants could rest between blocks. 
167 
Response time (SVTRT) was recorded. The ICC provided strong evidence of reliability, with mean values 
168 
of 0.912.

Discussion 
319 
This study aimed to investigate the effects of different fatigue types on action anticipation and 
320 
physical performance in high-level volleyball players. The main findings of this study showed that DF 
321 
and PF significantly reduced jump height and led to a decrease in the T-test completion time and spike 
322 
speed compared with MF and CG. DF led to significantly decrease jump height, T-test completion time, 
323 
and spike speed at Post 1 and CMJ height at Post 2 compared with PF. Conversely, MF did not lead to any 
324 
significant effects on these parameters. Notably, neither DF nor PF had a significant effect on action 
325 
anticipation in high-level volleyball players compared with the CG condition. MF led to significantly 
326 
decreased reaction times in the anticipation test at Post 1 compared with CG. DF and MF also led to 
327 
significantly decreased reaction times in the visual search test at Post 2 compared with CG. 
328 
PF had a negative impact on jumping, agility, and spiking performance when compared with MF and 
329 
CG. These results were in accordance with a study by Lin et al., which highlighted the adverse effects of 
330 
PF on jumping ability and landing strategy in volleyball players (Lin et al., 2021). Skurvydas et al. 
331 
showed that PF decreased maximum voluntary contraction and the central activation ratio in 
332 
long-distance runners, sprint runners, volleyball players, and untrained participants (Skurvydas et al., 
333 
2011). Marcora et al. showed that PF significantly affected cardiorespiratory responses and exercise 
334 
performance during high-intensity cycling in healthy men undergoing regular aerobic exercise (Marcora 
335 
et al., 2008). PF is regulated by a dual system balancing facilitation and inhibition, involving key brain 
336 
regions such as the limbic system, basal ganglia, motor cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and anterior 
337 
cingulate cortex (Tanaka et al., 2014; Tanaka & Watanabe, 2011). In the context of PF, the neural 
338 
excitability of the motor cortex decreases, thereby affecting neuromuscular performance (Thomas et al., 
339 
2017). This implies that PF may reduce motor cortex activation, thereby leading to a decline in physical 
340 
performance among high-level athletes. 
341 
For the MF condition in the present study, the results showed that MF did not influence jumping, 
342 
agility, or spiking performance among high-level volleyball players compared with the CG condition. 
343 
This was in line with a study by Kosack et al., which noted that MF induced by a 60-min Stroop task did 
344 
not significantly affect CMJ height or badminton performance (Kosack et al., 2020). Holgado et al. found 
345 
that MF induced by a 90-min AX-continuous performance test did not affect endurance in adult men

354 
2018). This may stem from differences in brain function and motor performance among different 
355 
individuals. High-level athletes often contend with diverse psychological pressures, including injuries, 
356 
errors, and negative feedback from spectators, coaches, teammates, and interpersonal conflicts, 
357 
competition, and rewards (Thompson et al., 2021), while also possessing higher cognitive functions. 
358 
These findings suggest that isolated MF may not significantly affect motor cortex excitability and 
359 
neuromuscular capacity among high-level volleyball players. 
360 
These results suggested that the combination of physical and mental loads has detrimental effects on 
361 
jumping, agility, and spiking performance compared with MF alone and the CG condition. Notably, DF 
362

364

365 
Fuentes-García et al., 2021). Studies have indicated that DF leads to heightened inhibition of prefrontal 
366

367 
resulting in a faster onset of fatigue compared to single-type fatigue conditions (R. Xu et al., 2018). This 
368

Limitations 
404 
Despite the use of counterbalancing in the present study, it must be acknowledged that conducting 
405 
multiple measurements could potentially induce learning effects during the action anticipation task and 
406 
visual search test. Additionally, fatigue-induced tasks lack high ecological validity. Russel et al. reported 
407 
that current research lacks perceptual–cognitive sport-specific tasks that can help us better understand the 
408 
implications of MF on high-level sporting performance (Russell et al., 2019). The conclusions of this 
409 
study are based on the experimental scenario, and their applicability to real matches or training settings 
410 
still requires further verification. Sport-based tasks should be used to induce fatigue in future studies. The 
411 
present investigations focused on the immediate and short-term consequences of fatigue, neglecting 
412 
potential enduring or long-term effects. It is essential for future longitudinal studies to explore the 
413 
prolonged effects of fatigue on athletic performance. The absence of notable differences in action 
414 
anticipation may indicate the presence of unexamined variables influencing performance. Subsequent 
415 
research should incorporate neurophysiological tools such as electroencephalography and functional 
416 
magnetic resonance imaging to explore the neurological mechanisms underlying fatigue and its influence 
417 
on both cognitive and physical performance (Tran et al., 2020). The current study’s investigation into 
418 
action anticipation aligned with the cognitive skills that neurofeedback training aims to enhance, 
419 
including concentration, attention, and decision-making—all vital for peak sports performance. The 
420 
current findings regarding the effects of different forms of fatigue on cognitive function have practical 
421 
applications in designing neurofeedback protocols. By customizing these protocols, we could enhance 
422 
athletes’ abilities to withstand MF, thereby potentially improving their predictive skills during 
423 
competition. This approach underscores the importance of neurofeedback in boosting key cognitive skills 
424 
for athletes and points to a strategic direction for future research aimed at mitigating the detrimental 
425 
effects of fatigue on performance (Cheng & Hung, 2020).
