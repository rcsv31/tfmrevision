Abstract 
The objective of this research was to analyze the duration of visual fixations of novice and 
expert basketball players before executing a motor action in a triple-threat situation while facing 
four defensive formations: man-to-man defense, 2-3 zone, 1-2-2 zone, and 1-3-1 zone. The 
results showed that man-to-man defense required more fixation time for expert offensive 
players when facing the 2-3 zone. Additionally, expert players exhibited significantly shorter 
fixation times compared to novices when facing the 2-3 and 1-2-2 defenses. The complexity of 
defensive movements and the constant pressure in man-to-man defense could be a determining 
factor that caused longer fixation times in expert players compared to the 2-3 defense, which 
has predetermined defensive positioning zones that may facilitate reading the game. On the 
other hand, the shorter visual fixations in expert players compared to novices in the 2-3 and 12-2 defenses highlight the importance of experience in developing efficient visual search 
strategies in basketball. 
 
Key words: Decision making, eye tracking, gaze, perception.

Introduction 
In the field of high-performance sports, perceptual ability has been identified as a crucial 
factor in determining athletes’ tactical skills. Mann et al. (2007) state that athletes with greater 
experience in capturing visual information from their environment exhibit more precise and 
efficient tactical performance. When addressing the concept of visual search, it is essential to 
break down its components to understand its functioning. The key ocular mechanism for 
capturing detailed information from the environment is known as ocular fixation (Rayner, 
2009). This process is carried out by the fovea voluntarily, focusing on a specific point in the 
environment for a duration that varies between 66 and 416 milliseconds (ms), with an average 
of around 218 ms (Lunati et al., 2013). 
Regarding fixation times, there is controversy in the scientific literature. While Reina 
and del Campo (2004), argue that 60 ms are sufficient to consider a fixation, Williams et al. 
(1994), suggest a minimum of 120 ms. This difference reflects the debate within the scientific 
community about the necessary time to classify an ocular fixation. 
Visual fixations play a central role in research on visual behavior, as they allow for 
understanding the perceptual and cognitive processes activated during the visual exploration of 
the environment. The analysis of total fixation time provides insights into how individuals 
direct their attention and process visual information (Rayner, 2009). The duration of fixations 
varies according to the type of stimulus, the task to be performed and the characteristics of the 
observer. Complex or novel visual stimuli tend to generate more fixations, as they require more 
visual processing, whereas simple or predictable stimuli elicit fewer fixations (Orquin and 
Mueller, 2013). 
The duration of eye fixation provides valuable data on how individuals assimilate and 
process visual information. Short fixations may indicate fast and efficient processing, where 
information is extracted in a short time, while prolonged fixations may suggest more thorough 
processing or difficulties in extracting relevant information (Orquin and Mueller, 2013). 
Although they are not the object of study in this research, there are other variables of 
visual behaviour that are also studied, such as: i) the place of fixation, ii) the time of fixation in 
each place and iii) the order or sequence in the observation points (Manzanares, Menayo and 
Segado, 2024; Ripoll, 1988). Likewise, among the actions performed by the pupil, we can 
distinguish tracking movements, which refer to the execution of following a moving object and 
reaches its maximum operating capacity at 100 degrees per second. On the other hand, saccadic

movements are studied, which are rapid movements of both eyes in the same direction, 
responsible for the rapid changes necessary to fixate important sources of information separated 
from each other (Babu et al., 2005). 
The aforementioned ocular actions are fundamental to the performance of athletes, as 
they directly influence their ability to identify and process visual stimuli in the environment. In 
this context, the stimuli that expert athletes focus on are used to predict and anticipate actions 
(Mann et al., 2007). As some research has shown, the fixations of experts are smaller in quantity 
and with a longer duration in the points they focus on compared to novices (Pérez et al., 2013). 
In this sense, Vickers (2016) mentions that experts maintain fixations 62% longer than nonexperts. 
On the other hand, although it is true that the study of visual behaviour in sports science 
dates back to the 1970s, with authors such as Bard et al. (1970), these studies were only carried 
out using photographs (Ruíz and Arruza, 2005). Over the years, studies have evolved to 
situations more similar to reality, as it has been shown that eye behaviours are different when 
an athlete is placed in front of a photograph or video in relation to a real scenario (Reina et al., 
2006; del Campo, 2015). Therefore, it is the in situ studies that will provide the most relevant 
information regarding the athlete's visual behaviour (Maarseveen and Oudejans, 2018). 
This leads us to reconsider the results found in past research and place more emphasis 
on analyzing visual behavior in competition-like situations, as contextual factors can modify 
gaze tracking. For example, in basketball, it has been found that players exhibit shorter fixation 
times and higher frequency of fixations when in states of anxiety, whereas calmer basketball 
players show longer fixation durations (Harle and Vickers, 2001). This leads us to think that in 
situ competition scenarios condition and modify athletes’ behavior. As demonstrated by Roja 
et al. (2000), the presence of an opponent modifies, for example, the height, speed, and angle 
of a basketball shot. Additionally, Gibson (1979) posits that there is an interdependence 
between perception and action, so a scenario closer to reality will provide more accurate 
information about the athlete’s visual behavior. 
Thus, studies concerning visual behavior in basketball range from gaze tracking in 
coaches during the game (Sousa and Pereira, 2013), predicting successful free throws between 
expert and novice gaze (Ishibashi, 2010), analyzing the gaze of defensive players on the ball 
handler (Sayazo, 2009), to the visual behavior of players performing pick and roll in 3x3 games 
(Van Maarseveen, 2018).

A recent study examining senior basketball players revealed that visual strategies during 
shooting are modulated by environmental constraints. Specifically, players exhibited fewer 
fixations on the rim when shooting against active defensive opposition compared to unopposed 
shots. This adaptive behaviour likely reflects the need to allocate attentional resources between 
the target (rim) and the opponent. On the other hand, visual strategies remained unchanged 
when basketball shooting was done under simulated gym audience noise (Amaro et al., 2024). 
Similar results were found by Van Maarseveen and Oudejans (2018), showing that youth 
basketball players exhibited distinct gaze behavior when executing jump shots both with a 
contest shot (against live defense) and without defensive pressure. When facing a contest shot, 
players demonstrated a later final fixation onset and extended fixation duration on the defender 
compared to undefended shots. Performance differences emerged clearly, with lowerperforming participants showing shorter absolute and relative final fixation durations, along 
with an earlier final fixation offset specifically in defended shooting situations. These findings 
confirm that defensive opposition significantly modifies visual patterns during basketball 
shooting compared to uncontested attempts. 
Consistent with these findings, Zhao et al. (2023) revealed distinct gaze patterns during 
free-throw execution, with experts exhibiting significantly fewer fixations on the front, bottom, 
and top-right basket regions compared to novices, and prolonged fixation durations on the front 
and top basket areas. The results suggest that experts employ more economical visual search 
strategies, allocating attention to task-relevant areas. These findings align with Marques et al. 
(2023), who demonstrated differences in visual attention patterns between U16 and professional 
basketball players during jump shots from various court locations. Their eye-tracking analysis 
revealed that professional athletes exhibited longer first fixation durations, extended quiet eye 
periods, and greater shooting accuracy across all tested angles. These results robustly establish 
competitive experience as a key determinant of visual search efficiency in basketball shooting. 
 However, although studies on visual behavior in basketball are increasing, there are 
still many knowledge gaps to be discovered. In this regard, when reviewing scientific articles 
about the visual fixations of the attacker against different defensive formations in a 5 vs 5 
situation, there is no data available. Therefore, the sources of information on visual behavior in 
basketball mainly rely on the empirical knowledge of coaches (Coach Frikki, 2022). 
The previous review invites us to analyze gaze behavior in situ, as the number of studies 
in real sports contexts is still scarce, and even more so, 5x5 game situations in basketball have 

--- [Página 6] ---
not been studied. These actions are among the most used in this sport, representing around 60% 
of total attacks (Crespo and Pilas, 2017), and therefore, justify further study. Therefore, the 
objective of this research has been to analyze the duration of visual fixations in novice and 
expert players before executing a motor action, in a triple-threat situation during a 5x5 game, 
considering different defensive formations in basketball. The study is expected to demonstrate 
that more experienced players will have shorter fixation durations than novices when facing 
different defensive formations in a triple-threat situation in basketball. 
Materials y methods 
Participants 
This study involved 21 expert and novice basketball players (N=21), selected nonprobabilistically through expert judgment. The group of expert players consisted of 10 female 
players (N=10) from the senior category of the Real Murcia basketball team. This group had an 
average weight of 64.90±4.93 kg, a height of 171.80±4.34 cm, and an age of 19.40±2.17 years. 
The group of 11 novice players (N=11) included 5 undergraduate students and 6 master’s 
students from the Faculty of Sport at the Catholic University of San Antonio de Murcia. This 
group had an average weight of 78.73±11.89 kg, a height of 179.45±8.35 cm, and an age of 
26.55±6.21 years. All participants gave their consent to participate in the research. The study 
was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Catholic University of Murcia with code 
CE072009. 
Experimental Procedures and Data Collection 
To analyze visual behavior of basketball players, a warm-up was conducted prior to the 
measurement process. This warm-up included cephalocaudal joint mobility exercises, 10 
minutes of continuous running to raise body temperature, and exercises for mobilizing the lower 
and upper limbs, dynamic flexibility, and post-activation potentiation through horizontal and 
vertical jumps with unipedal support. Finally, from the triple-threat position, behind the threepoint line, and with passive defense, 10 one-on-one confrontations were performed from the 
right or left perimeter, according to the attacker’s preference. 
The complete experimental procedure required 4 hours of total testing time across all 
participants (Figure 1). At the end of the warm-up, the players were equipped with the eyetracking system, and a familiarization process with the test to be performed was carried out. 
During this period, three actual trials of the sports task were executed. The test began with a

controlled 5x5 offense, where the player received the ball in a triple-threat position from either 
the left or right perimeter, depending on their preference. The player performed 4 repetitions of 
each of the following tasks: i) Task 1: 1-2-2 defense, ii) Task 2: 2-3 defense, iii) Task 3: 1-3-1 
defense, iv) Task 4: Man-to-man defense. 
During the sports task, according to the player's discretion, they could choose between 
using the open or cross attack step to penetrate (Freestyle Sports, 2022), take a jump shot, 
dribble, or pass. A 1-minute rest was given between each task.

Visual behavior recording 
All participants evaluated for visual behavior used the Tobii Pro Glasses 2 Version 1.1.3 
at 50Hz, with an h.264 camera format and resolution of 1920 x 1080 pixels, at a frequency of 
25fps, within a 90º field of view of the scene camera (16:9). This system allows for the 
recording of fixations and saccadic movements of the pupil through a video image of the 
binocular corneal reflection, using an integrated video camera (Figure 2). To record the 
fixations, the task was divided into three phases: i) Reception. This period starts when the 
athlete requests the ball and ends when they hold it. Fixations made during this phase were not 
counted; ii) 1x1. The player in possession of the ball, in a triple-threat position, analyzes the 
possible solutions to their situation. All fixations and saccadic movements were recorded during 
this phase; iii) Outcome. A movement is performed, such as a shot, pass, penetration with an 
open or cross step, or dribble. During this period, the last fixation before execution was counted.

Statistical analysis 
For the statistical analysis of the data, SPSS software version 29.0 for Windows (SPSS, 
Chicago, IL-USA) was used. The significance level for the statistical tests was set at p ≤ 0.05. 
The Shapiro-Wilk test detected a lack of normality in the data distribution, so non-parametric 
statistical tests were applied in the analysis. To determine whether there were significant intragroup differences in the total fixation times recorded by the players across the four types of 
defensive situations, the Friedman ANOVA was applied. To calculate the effect size, Kendall's 
coefficient of concordance (W) was applied, which provides a measure of the consistency of 
the rankings across the blocks. The values range from 0, indicating no agreement between the 
rankings, to 1, which is interpreted as perfect agreement. To confirm the effects of experience—
experts vs. novices—on total fixation times based on the type of defense, the non-parametric 
Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied. To determine the effect size, eta squared (η²) was calculated, 
which provides a measure of the magnitude of the effect of the differences between the types 
of defense for each of the two player groups. The values range from 0.01—small effect, 0.06—
moderate effect, and 0.14 or more—large effect.

The analysis of the results for the novice player group did not show significant 
differences in the ranks of total fixation times corresponding to the four types of defense 
(χ²=5.3, p=0.149; W=0.04) suggesting novice players maintain consistent visual patterns 
regardless of defensive scheme. In contrast, differences were observed in the same variable for 
the expert player group (χ²=11.7, p=0.009), although with a small effect size (W=0.1). This 
indicates expert players adapt their visual attention based on defensive configurations. 
Pairwise comparisons were conducted to identify between which types of defense these 
differences existed, using the Bonferroni adjustment. The results indicated that the difference 
in total fixation times between the 2-3 defense and personal defense was significant (χ²=-3.3, 
p<.001) with experts showing shorter fixations in the 2-3 zone. No significant differences were 
observed between the remaining types of defense. 
On the other hand, the results of the analysis indicated significant differences in total 
fixation times in the 1-2-2 defense between expert and novice players (χ²=10.7, p<.001), with 
an effect size ranging from moderate to large (η²=0.11). Similarly, significant differences were 
also observed in the 2-3 defense between both groups (χ²=14.8, p<.001), with a large effect size 
(η²=0.16), revealing these zone defenses as key differentiators in visual behavior. No 
differences were observed between the remaining types of defense. 
Discussion 
The analysis of each attempt by the expert players when facing different defensive 
formations revealed that personal defense took more time in their total fixations than the 2-3 
defense. Additionally, it was observed that the 1-2-2 and 2-3 defenses had longer total fixation 
times for novice players compared to expert players. 
Personal defense requires a high degree of body control, which leads to a greater loss of 
possessions for offensive teams compared to other defensive strategies. This type of defense is 
particularly prevalent among successful teams (Ángel et al., 2006; Sampaio et al., 2016; 
Mahmoud, 2011). It is likely that the prolonged time spent in visual fixations by expert 
offensive players against personal defense in this study is related to the proximity of the 
defender, which makes it difficult to execute comfortable shots (Serna et al., 2022). This 
increased duration of fixations could be attributed to personal defense reducing shooting 
options and increasing the chances of ball theft by the defending team. These characteristics 
may explain the preference for personal defense in senior categories (Ángel et al., 2006; 
Bardavío and Arroyave, 2015).

On the other hand, in the 2-3 zone defense, defenders position themselves to cover 
specific areas of the court rather than following a particular offensive player (Arbex, 2020). 
This organization allows offensive players to have a clearer view of the defense and quickly 
detect openings and anticipate coverages. Consequently, this defensive arrangement could 
explain the shorter fixation times observed in expert players when facing the 2-3 zone defense 
compared to personal defense (Molinuevo, 2003). 
The 2-3 defense in basketball is known for its ability to close spaces and make it difficult for 
the opposing team to take outside shots by positioning two players near the three-point line to 
challenge long-distance attempts. This arrangement also complicates penetrations toward the 
basket, particularly when defenders communicate well and move quickly to close gaps. 
Furthermore, this defense is effective in providing help defense against tall players in the low 
post area (Wissel, 2011; Cunha, 2003). 
For less experienced players, this defensive formation can present a considerable challenge. 
The lack of familiarity with this structure necessitates greater visual exploration effort for 
decision-making, as these players require more time to analyze the situation and anticipate the 
offensive actions of the opponent (Mann et al., 2007; Vine et al., 2011). This is because a wellexecuted defense like the 2-3 would complicate the players' vision of the game for those who 
are not accustomed to facing it. 
In contrast, players with greater sports experience have a significant advantage when facing the 
2-3 defense. Their prior knowledge and exposure to this type of formation allow them to 
identify defensive patterns more quickly, which shortens the time needed for visual fixations 
and facilitates decision-making during the game (Abernethy and Russell, 1987). According to 
Aguilar-Hidalgo (2016), the 2-3 defense is one of the most commonly used in basketball, 
implying that more experienced players have had more opportunities to encounter this 
arrangement and, therefore, have developed a greater ability to recognize and react to its 
patterns. 
Similarly, the 1-2-2 defense showed greater use of time in the total fixations of novice players 
compared to expert players. A possible explanation for this behavior is that this defensive 
formation, by placing one player at the top of the perimeter, two on the midline, and two on the 
low post line, allows for balanced coverage both on the perimeter and in the paint. 
Consequently, it exerts pressure on the perimeter and controls the pace of the game (Zheng et 

--- [Página 12] ---
al., 2010). Such aspects could be a reason for the longer fixation times observed in the novice 
players of this study. 
This type of defense is effective in forcing mistakes from the opposing team, as the defensive 
players can quickly close gaps and compel offensive players to make rushed decisions. 
Furthermore, the 1-2-2 configuration allows defenders to switch between pressure on the threepoint line and interior defense, adapting to the needs of the game in real-time (Rose, 2012). The 
smooth transition between different areas of the court complicates the offensive players' vision 
of the game, especially for less experienced players, who may struggle to identify openings and 
find effective attack angles (Mann et al., 2007; Gómez et al., 2010). 
For players with more experience, the 1-2-2 defense can be managed more effectively due to 
their familiarity with the patterns and rotations necessary to counter it. These players are able 
to anticipate defensive movements and adjust their play accordingly, using fakes and quick 
changes of direction to disrupt the defense (Abernethy and Russell, 1987). Perhaps these are 
some of the reasons why the expert players in this study exhibited shorter total fixation times. 
Experience plays a crucial role in the efficiency of visual fixations during decision-making in 
sports. In the present study, it was found that novice players maintain their visual fixations for 
a significantly longer period than experienced players. This trend can be explained by the 
novices' lower familiarity with defensive situations, which forces them to spend more time 
searching for adaptations that respond to the most relevant stimuli. In contrast, more 
experienced players adapt more quickly due to their greater knowledge and mastery of the 
game, as suggested by Williams et al. (1994), who argue that experienced athletes possess more 
efficient visual tracking mechanisms. 
Additionally, previous studies have reported similar results. For example, Mann et al. (2007) 
demonstrated that expert players tend to make shorter fixations compared to novices, while 
Williams et al. (1994) observed similar patterns in football players with different levels of 
experience. These findings reinforce the hypothesis that experience allows expert players to 
direct their visual attention more effectively toward key areas of the game, as noted by 
Abernethy and Russell (1987). 
However, these results are not uniform across all sports disciplines. For example, Pérez et al. 
(2013) found that expert taekwondo players made fewer visual fixations, but of longer duration, 
compared to novices, suggesting that the duration and number of fixations may vary depending 
on the nature of the sport and the specific task. This phenomenon has also been observed in 

--- [Página 13] ---
other studies on visual search, such as the one by Sáez-Gallego et al. (2013), which investigated 
individuals with different levels of experience in various contexts. 
The variability in visual fixation patterns may be influenced by several factors, such as the 
environment in which the task takes place and the specific characteristics of the task itself 
(Williams, 2004). In this regard, although other studies have shown that experts may spend 
more time on their fixations, these situations have occurred in opposition sports (Pérez et al., 
2013). On the other hand, a collaborative-opposition sport provides more visual elements that 
could complicate the detection of key aspects for a person who is not familiar with that 
environment (Williams, 2004). Moreover, visual behavior can differ significantly between a 
controlled environment and a real competitive situation, a notion that is also supported by more 
recent research (Maarseveen and Oudejans, 2018; Roca et al., 2018). Del Campo et al. (2015) 
highlighted that even within the same discipline, motor tasks can be so diverse that visual 
fixations may vary considerably. 
Conclusion 
The man-to-man defense required a longer visual fixation time compared to the 2-3 defense in 
expert players. This behavior of the offensive players against man-to-man defense may be 
attributed to the greater mobility of the defense in covering spaces based on the characteristics 
of the attacking player. These constantly adjusting spaces can make the offensive player's 
perception of the game more complex and less predictable, slowing down their reaction time. 
This added complexity in perception could explain why man-to-man defense is often preferred 
by successful teams, as it creates more challenging scenarios for the opponent to anticipate. In 
contrast to the 2-3 defense, whose initial positioning leaves the "wings" and "corners" 
unprotected, man-to-man defense forces offensive players to spend more time on their visual 
fixations due to the constant pressure and reduction of shooting opportunities, while increasing 
the chances of ball interception. 
On the other hand, the 2-3 defense allows defensive players to focus on specific areas of the 
court rather than following a particular player, giving offensive players a better overall view of 
the defensive setup. This enables them to quickly identify open spaces and gaps in coverage, 
thus reducing the time needed for their visual fixations. However, this perceptual advantage 
may be limited when facing man-to-man defense, which is specifically designed to minimize 
such opportunities.

The differences in total fixation time between novice and expert players in this study became 
evident when facing the 1-2-2 and 2-3 defenses. Experienced players demonstrated a superior 
ability to discern and focus their attention on critical aspects of game dynamics with less time 
spent on their total fixations, a trait that is likely a result of their experience. 
This perceptual mastery could be related to the efficiency of their visual tracking mechanisms. 
Unlike novice players, experts exhibited shorter durations in their visual fixations, suggesting 
a quicker and more effective adaptation on the court. This phenomenon could be explained by 
the ability of experienced players to direct their attention more precisely toward critical areas, 
thereby optimizing their performance. 
The difference in competencies between novice and expert players could be attributed to the 
former being in an early stage of development, still lacking the visual skill and dexterity of their 
more experienced players. This translates into a less effective approach to identifying key areas. 
This disparity highlights the relevance of experience in the development of visual perceptual 
skills. 
Limitations 
Although this study was conducted in situ, certain situations could not be replicated, such as 
the intensity of the game, stress from the public, stress on the referee, and the context of the 
season (team position in the league, game timing, quarter of the game, and fatigue). All these 
factors may create circumstances that could alter player perception. Moreover, even though the 
study involved expert players, it could be interesting to test this protocol with first-division 
expert players to determine whether the patterns remain consistent or not. 
Likewise, the data collected in this investigation does not account for information about 
peripheral vision, which provides crucial input for player decision-making. Similarly, 
individual playing styles which may predispose athletes to certain movement patterns were not 
considered, potentially influencing the visual search outcomes. 
Practical applications 
The findings of this study offer practical applications for coaches seeking to enhance their 
team's defensive performance. By analyzing player decision-making patterns and spatial 
awareness, these insights can help design more effective defensive strategies tailored to specific 
game scenarios. Furthermore, the data provides a framework for adapting defensive 

--- [Página 15] ---
organization based on the opponent's tactical approach and skill level, allowing for dynamic ingame adjustments. 
Beyond strategy development, this knowledge serves as a valuable coaching tool for player 
development. It can be used to train athletes in high-performance defensive systems, 
emphasizing situational awareness, quick decision-making, and optimal positioning. 
Additionally, the study’s methodology could be incorporated into training drills to simulate 
real-match conditions, bridging the gap between theory and practical application. Ultimately, 
these contributions may lead to more structured and adaptable defensive play at both individual 
and team levels.

Areas where the players made fixations. 
1) Free space between players: This refers to fixations made in the free space between two 
players. 
2) Space outside the players: Fixations made in areas outside the playing zones between 
players. 
3) Space between the direct defender and the attacker: These are the fixations made by the 
offensive player with the ball in the space created between their direct defender and 
themselves. 
4) Outside teammates on the strong side: Refers to fixations made by the player with the ball 
on teammates who are one pass away and positioned around the perimeter (three-point line) 
or in mid-range shooting areas. 
5) Outside teammates on the weak side: Refers to fixations made by the player with the ball 
on teammates who are two or more passes away and located around the perimeter (around 
the three-point line) or in mid-range shooting areas. 
6) Inside teammates on the strong side: Refers to fixations made by the player with the ball on 
teammates who are in the low post area and are one pass away. 
7) Inside teammates on the weak side: These are fixations made by the offensive player with 
the ball on teammates positioned in the low post who are two or more passes away. 
8) Indirect outside defenders on the strong side: These are fixations made by the offensive 
player with the ball on defenders marking off-ball teammates who are one pass away and 
positioned around the perimeter (around the three-point line) or in mid-range shooting areas. 
9) Indirect outside defenders on the weak side: Refers to fixations made by the offensive player 
with the ball on defenders marking off-ball teammates who are two or more passes away 
and located around the perimeter (around the three-point line) or in mid-range shooting 
areas. 
10) Indirect inside defenders on the strong side: These are fixations made by the offensive 
player with the ball on defenders marking off-ball teammates who are in the low post and 
one pass away. 
11) Indirect inside defenders on the weak side: Refers to fixations made by the offensive player 
with the ball on defenders marking off-ball teammates who are two or more passes away in 
the low post. 
12) Head of the direct defender: Fixations made by the offensive player with the ball on the 
head of their direct defender.
