Abstract
Background
This study employs the expert-novice paradigm to examine the visual 
search characteristics of expert players through basketball-specific 
decision-making tasks. 
Methods
A total of 48 female college students (24 basketball players and 24 
regular college students) were selected as participants. First person video 
stimuli were used to explore the behavioral indicators and eye movement 
characteristics of professional basketball players in sports decision-making 
tasks using the eye tracker.
Results
(1) Compared with novice players, expert players demonstrated 
significantly shorter reaction times, higher decision accuracy, and greater 
decision-making confidence in the task. (2) Expert players exhibited shorter 
fixation durations and fewer fixation counts. There was a significant 
difference in fixation duration and frequency between novice and expert 
players in the relevant and irrelevant areas of interest, with the expert 
group showing longer fixation durations and higher fixation frequencies in 
the relevant areas of interest. The scanning trajectory of the expert group 
was simpler and more centralized.
Conclusion
Expert players demonstrate faster reactions and higher accuracy in 
sport-related decision-making tasks, exhibiting visual search advantages,

Introduction
In competitive sports, decision-making ability is a key determinant of 
performance in complex and dynamic game situations. In team sports such 
as basketball, successful performance relies not only on technical execution 
but also on the ability to perceive, interpret, and respond to rapidly 
changing environmental information in a timely and accurate manner 1. 
Accordingly, the role of perceptual–cognitive skills and their development in 
expert athletes has become a central research focus in sport science. 
Perceptual-cognitive skills in sport refer to athletes’ ability to efficiently 
detect, interpret, and integrate task-relevant environmental information to 
anticipate events and make accurate decisions under temporal and spatial 
constraints 2.
Recent studies consistently demonstrate that high-level athletes 
outperform novices in information processing efficiency, pattern recognition 
speed, 
and 
decision 
accuracy—abilities 
collectively 
referred 
to 
as 
perceptual–cognitive expertise 3. This expertise is not merely a function of 
faster motor responses, but rather reflects the efficient extraction and 
integration of task-relevant visual cues, supported by refined visual 
attention control, optimized working memory, and predictive internal 
models developed through long-term practice 4. Differences between expert 
and novice athletes are particularly evident in visual search behavior. Metaanalytic evidence indicates that expert athletes tend to use fewer but more 
informative fixations, achieving higher information uptake efficiency and 
more effective coordination between foveal and peripheral vision 
5. 
However, this pattern is not universally observed, as expertise-related 
fixation behavior appears to vary depending on task demands and 
contextual constraints 2.
Visual attention is fundamental to decision-making in open-skill sports 
such as basketball, where players must rapidly extract and integrate 
information from opponents, teammates, and spatial configurations under 
time pressure 6,7. Decision quality depends on underlying perceptual–
cognitive representations and the efficiency of visual search strategies 8,9. 
From a cognitive perspective, differences in attention allocation and 
information processing speed are key factors distinguishing expert from 
novice performance, particularly in dynamically changing and temporally 
constrained situations 10,11.
Within this framework, eye-tracking methodologies have been widely 
used to investigate visual search characteristics during sport decisionmaking. Eye-movement indicators (e.g., fixation duration and fixation count) 
provide insights into visual search strategies, whereas behavioral measures 
(e.g., reaction time and decision accuracy) reflect decision-making 
efficiency 12. Across various sports, expert athletes often demonstrate more

efficient visual search strategies than novices, reflected in fewer fixations13, 
more structured gaze patterns, faster responses, and higher decision 
accuracy. However, it is important to distinguish these overall search 
characteristics from event-specific gaze behavior. In many in-situ and 
mobile eye-tracking studies, experts exhibit longer fixation durations at 
critical task moments—commonly described as the “Quiet Eye”14—
indicating sustained attentional engagement with task-relevant information 
prior to action execution. These characteristics suggest a more efficient and 
goal-directed information sampling strategy.
Importantly, the ecological validity of perceptual–cognitive research 
depends heavily on the nature of the experimental stimuli. Although mobile 
eye-tracking studies conducted in naturalistic sport environments offer high 
ecological validity, they often face challenges related to experimental 
control and stimulus standardization. Consequently, many studies have 
relied on laboratory-based stimuli, frequently presented from a third-person 
perspective using static images or broadcast-style video footage 15,16. 
However, first-person visual perspectives more closely resemble the 
athlete’s perceptual experience during actual play and may better capture 
the information constraints relevant to in-game decision-making 17,18. 
Therefore, the aim of the present study was to examine the visual 
search characteristics and decision-making performance of expert and 
novice female basketball players using basketball-specific decision-making 
tasks presented via first-person video stimuli. Based on the dual processing 
theory, decision-making is divided into intuitive and cognitive processing, 
manifested as fast automatic and slow analysis, respectively. This study 
uses reaction time (≤ 600 ms and ≤ 1200 ms) as the basis for operational 
classification 2. Adopting a classic expert–novice paradigm, this study 
integrates behavioral and eye-movement measures to investigate how 
perceptual–cognitive expertise manifests in specialized basketball scenarios, 
thereby providing methodologically informed evidence for future sport 
decision-making research.

Apparatus
The experiment was conducted using the Eyelink eye-tracking data 
collection and analysis system developed by SR Research Ltd. in Canada 
(SR Research Ltd., 2023). A Lenovo laptop (model E15), running on 
Windows 10, was used as the experimental device. The laptop featured a 2.4 
GHz CPU, a 15.6 inch display with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels, and a 
refresh rate of 60 Hz. The seat height was adjusted to ensure that 
participants were seated comfortably in front of the monitor, with their eyes 
approximately 60 cm away from the screen. Eye movement data were 
collected using the Eyelink Portable Duo portable eye tracker, which had a 
sampling rate of 1000 Hz. The high sampling rate (1000 Hz) may have 
increased sensitivity to micro-variability in gaze signals despite standard 
filtering procedures. The experimental program ran at a resolution of 1024 
× 768 pixels.
Stimuli
A total of 129 half-court attack videos were recorded, covering three types 
of actions: passing, breakthrough, and shooting 22. The videos were 
recorded in high-definition with a resolution of 960 × 640 pixels.
Stimulus Material Production: The videos were filmed indoors under 
optimal lighting conditions, with no extraneous objects or individuals 
present to avoid interference. The attack scenarios selected for the 
experiment were derived from forward field attacks in the WCBA league, as 
determined through discussions with basketball coaches and players. Ten 
high-level female basketball players were recruited to recreate the game 
scenarios on-site. Players from the two teams were distinguished by

Procedure
The experiment was conducted in an eye-tracking laboratory with no noise 
interference and stable lighting conditions to ensure smooth completion of 
the experiment by participants. Only two individuals were present in the 
laboratory: the principal investigator and the participant, with the principal 
investigator accompanying the participant throughout the experimental 
process. Participant information collection (including demographic data, 
basketball training experience, and health status confirmation) was 
conducted prior to the experimental task. The experimental program was 
developed using Experiment Builder 2.3 software, and the stimulus 
materials were presented on the full screen (Fig. 1). Each video clip 
depicted an offensive basketball scenario that led to a single optimal 
decision outcome (pass, shoot, or breakthrough). After 3000 ms of video 
presentation, the clip was occluded, and participants were required to 
indicate the most appropriate decision by pressing one of three designated 
response keys corresponding to these options.Reaction time was defined as 
the interval between video occlusion and the participant’ s key press. Thus, 
participants were responding to the anticipated tactical outcome of the 
offensive situation shown in the video.
Decision type was operationally defined based on response latency 
thresholds, with responses ≤600 ms classified as intuitive and those ≤1200 
ms classified as cognitive 23. Thus, response time served as a predefined 
criterion for categorizing decision-making processes, rather than as a post 
hoc outcome variable. The presentation of stimulus materials and the 
participants’ response times were automatically recorded by the program 
with an accuracy of 1 ms. The experimental procedure consisted of a 
preparation stage and a formal experimental stage. The preparation stage 
included eye tracker calibration, experimental task instructions, participant 
information collection, and keyboard key practice.
After each experiment, the screen immediately displayed the ‘Decision 
Confidence Level Scale’, which used an 11-point Likert scale ranging from 0 
to 10, representing a continuum from no confidence to 100% confidence. 
Participants reported their confidence scores using a mouse, with higher 
scores indicating greater confidence in decision-making.

The study adopted a 2 (group: experts, novices) × 2 (decision type:

Eye-movement visualization and qualitative analysis
Visual indicators included gaze trajectory maps and fixation heat maps, 
which were used to qualitatively illustrate gaze behavior patterns. In 
fixation heat maps, areas with longer fixation durations were represented 
by warmer colors (e.g., red), whereas areas with shorter fixation durations 
were shown in cooler colors (e.g., green).
In the present study, heat maps were used as illustrative examples to 
demonstrate typical gaze distribution characteristics derived from the eyetracking data, rather than as primary outcome measures. Representative 
heat maps were selected to visualize general differences in visual attention 
allocation between expert and novice players. Specifically, expert players 
tended to concentrate their gaze on task-relevant areas of interest (AOIs), 
whereas novice players distributed their attention more broadly, including 
both relevant and irrelevant regions.
It should be noted that the presented heat maps were randomly selected for 
visualization purposes and do not constitute a basis for statistical inference.

Discussions
The present study employed eye tracking technology to investigate 
visual search characteristics and decision making performance in expert 
and novice basketball players under video based, sport specific scenarios. 
Overall, the findings consistently demonstrated that expert players 
exhibited faster reaction times, higher decision accuracy, and greater 
decision confidence than novices. At the level of visual behavior, experts 
showed shorter fixation durations, fewer fixations, and a more efficient 
allocation of gaze, collectively reflecting a more economical and goaldirected visual search strategy18. The theoretical contribution of this study 
lies in refining the understanding of perception–decision integration in sport 
expertise. 
The observed expert advantages can be interpreted within the 
framework of perceptual cognitive expertise. Rather than relying on faster 
basic physiological responses alone, expert players appear to benefit from 
extensive 
domain 
specific 
experience 
that 
supports 
rapid 
pattern 
recognition, probabilistic anticipation, and selective attention to task 
relevant information 26. Through long term training and competition, 
experts develop structured “knowledge representations” that allow them to 
extract high value information from complex visual scenes while minimizing 
unnecessary information sampling. This mechanism likely underlies the 
reduced number of fixations and shorter fixation durations observed in the 
expert group, consistent with previous findings in sport related visual 
search research 9,15. The shorter overall fixation durations observed in the 
present video-based paradigm may reflect efficient information extraction 
rather than reduced attentional engagement. Unlike mobile eye-tracking 
studies examining action-coupled performance and Quiet Eye phenomena, 
our task required perceptual judgments without motor execution, which 
may promote faster visual sampling strategies in expert players.
In addition, fixation duration and fixation frequency differed between 
intuitive and cognitive decision making conditions. Longer fixations during 
cognitive decision making likely reflect increased demands on information 
integration, 
motor 
planning, 
and 
response 
programming, 
including 
direction, force, timing, and limb coordination 27. Experts appear better 
able to integrate limited visual cues from the video stimuli with prior

Importantly, the present findings should be interpreted within the 
constraints of the experimental paradigm. All tasks were performed using 
video-based stimuli, which allow for high experimental control and 
systematic manipulation of perceptual information, but do not fully capture 
the dynamic perception action coupling present in real game situations. 
Therefore, the observed expertise advantages should be understood as 
reflecting perceptual cognitive tendencies under controlled, representative 
task conditions, rather than as direct indicators of in-game decision-making 
performance.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although video-based 
paradigms allow for precise experimental control and isolation of 
perceptual–attentional processes, their ecological validity remains limited. 
In real competitive settings, decision making is additionally shaped by 
motor execution demands, dynamic interpersonal interactions, time 
pressure, and emotional influences, which may alter how visual search 
strategies are deployed on the court. Second, the video scenarios focused 
exclusively on offensive situations (passing, shooting, and breakthroughs), 
without incorporating defensive behaviors such as blocking or stealing. 
Third, the present study examined behavioral and eye-movement measures 
only, without directly assessing the underlying neural mechanisms.

Conclusions
(1) There are significant differences in decision-making performance and 
visual search behavior between basketball expert and novice. Expert 
players exhibit faster reaction times, higher decision-making accuracy, and 
greater confidence than novice.
(2) In terms of visual behavior, expert players showed shorter gaze 
duration and lower number of fixations, indicating that visual search 
strategies are more effective. Although there was no significant difference 
in overall fixation duration and fixation frequency between groups of 
different decision types, experts allocated a larger proportion of fixation to 
relevant areas of interest.
