Mental fatigue impairs soccer-specific decision-making skill
Mitchell R. Smitha,b, Linus Zeuwts
b, Matthieu Lenoirb, Nathalie Hensb, Laura M. S. De Jongc and Aaron J. Couttsa
aSport and Exercise Discipline Group, Faculty of Health, University of Technology Sydney (UTS) Sydney Cricket Ground, Moore Park, New South

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the impact of mental fatigue on soccer-specific decision-making. Twelve
well-trained male soccer players performed a soccer-specific decision-making task on two occasions,
separated by at least 72 h. The decision-making task was preceded in a randomised order by 30 min of
the Stroop task (mental fatigue) or 30 min of reading from magazines (control). Subjective ratings of
mental fatigue were measured before and after treatment, and mental effort (referring to treatment)
and motivation (referring to the decision-making task) were measured after treatment. Performance on
the soccer-specific decision-making task was assessed using response accuracy and time. Visual search
behaviour was also assessed throughout the decision-making task. Subjective ratings of mental fatigue
and effort were almost certainly higher following the Stroop task compared to the magazines.
Motivation for the upcoming decision-making task was possibly higher following the Stroop task.
Decision-making accuracy was very likely lower and response time likely higher in the mental fatigue
condition. Mental fatigue had unclear effects on most visual search behaviour variables. The results
suggest that mental fatigue impairs accuracy and speed of soccer-specific decision-making. These
impairments are not likely related to changes in visual search behaviour.

Introduction
Soccer match play demands frequent bouts of high-intensity
activity, intermittently distributed among prolonged periods
of low-intensity activity (Mohr, Krustrup, & Bangsbo, 2005).
This physically fatiguing activity profile contributes to reductions in performance observed towards the end of a match
(Rampinini, Coutts, Castagna, Sassi, & Impellizzeri, 2007;
Rampinini et al., 2008) and in subsequent matches (Mohr
et al., 2005; Nédélec et al., 2012). Soccer is also cognitively
demanding as it requires sustained attention to make quick,
accurate decisions based on the retrieval and processing of
information from a dynamic environment (Nédélec et al., 2012;
Walsh, 2014). These demands suggest that soccer may also be
mentally fatiguing, with reductions in performance resulting
from a combination of physical and mental fatigue. Indeed,
recent research has shown that mental fatigue impairs soccerspecific physical and technical performance (Smith et al., 2016;
Smith, Marcora, & Coutts, 2015).
Mental fatigue is a psychobiological state induced by sustained
periods of demanding cognitive activity and characterised by
feelings of tiredness and lack of energy (Boksem & Tops, 2008;
Marcora, Staiano, & Manning, 2009). The negative impact of mental fatigue on cognitive (Boksem, Meijman, & Lorist, 2005, 2006)
and motor (Duncan, Fowler, George, Joyce, & Hankey, 2015; Lal &
Craig, 2001) performances is well established, and may be attributable to an impaired capability to maintain attentional focus
(Boksem et al., 2005), monitor and adjust performance (Lorist,
Boksem, & Ridderinkhof, 2005), react quickly and accurately
(Boksem et al., 2006), and identify and respond to meaningful
cues in the visual search field of use for action preparation
(Boksem et al., 2006; Lorist et al., 2000). Therefore, mental fatigue
may also impair soccer-specific decision-making, which relies on
these cognitive abilities (Afonso, Garganta, & Mesquita, 2012).
Previous research has shown that experienced soccer
players make faster, more accurate decisions than their lessexperienced counterparts (Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, Mazyn, &
Philippaerts, 2007). These discrepancies are partly determined
by differences in visual search behaviour between players of
different experience levels (Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, Mazyn,
et al., 2007; Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, & Philippaerts, 2007).
Therefore, it is important to assess the impact of mental
fatigue on decision-making skill and visual search behaviour
as these factors appear to be important contributors to successful soccer performance.
To date however, no research has examined the effects of
mental fatigue on soccer-specific decision-making. Previously,
Laborde and Raab (2013) revealed that both positive and
negative mood states impaired team handball players’ decision-making skill compared to a neutral mood control group.
Additionally, Hockey, John Maule, Clough, and Bdzola (2000)
revealed a fatigue-related increase in riskiness of decisions in
hypothetical, everyday situations. Together, these investigations indicate that mood can affect executive functioning,
justifying further research into the impact of psychological
manipulations on sport-specific decision-making.
The purpose of this investigation was to examine the effects
of induced mental fatigue on soccer-specific decision-making

Methods
Participants
Twelve male soccer players (age: 19.3 ± 1.5 y; position: striker = 1, central midfield = 8, central defence = 3) participated
in this randomised, counter-balanced crossover investigation.
All players were currently competing in Belgian national (divisions 3–4) or provincial (divisions 2–4) competitions and had
been playing soccer for at least 10 years (13.1 ± 2.6 y).
Participants signed an informed consent form outlining potential risks and describing the studies procedures which were
approved by the Ghent University Hospital Ethics Committee
(Project #: 2002052) and conformed to the Declaration of
Helsinki. Participants were provided with information outlining
the study’s procedures but were not informed of its aims.
Experimental overview
Participants completed one familiarisation, plus two testing
sessions (control and mental fatigue). The familiarisation session involved an explanation of all testing procedures and
equipment. Participants were also provided instructions to
sleep for eight hours, refrain from consumption of alcohol
and avoid vigorous exercise in the 24 h before each of the
testing sessions; avoid caffeine and demanding mental activity
the day of testing; and consume a reasonable meal approximately 1.5 h before the upcoming testing sessions which were
separated by at least 72 h.
Upon arrival for testing, participants completed a pre-test
checklist, assessing compliance with instructions, as well as a
visual analogue scale (VAS) for the assessment of mental
fatigue. A standardised explanation of the decision-making
task was then displayed on a projector screen to reinforce
familiarity for the upcoming test. Participants then walked to
an adjacent room where the control or mental fatigue treatment was administered. Immediately following treatment, participants returned to the testing room where they completed
VAS for subjective assessment of mental fatigue, mental effort
and motivation. Participants were then fitted with a modified
baseball cap housing the eye-tracking system.
Following calibration, participants completed four practice
trials of the soccer-specific decision-making task, followed by
33 testing trials, with an inter-trial interval of approximately
45 s. The calibration of the eye-tracking system was checked
following the four practice trials and after every 11 test trials.
Participants again provided subjective ratings of mental fatigue and mental effort following the decision-making task,
which lasted approximately 35–40 min.
Treatment
Mental fatigue was induced using a 30-min paper version of a
modified Stroop colour–word task. Four words (red, blue, green,
yellow) were repeatedly printed in a random order, and
participants were required to verbally respond to each word.
The correct response corresponded to the ink colour of the word
(red, blue, green, yellow), rather than the words meaning.
However, if the ink colour of the word was red, the correct
response corresponded to the meaning of the word, rather
than its printed colour. Verbal responses were monitored by a
member of the research team who sat opposite the participants
throughout treatment. To increase motivation for the task, a
competitive environment was created, whereby participants
were challenged to successfully complete more words than
other participants within the 30-min treatment period.
The control treatment involved 30 min of reading from a
selection of magazines. The magazines varied in theme, including sport, cars and travel, and were identified by pilot testing as
emotionally neutral. Participants completed both treatments in
the same room, under the supervision of the same researcher.
Subjective ratings
Subjective ratings of mental fatigue, mental effort and motivation were recorded using the same 100-mm VAS as used in
previous research investigating the impact of mental fatigue
on soccer-specific performance (Smith et al., 2016). The scales
were anchored at one end with “none at all” and at the other
end with “maximal”. Mental fatigue was measured pre- and
post-treatment as a manipulation check, and also after the
decision-making task. Mental effort was assessed following
treatment and the decision-making task, while motivation
was only measured post-treatment.
Decision-making task
This study utilised the soccer-specific decision-making task previously developed and validated by Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams,
Mazyn, et al. (2007). This task required participants to observe
film-based simulations of offensive soccer play, projected onto a
4.3 × 2.5-m screen positioned 4.4 m in front of the participant.
Participants were required to imagine themselves as an offensive
midfielder, playing in a central position, who were easily identifiable on screen, as they wore a yellow vest over their playing
jersey. Sequences of play varied in number, and position of
players on screen (2 vs. 1, 3 vs. 1, 3 vs. 2, 4 vs. 3 and 5 vs. 3), as
well as in duration, ranging from 3.0 s to 9.7 s. Each sequence
ended with a pass to the yellow player and film was occluded as
the yellow player received the ball.
As the ball was passed to the yellow player on screen,
participants were required to make quick and accurate decisions
by taking the appropriate action with the ball positioned in front
of them. In each sequence, participants had the option of passing to any player on screen, shooting towards goal or dribbling
the ball “around a defender”. Participants were then required to
verbalise their response to ensure that they had carried out the
intended action. Task performance was assessed using response
accuracy, response time and visual search data.

Visual search
Visual search data were recorded using an IviewX Head
mounted Eye-tracking Device (SMI, Teltow, GER). Participants
wore a modified baseball cap, housing an infra-red eye camera, which recorded left eye movements at 50 Hz using pupil
position and corneal reflex. A second camera recorded images
of the scene at 25 Hz. Both cameras were connected via USB
cable to a notebook which was placed in a small backpack
worn by the participants. Data from both cameras were combined using BeGaze 3.0 software (SMI, Teltow, GER), which has
a spatial accuracy of 1°. Visual search data was analysed frame
by frame using video analysis software (Kinovea open source
project, www.kinovea.org). Technical and calibration issues
resulted in poor tracking of gaze behaviour during some testing sessions. Therefore, visual search data are presented for
eight participants.
Search rate was assessed using the mean number of visual
fixations per second (n · s−1), and mean fixation duration. A
fixation was defined as a period where the eye remained on
one area of interest for equal to or greater than 120 ms. The
percentage of total viewing time (per trial) spent in fixation
(fixation %) was also calculated, and further broken down into
measurement of fixation % for different locations. Fixations
were allocated to one of nine areas: yellow player (YP); player
with ball (PB); ball (B); unmarked attacker (A); closely marked
attacker (A/D); defender (D); goalkeeper (K); free space (FS)
and an unspecified category for fixations that could not be
categorised under the aforementioned locations (U).
Fixation order refers to the sequence of searching utilised
by the participants. As in previous investigations (Vaeyens,
Lenoir,
Williams,

Burwitz, & Williams, 1994), fixation order was measured as
the number of times per second (n · s−1) that participants
alternated their gaze between the player with the ball,
another area of the display and then back to the player with
the ball.
Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise stated.
Prior to analysis, data were tested for normality and logtransformed when non-normally distributed. A contemporary
magnitude-based approach was used to assess chances of
differences between experimental conditions being trivial or
true (greater than the smallest worthwhile change; 0.2 × the
between-subjects SD) (Hopkins, Marshall, Batterham, & Hanin,
2009). Chances of true difference were qualitatively assessed
as <0.5%, almost certainly not; <5%, very unlikely; <25%,
unlikely; 25‒75%, possibly; >75%, likely; >95%, very likely
and >99.5%, almost certainly. If the chances of a variable
having higher and lower differences were both >5%, the effect
was considered unclear. Effect sizes (ES) were calculated to
assess the magnitude of difference between groups and are
reported ± 90% confidence limits. ES of <0.2, 0.2–0.6, 0.6–1.2,
1.2–2.0 and >2.0 were considered trivial, small, moderate,
large and very large, respectively. If the 90% confidence interval of the ES was greater than ± 0.2 in both directions, the
effect was considered unclear.

Discussion
The aim of the current investigation was to determine the
impact of mental fatigue on soccer-specific decision-making,
and gaze behaviour. In agreement with our primary hypothesis, mental fatigue impaired both speed and accuracy of
soccer-specific decision-making. However, only minor differences in gaze behaviour were observed between conditions.

(such as the Stroop task) produce lower motivation for
“have-to” tasks, and higher motivation for “want-to” tasks.
This may have resulted in reduced motivation to perform
the Stroop task, but a possibly higher motivation to perform
the soccer-specific decision-making task. Nevertheless, motivational differences cannot explain any performance differences in the current investigation as participants were highly
motivated to perform the soccer-specific decision-making
task in both conditions.
The primary finding of the current investigation was that
mental fatigue impaired accuracy and speed of soccer-specific
decision-making. This impaired decision-making skill in mentally fatigued athletes is not surprising, as previous investigations have revealed the negative impact of mental fatigue on
reaction time and accuracy in cognitive tasks (Boksem et al.,
2005; Lorist et al., 2005), and the negative impact of mood
manipulation on decision-making skill in team handball
players (Laborde & Raab, 2013).
One potential explanation for the mental-fatigue-induced
impairment in decision-making skill is that players may have
been unable to effectively utilise environmental cues to prepare for their upcoming decisions. Indeed, previous investigations using computer-based cognitive tasks have revealed that
mental fatigue negatively impacts preparation and planning
processes (Boksem et al., 2006; Lorist et al., 2000). In a soccerspecific context, this may manifest as a reduced capability to
interpret and/or anticipate the movement of players and the
ball. Future research utilising electroencephalography may
provide further insight on this hypothesis, as specific changes
in brain activity are associated with preparatory and anticipatory processes (Boksem et al., 2006).
Decision-making ability of team sport athletes is affected
by preferences for intuition and deliberation. Raab and
Laborde (2011) found that team handball players who preferred intuitive decision-making made faster and better decisions than those who preferred deliberative decision-making.
Indeed, when athletes are under time pressure to choose from
multiple plausible options, taking the first option often results
in the fastest and most accurate decision (Laborde & Raab,
2013; Raab & Laborde, 2011). Although decision-making preferences were not measured in the current investigation, it is
possible that mentally fatigued players were more deliberative
in their decision-making, leading to slower and less accurate
responses.
In contrast to our hypothesis, mental fatigue had minimal
impact on visual search behaviour during the soccer-specific
decision-making task. Between-condition differences in search
rate were observed only in 2 vs. 1 formations and percentage
of time fixating was different only in 3 vs. 2 formations.
Furthermore, while overall fixation order was possibly lower
in the mental fatigue condition, the size of the effect was
unclear, and no true between-condition differences existed
for specific formations. However, when mentally fatigued,
players appeared to spend a higher percentage of time fixating on defensive players and a lower percentage fixating on
offensive players (Figure 3A).
Previous research (Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, & Philippaerts,
2007) identified similar results, with successful decisionmakers fixating more on the player with the ball and attacking
players, and less time fixating on the ball and defensive
players. In contrast to these previous results, in the current
investigation there were very few between-condition differences for search rate, fixation percentage and fixation order.
This may be due to the limited sample size of the current
investigation. Nevertheless, it is difficult to determine whether
these small changes in visual search behaviour were responsible for the observed decline in decision-making performance
in mentally fatigued players.
In soccer, successful decision-making also relies on a
player’s capability to identify, select and then programme
the correct action in response to opposition or teammate
postural cues, recognise meaningful patterns in play and
determine situational probabilities (Roca, Ford, McRobert, &
Williams, 2013; Williams, Ford, Eccles, & Ward, 2011). In the
current investigation, it is possible that despite minimal differences in visual search behaviour, players’ information processing was less effective, resulting in impaired decision-making
skill. Indeed, mental fatigue has been shown to impair executive functioning and information-processing during cognitive
tasks (Meijman, 1997; Van der Linden, Frese, & Meijman, 2003),
which may partly be due to the effects of mental fatigue on
attention.
Mental fatigue has been associated with changes in attentional focus from goal-directed to stimulus-driven attention
(Ackerman, 2011; Boksem et al., 2005), which could lead to
inattentional blindness, whereby observers fail to notice
important cues such as unmarked teammates (Memmert &
Furley, 2007). Therefore, fatigue-related impairments to decision-making in the current investigation may partly be
explained by ineffective information-processing and changes
in attentional focus, rather than altered visual search patterns.
Indeed, mentally fatigued participants in a previous examination did not report difficulties with visual perception, but did
report more attentional difficulties (Van der Linden & Eling,
2006). As previously suggested (Smith et al., 2016), these
potential changes in attentional focus may be more significant
in ecological settings such as full-sized or small-sided games,
where the number of irrelevant stimuli is greater than that of a
controlled setting.

While the current investigation clearly reveals the negative
impact of mental fatigue on accuracy and speed of soccerspecific decision-making, some limitations should be acknowledged. Due to technical difficulties, visual search behaviour
was only assessed in eight participants. It is possible that
additional data may have provided a clearer assessment of
visual search patterns and potential mechanisms underlying
performance changes. However, this limitation does not
negate the substantial impairments to soccer-specific decision-making induced by mental fatigue. Furthermore, future
research should take a more practical approach to assessing
the impact of mental fatigue on soccer performance. Indeed,
although anecdotal reports exist, it is unknown whether
match play or common pre-match activities induce mental
fatigue. If so, players and staff should reassess pre-match
routines and implement strategies that may attenuate the
negative impact of mental fatigue on soccer performance.
Conclusions
This investigation demonstrates that mental fatigue impairs
both accuracy and speed of soccer-specific decision-making
skill. It is unlikely that these impairments are related to
changes in visual search behaviour. Alternatively, it is possible
that mental fatigue impaired players’ capability to utilise environmental cues and/or resulted in changes to attention and
decision-making strategies. Nevertheless, the results of the
current investigation have significant implications for soccer,
where the capability to make quick and accurate decisions is
critical for success.
