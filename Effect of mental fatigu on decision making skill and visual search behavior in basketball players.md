ABSTRACT
The objective was to analyze the eﬀect of mental fatigue induced
by a prolonged period of a sport-based videogame on decisionmaking skill and visual search behaviour in basketball players.
Sixteen male basketball players participated in the study. The
experiment was a randomised counterbalanced crossover design.
They underwent the two experimental conditions [Control (CON)
and videogame (VID)], interspersed by a one-week washout
interval. We measured decision-making skill and visual search
behaviour before (baseline) and after cognitive manipulations
(VID and CON) in the two experimental sessions. Accuracy
decreased, and the decision-making response time increased only
in the VID condition (p < 0.05). The number of ﬁxations only
changed for the VID condition (p < 0.05), while the duration of
ﬁxations remained similar in both experimental conditions (p >
0.05). The mental fatigue induced by sport-based videogame
impaired decision-making and altered visual search behaviour in
basketball players.

Introduction
In team sports that require territory invasion to score points (also called “invasion sports”),
the accuracy and consistency of movements are crucial, as well as eﬃciently processing
the surrounding information to adopt the correct action in a given situation (Kalén
et al., 2022; Page et al., 2019). To select the most appropriate action, players need to
track multiple moving objects in a short time (Alder et al., 2021). For example, basketball
is a fast-paced team sport that requires attention to the position and movement of teammates and opponents, the ball’s spatial position, and the court simultaneously (Page et al.,
2019). A combination of general visual skills (e.g., visual search behaviour) and performance-relevant perceptual-cognitive skills (e.g., decision-making) are essential to elite athletes’ performance in basketball, mainly because this it requires attention in diﬀerent
visual key areas related to decision-making with high speed, accuracy, and complexity
(Faubert & Sidebottom, 2012; Hadlow et al., 2018; Kalén et al., 2022). Previous ﬁndings

Decision-making is deﬁned as the process of choosing one alternative from a range of
diﬀerent alternatives (Bar-Eli et al., 2011). Otherwise, eﬀective decision-making is choosing
the best alternative, which might be the critical point between success and failure. There are
several perspectives and theoretical frameworks about sports decision-making [e.g., processing information (cognitive knowledge), ecologic dynamic (perception-action coupling),
and naturalistic] (Ashford et al., 2021), which present inherently diﬀerent points of view in
human behaviour (Bar-Eli et al., 2011). Expert players improve their capability to extract and
process cues from the environment, recognising and interpreting familiar playing patterns
and forming expectations by computing situational probability (Ashford et al., 2021). These
selection processes are an intermediate agent between what is perceived and which action
is chosen by the player. In that sense, the present study is in accordance with the processing
information approach. The processing information perspective deﬁnes decision-making as
a structured and higher cognitive process (Cotterill & Discombe, 2016).
In the specialised literature, it is well established that elite decision-makers, while often
indistinguishable from other performers on physical attributes, possess superior perceptual-cognitive skills than their near-elite and novice counterparts (Vaeyens et al., 2007).
Indeed, these athletes have better recognition and anticipation patterns and diﬀerent
visual search strategies (Klostermann et al., 2020; Vaeyens et al., 2007), underpinning
their superior decision-making abilities. Team sport elite decision-makers tend to demonstrate visual search patterns with an increased number of short-time ﬁxations during
sports scenes with several relevant visual stimuli (Vaeyens et al., 2007). The visual
search pattern seems to change for quite-eye behaviour (i.e., lower number of ﬁxations
of long-time) in sport scenes with less relevant stimuli (Smith et al., 2016). Thus, Scientists
seem to consider visual search behaviour as the main mechanism for decision-making skill
in team sports (Bar-Eli et al., 2011; Klostermann et al., 2020; Vaeyens et al., 2007).
Speciﬁc regions of the brain play a leading role in the decision-making process. Previous ﬁndings revealed brain activation during decision-making tasks, mainly in the left
frontoparietal region (Fink et al., 2019). Overall, these brain region networks support
higher-order executive functions such as visual search, which is the processing and integration of sensorimotor and somatosensory information (Fink et al., 2019). The primary
visual cortex has ramiﬁcations with the middle-temporal area, which presents strong sensitivity to object movement, especially direction and speed (Hülsdünker et al., 2018).
Otherwise, the posterior parietal cortex presents an interface between the primary
visual cortex and the pre-supplemental motor area, besides connecting with the frontal
lobe, exerting an important function on visuomotor transformation (Hülsdünker et al.,
2018). In its turn, the pre-supplementary motor area plans motor actions based on perceived and processed information by the primary visual cortex (Hülsdünker et al.,
2018). Thus, activities that negatively aﬀect the visuomotor system and its neural connections, such as mental fatigue, should be investigated since it might impair basketball
players’ visual search behaviour and decision-making.

Bar-Eli (1997) suggested a model of psychological performance crisis in competition
(i.e., crisis perspective) that indicates athletes might reach, under extreme arousal
levels, a state in which the ability to cope adequately with competitive requirements
deteriorates, aﬀecting their performance (e.g., decision-making). Previous ﬁndings with
amateur basketball players revealed poor decision-making behaviour for game situations
with high levels of criticality (Bar-Eli & Tractinsky, 2000). Another study reported that basketball players made worse decisions in situations categorised as high complexity than
low complexity and for tasks categorised as high pressure compared to low pressure
(Kinrade et al., 2015). Similarly, Hepler and Kovacs (2017) found that mental stress may
impair information processing speed for decision-making in recreational basketball
players. Hepler (2015) also found that mental stress slows down decision speed. In
turn, a slow decision speed will aﬀect the quality of decisions in dynamic, time-pressured
situations. Making a low-quality decision or hesitating for a fraction of a second could
mean the diﬀerence between success and failure for team sport athletes. It was argued
that the extent to which vulnerability to crisis perspective (Bar-Eli, 1997) depends on
experience is related to the quality and quantity of cognitive mechanisms available to
cope with potential mental stress, for example, mental fatigue.
Studies have shown that prolonged periods of demanding cognitive activity induce
mental fatigue (Martin et al., 2018; Van-Cutsem et al., 2017), characterised as a psychobiological state of feelings of tiredness and lack of energy (Marcora et al., 2009). Possible
mechanisms underlying mental fatigue eﬀects are linked to energetic resource depletion
(e.g., brain phosphocreatine) (Martin et al., 2018) and complex neural mechanisms (FrancoAlvarenga et al., 2019; Van-Cutsem et al., 2017), that is, frontal theta-wave activity increased
(i.e., impaired frontal brain function) (Pires et al., 2018; Wascher et al., 2014). A recent metaanalysis found a frontal midline theta-wave activity increased in healthy mentally fatigued
subjects (Tran et al., 2020). Although the consequences of mental fatigue have been
assessed in multiple research ﬁelds (Martin et al., 2018; McMorris, 2020; Tran et al.,
2020), the exact mechanisms leading to the occurrence of mental fatigue remain
unknown. However, some theoretical frameworks were created to explain mental
fatigue. A reward and energetical costs framework for mental fatigue was proposed
(Boksem & Tops, 2008), which involves an integrated evaluation of both expected
rewards and energetical costs associated with continued performance. The authors proposed that the feeling of mental fatigue corresponds to a drive to disengage behaviour
or impair attention resources when energetical costs continue to exceed perceived
rewards of task performance. More recently, the brain interoceptive role framework was
proposed (McMorris, 2020), which indicates that mental fatigue leads to diﬀerent predictions of the expected sensory consequences of undertaking the physical or cognitive task
than in the control condition. According to the interoceptive role framework (McMorris,
2020), the person is also likely to perceive themselves as having fewer attention resources
available than in the control situation due to the perceived cognitive fatigue, thus aﬀecting
the decisions made by the interaction between some brain areas (e.g., anterior insula
cortex, anterior cingulated cortex, and ventromedial prefrontal cortex).
Regarding the mental fatigue, it has been reported that video of tactical instructions
(Filipas et al., 2021), playing sport-based videogames (Fortes et al., 2020), and using
social media on smartphones (Fortes et al., 2019) for a prolonged period (e.g., at least
20-min) might cause mental fatigue in athletes. Mentally fatigued athletes showed

impaired decision-making (Gantois et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2016; Trecroci et al., 2020) and
altered visual search behaviour (Alder et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2016). However, both
studies induced mental fatigue using cognitive tasks with low ecological validity (i.e.,
Stroop task) and real-world strategies should be tested.
In a real-world context, Thompson et al. (2020) showed that ∼10% of English academy
players reported playing videogames as a pre-match activity. Balk and Englert (2020) also
outline that playing video games puts additional strain on athletes instead of relaxing
them. It is common for team sports athletes to use sports-based videogames as a prematch activity. The sport-based videogames present demand with a high amount of
visual search in a short time. On the one hand, the sport-based video games may be
used as a cognitive warm-up for subsequent match play because they are characterised
by constantly changing scenery (Oei & Patterson, 2014), which requires greater sustained
attention, cognitive inhibition, and cognitive ﬂexibility.
On the other hand, if sport-based videogame is used for prolonged periods (e.g., >30min), they could induce mental fatigue and impair perceptual-cognitive skills in team
sport players. Sheikholeslami et al. (2007) found that the frontal midline theta-wave
activity increased (i.e., deactivation of frontal brain area) over time during videogame
play in healthy humans. Therefore, it could be speculated that sport-based videogames
for prolonged periods before a training session or a match would cause mental fatigue,
impair decision-making, and change visual search behaviour in basketball players. For
instance, Fortes et al. (2020) observed that 30-min playing sport-based video games
caused mental fatigue and impaired passing decision-making in professional soccer
players. However, this study presents some limitations, such as the lack of brain electrophysiological measures (e.g., electroencephalogram) as a mental fatigue marker and eyetracker during decision-making tasks to assess visual search behaviour.
Here we aimed to analyze mental fatigue eﬀects induced by a prolonged period of
sport-based videogames on decision-making skill and visual search behaviour in basketball players. From a practical standpoint, the present investigation might indicate whether
decision-making and visual search behaviour are impaired when basketball players are
exposed to mental fatigue induced by sport-based videogames. The present study was
designed to provide important insight into the underlying mechanisms of mental
fatigue caused by a prolonged sport-based video game and the eﬀects on subsequent
decision-making in team sport athletes. Considering the recent scientiﬁc literature
(Fortes et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2016), the present investigation hypothesised that
mental fatigue induced by sport-based videogames impairs decision-making (i.e.,
impaired accuracy and response time) and changes visual search behaviour (i.e., lower
number of ﬁxations and higher duration of ﬁxations) in basketball players.

Experimental design
A randomised, counterbalanced, and experimental crossover design (i.e., two withinfactors) was adopted. The present study was conducted in a within-factors design (two
experimental conditions × two times). We tested basketball players for diﬀerent experimental conditions (i.e., videogame and control trials) and times (i.e., baseline and postexperiment). Participants visited the laboratory on four separate occasions. The participants underwent the two baseline visits (i.e., familiarisation and reliability for measures)
and then participated in two experimental conditions, separated by a one-week
washout interval to guarantee full recovery.
During the two baseline visits, the participants were familiarised with the mental
fatigue measures (i.e., subjective, behavioural, and physiological). We also collected
data for decision-making (i.e., accuracy and response time) and visual search behaviour
during the decision-making task to analyze measurements’ reliability. Then, we randomise
the order of the two experimental conditions and participants using a random number
table generator (www.randomizer.org). The two conditions comprise a decision-making
task using eye-tracker (visual search behaviour) with and without mental fatigue inducement (i.e., sports genre video games or control). A one-week washout period interspersed
the conditions (see Figure 1). It is important highlight that there was no found an experimental order eﬀect [F(1, 15) = 0.96; p = 0.74 (CI95% = 0.61 to 0.85); ηp2 = 0.002 (CI95% = 0.001
to 0.01)]. As noted above, the participants maintained their training routines during the
washout period. The participants refrained from any physical exercise and alcohol ingestion 48-h before experimental sessions, as well as from caﬀeine for at least four hours
before beginning the sessions.
The videogame (VID) and control (CON) trials were designed similarly (Figure 1). Trials
lasted about 1.5 h, and before trials, the participants completed the pretrial checklist. We
used the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Stroop task, and an electroencephalogram (EEG) to
assess mental fatigue in experimental sessions. Assessments occurred immediately before
and after sport-based video games (VID) or watching a documentary TV show about the

Mental fatigue protocols
Participants watched 60 min of coaching videos about the Olympic Games on a 48-inch
screen in the CON condition. The videos were watched in pairs (i.e., same pair for VID
experimental condition). Studies related to mental fatigue and human performance
have long used these emotionally neutral documentaries in control conditions
(Marcora et al., 2009; Moreira et al., 2018) because neither cognitive performance
(Fortes et al., 2019; Lopes et al., 2020) or underlying brain mechanisms of mental
fatigue were altered (Franco-Alvarenga et al., 2019).
The video game protocol (NBA live 19, EA Sports, EUA) lasted 60-min. Each video game
console (Playstation 4, Sony, Japan) was attached to a 48-inch TV. The player’s pairs were
selected on both baseline visits considering each athlete’s performance level at the NBA
live 19.
Manipulations checks
We used subjective (e.g., VAS), behavioural (e.g., Stroop task), and physiological (e.g., EEG)

Visual analog scale (VAS)
The subjective rating of mental fatigue was assessed using the 100 mm VAS previously
adopted (Smith et al., 2019). This scale has two extremities anchored from 0 (none at
all) to 100 (maximal). No other descriptor was presented in the VAS. The participants
were required to answer, “How mentally fatigued you feel now?”. The deﬁnition of MF
(e.g., a psychobiological state caused by prolonged periods of demanding cognitive
activity) was provided to participants and examples of “none at all” (no feelings of tiredness and lack of energy) and “maximal” (maximum feelings of tiredness and lack of

quantify values, we measured distance in millimetres from 0 to 100, indicated by the
participant.
As a manipulation check, a subjective rating of motivation was recorded using a 100mm VAS anchored by the words “not at all” and “maximal”. This scale has been previously
used in mental fatigue studies (Pires et al., 2018; Smith et al., 2019). Rating of motivation
was measured at pre-treatment and post-treatment.
Stroop task
The computerised version of the Stroop task (Graf et al., 1995) assessed inhibitory control
and selective attention before and after cognitive manipulation (i.e., videogame and
documentary). Since a decrement in the response time to the Stroop task is indicative
of mental fatigue (Gantois et al., 2020 ; Van-Cutsem et al., 2017), an incongruent stimulus
(pre- and post-cognitive manipulation) of 50 trials with 500 ms of the interval between
response and a new stimulus was used. The stimulus did not fade from the screen until
any response was given. In this task, four words (blue, yellow, red, and green) were presented in Arial font 60, once at a time, in random order, at the centre of a computer screen
(15 in.). The words were inked with blue, yellow, red, or green in an incongruent manner
(e.g., word blue painted with red ink). Subjects were instructed to press a coloured button
on the computer keyboard corresponding to the correct response as quickly and accurately as possible. The mean values of accuracy and response time were recorded for
analysis. The tests were carried out on a full-HD screen (1800 × 1260 pixels) laptop
(MacBook Pro, A1502 model, USA). The intraclass coeﬃcient correlation (ICC) was used
to determine the reliability of Stroop task accuracy in baseline visits (ICC = 0.99, CI95% =
0.97 to 0.99). The same test also was utilised for to determine the reliability of Stroop
task response time in baseline visits (ICC = 0.98, CI95% = 0.96 to 0.99).

Measures
Decision-making test development
Videos for the decision-making task were custom-made for this study. Previous studies
adopted a similar approach to evaluate decision-making in basketball players (Furley
et al., 2013). Before commencement, ten professional basketball players of nationallevel were asked to record video sequences of ﬁve-on-ﬁve situations with a helmet
camera (GoPro HD Hero 3; GoPro Inc., San Mateo, CA, USA) from the ﬁrst-person perspective. The videos were recorded at a resolution of 1280 × 960 pixels at 48 frames per second
and an angle of 170°. The horizontal and vertical image area captured the teammates,
opponents, and the ball. So, each scene of the decision-making task from a ﬁrst-person
perspective showed nine actors (four of them playing the role of “teammates” and ﬁve
acting as “opponents”) performing pre-determined variations of distinct oﬀensive or
defensive patterns of basketball. Immediately before a critical moment in the match
(e.g., a shoot), the video faded. At this point, the participants had to answer what
action they would perform in that situation. Participants would choose the oﬀensive
(dribble, pass, or shoot) and defensive (block, steal, or move) actions for each scenario.
A panel of three qualiﬁed basketball coaches watched the whole video to determine
whether the athlete’s action provided a realistic basketball pattern. The three coaches
who assessed the videos were individually presented with sketches of the pre-determined
actions and asked to rank the three options. Only videos with 100% agreement were part
of the study. The videos averaged 9.4 s (SD = 1.2), and a total of 100 diﬀerent videos were
recorded. Twenty videos were used in the study (the videos were randomly chosen),
being ten simulating oﬀensive and ten defensive actions.
Decision-making task
The videos began with a black screen during 3,000 ms, containing a white text with a trial
number. After that, another black screen showed a white text of a “3, 2, 1” countdown that
lasted 3,000 ms. The same twenty videos (ten simulating oﬀensive and ten defensive
actions) were used for all participants. The order of presentation remained identical for
all the participants at baseline and post-experiment moments. However, the order of presentation was randomised for baseline and post-experiment. So, the same plays were presented in the baseline and post-experiment, albeit in a diﬀerent order. Each decisionmaking test took approximately 10 min to complete across each of the two conditions
described in the experimental design. The decision-making tests were back-projected
onto a 2-dimensional screen (2.50 m high × 3.20 m), and participants stood ∼2 m from
the screen. For each scenario, the basketball player had three options [e.g., oﬀensive
(dribble, pass, or shoot) and defensive (block, steal, or move) actions for each scenario],
which were scored one to three (i.e., 3 = best choice, 2 = second best choice, and 1 =
poor choice). So, the minimum score was 20 (i.e., 20 scenes × 1 = 20) and maximum score
was 60 (i.e., 20 scenes × 3 = 60 points). The total decision-making score of each player
was converted to a percentage for analysis. Percentage accuracy values were established
for each participant by dividing the number of points awarded by the total number available and then multiplying by 100 (e.g., 40 points score was converted for 40/60 × 100 =
67%). The response time (i.e., latency between stimulus and verbal response) for the
decision-making task was also calculated. The sessions were recorded and analyzed

Visual search behaviour
The visual search data was measured using portable Eye Tracking-XG (Applied Science Laboratories, USA) equipment with a sampling frequency of 60 Hz during the decision-making
task. A second camera with a frequency of 25 Hz attached to the eye-tracking-XG glasses
worked as the footage of the game scenario. Data from both cameras were combined
using the Gaze Tracker software (Applied Science Laboratories, USA). Visual search data
were analyzed frame-by-frame using video software (Kinovea open source project,
www.kinovea.org). Visual behaviour during the decision-making task was assessed by
the number and duration of ﬁxations obtained, similar to a previous study (Alder et al.,
2021; Smith et al., 2016). Also, the values were averaged across the 20 trials of each participant. Fixation was deﬁned as the individual’s stationary gaze at a speciﬁc location for a
minimum of 120 ms or four frames within 3° visual angle (or less) (Fortes et al., 2021).
So, the number of ﬁxations was the mean of all ﬁxations during the experiment (Fortes
et al., 2021). Besides, the ﬁxations’ duration was deﬁned as the mean duration of all
ﬁxations higher than 120 ms observed during the experiment (Fortes et al., 2021). The
ICC was used to determine the reliability of number of ﬁxations in baseline visits (ICC =
0.96, CI95% = 0.93 to 0.98). The same test also was utilised to determine the reliability of
the duration of ﬁxations in baseline visits (ICC = 0.94, CI95% = 0.92 to 0.99).
Statistical analysis
The Shapiro–Wilk test evaluated data distribution. The Levene test veriﬁed homoscedasticity. The multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to analyze to analyze condition (CON versus VID) × time (pre-versus post-cognitive manipulation) interaction for
VAS (mental fatigue and motivation), Stroop task (accuracy and response time), and
EEG (theta wave). The same test was used to analyze condition (CON versus VID) × time

Discussion
This study analyzed the eﬀect of prolonged video game use (VID) on decision-making
skill and visual search behaviour in male basketball players. The main ﬁndings showed
impaired decision-making and altered visual search behaviour (i.e., number of ﬁxations)
of the athletes, corroborating the study’s hypotheses, except for the duration
of ﬁxations. Interestingly, the VID condition altered the mental fatigue markers investigated (i.e., electrophysiological, behavioural, and perceptual). The present investigation was the ﬁrst to demonstrate changes in the three mental fatigue markers
after a sports genre video game. We recommend more scientiﬁc studies to conﬁrm
these ﬁndings.
Considering that mental fatigue presents multiple consequences, we used three
markers changed following the intervention (i.e., EEG, Stroop task, and VAS) to identify
the mental fatigue level. Regarding the CON condition, the ﬁndings showed no
changes across diﬀerent levels of measurements. Previous studies also found no
mental fatigue after 30-to 60-min watching emotionally neutral documentaries in the
control conditions (Gantois et al., 2020; Lopes et al., 2020; Marcora et al., 2009). About
the VID condition, the results indicated an increase of theta wave on the prefrontal
cortex (PFC), subjective rating of mental fatigue, and response time in the Stroop task
with a large ES. No eﬀect on motivation was observed. These ﬁndings corroborate
other mental fatigue studies (Gantois et al., 2020; Pires et al., 2018; Smith et al., 2019),
although strategies to induce mental fatigue diﬀered. Electrophysiological changes in
the brain (Pires et al., 2018; Wascher et al., 2014), increased response time in inhibitory
control tasks (Gantois et al., 2020; Marcora et al., 2009), and subjective sensation of
exhaustion and lack of energy (Smith et al., 2019; Van-Cutsem et al., 2017) are mental
fatigue indicators. According to Smith et al. (2019), the mental fatigue’s subjective
rating is more sensitive than EEG or behavioural measures (e.g., ﬂanker test), considering
the psychobiological nature of mental fatigue. Moreover, it seems that taking at least 30min of high cognitive demand causes mental fatigue in team sports athletes (Fortes et al.,
2019; Gantois et al., 2020). In the present investigation, a sport-based video game induced
mental fatigue for a prolonged period (i.e., 60-min). The sports genre video game contains
many characteristics that lead to high cognitive demand, such as unpredictability, visual
stimulus, quick response requirements, high perceptual load, the necessity to select
action plans, and an emphasis on peripheral processing (Oei & Patterson, 2014). So,
sport-based video games seem to require high cognitive demands and, once used per
prolonged period, may impair brain attention resources corroborating the theoretical framework (Boksem & Tops, 2008; McMorris, 2020).

Accuracy and response time were impaired after 60-min of a sports genre video game.
These results are not surprising since previous studies about team sports athletes also
revealed the negative impact of mental fatigue on response time and accuracy of
decision-making (Gantois et al., 2020; Hepler, 2015; Hepler & Kovacs, 2017; Smith et al.,
2016; Trecroci et al., 2020). The impaired decision-making skill after mental stress
conﬁrms the psychological performance crisis model (Bar-Eli, 1997). Considering the processing information perspective (Cotterill & Discombe, 2016), it seems that mentally fatigued basketball players change higher cognitive processes, which may aﬀect decisionmaking. It seems that mentally fatigued athletes present approximately 7% of impairment
in decision-making (Fortes et al., 2019; Gantois et al., 2020; Smith et al., 2016). In the
present study, accuracy was impaired 6.4% after 60-min of high cognitive demand. One
potential explanation for the mental fatigue-induced impairment in decision-making is
that players may have been unable to eﬀectively utilise environmental cues to prepare
for their upcoming decisions (Smith et al., 2016). In a basketball-speciﬁc context, this
may reduce the ability to interpret players and ball movement. The ﬁndings of visual
search behaviour in the present study indicated changes for the number of ﬁxations
throughout decision-making tasks when mentally fatigued after 60-min of a sports
genre video game. These results may explain the impaired decision-making skill.
Interestingly, previous investigations support our ﬁndings (Alder et al., 2021; Smith
et al., 2016). Mental fatigue has been associated with attentional focus changes from
goal-directed to stimulus-driven attention (Wascher et al., 2014), leading to inattentional
blindness (i.e., failure to notice crucial cues). When mentally fatigued, basketball players
appeared to perform fewer ﬁxations for relevant cues (e.g., opponent or team colleagues).
Thus, a comprehensive search strategy involving many ﬁxations is required to make themselves aware of the players’ positions, movements, and passing opportunities (Fortes
et al., 2021; Vaeyens et al., 2007). Some scientists attributed the higher number of
ﬁxations made by skilled players to their tendency to search for additional locations to
identify the gaps between opponents (Fortes et al., 2021; McRobert et al., 2011). The
authors of these studies seem to agree that diﬀerences in ﬁxations reﬂect the ability of
players to adapt their visual search behaviour to the continuously changing demands.
In that sense, it seems mentally fatigued players can have their visual search strategy
impaired. Once the visual search behaviour is impaired, the mentally fatigued player
will track smaller amounts of relevant cues to the game context.
Also, the players’ perception modiﬁcations (e.g., position of teammates and
opponents) may explain such decision-making impairment (Van-Cutsem et al., 2019).
Mentally fatigued athletes do not correctly process the best choices in the game
context. Besides, they need more time to make a better decision (Trecroci et al., 2020).
Although a mentally fatigued player might dribble and take advantage of his opponent,
the action might not be as eﬀective as it should be. For example, the dribble might not
create space in the opponent’s defense, challenging scoring achievements. Speciﬁcally,
the player in possession of the ball is likely to explore information related to the direct
opponent’s body position. Environmental factors such as opponent pressure may aﬀect
the decision-making process. A recent investigation found that the quality of the
decision-making choice under opponent pressure was signiﬁcantly lower than without
opponent pressure for soccer players (Musculus, Bader, Sander, & Vogt, 2021). This
ﬁnding could indicate that opponent pressure disturbed the decision-making process

Regarding the duration of ﬁxations throughout the decision-making task, the results

The ﬁndings corroborate a previous study (Alder et al., 2021). On the other hand, Smith
et al. (2016) found a decrease in the ﬁxation duration. Both studies were conducted
with a diﬀerent sport than basketball. Besides, the induction of mental fatigue also
diﬀered between investigations. Both scientiﬁc investigations used the Stroop task for
30-min to induce mental fatigue in soccer players (Alder et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2016),
while the present study adopted 60-min of a video game. Also, the conﬁguration and contrasts of the decision-making task were diﬀerent. Alder et al. (2021) used anticipation tasks
with visual occlusion for oﬀensive trials with reduced cue numbers. Smith et al. (2016)
applied a decision-making task with varied cue numbers (i.e., 1 vs. 1 at 5 vs. 5), while in
the present study, the decision-making task was used with trials adopting the same
cue numbers (i.e., 5 vs. 5). These diﬀerences between studies might explain the divergence ﬁndings. More studies are required to clarify if mental fatigue can aﬀect the duration of ﬁxations.
While the current investigation reveals the negative impact of mental fatigue induced
by a sports action video game on basketball-speciﬁc decision-making and visual search
behaviour, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cognitive task for inducement
mental fatigue (i.e., sport-based videogame) was not controlled. The mental load may be
diﬀerent between basketball players, which may cause diﬀerent magnitudes of mental
fatigue. Besides, we did not examine the Stroop task’s level diﬀerence (e.g., the performance diﬀerence between congruent and incongruent stimuli). So, it is diﬃcult to provide
any speciﬁc inference about “inhibitory control”. Thus, the results (i.e., manipulation
check) should be interpreted with caution. Also, the decision-making task was performed
on a two-dimensional screen, as previous studies also used (Fortes et al., 2021; Smith et al.,
2016; Vaeyens et al., 2007). Besides, the required response to the stimuli during the
decision-making task is also a limitation because, in a game, players do not verbally
respond to stimuli; they act. Therefore, this decision-making task is not representative
of the performance environment in terms of how stimuli are detected (perception) or
how players respond to the stimuli (action). Regarding the importance of perceptionaction coupling in motor control, this limitation could be mentioned. However, these
limitations do not undermine the substantial impairments to basketball-speciﬁc
decision-making induced by mental fatigue. Future research should take a more practical
approach to assess mental fatigue’s impact on basketball performance.
Conclusions
This investigation demonstrates that mental fatigue induced by a sports action video
game impairs basketball-speciﬁc decision-making skill. It is unlikely that these impairments are related to changes in the number of ﬁxations in visual search behaviour. Alternatively, mental fatigue may impair players’ ability to perceive environmental cues,
resulting in attention and decision-making strategies changes. Nevertheless, the
current investigation results have signiﬁcant implications for basketball, where making
