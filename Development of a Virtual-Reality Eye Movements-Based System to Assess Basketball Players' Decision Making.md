Abstract
Background and Purpose: This study developed a reliable and ecologically valid
virtual reality eye movements-based assessment system to evaluate basketball
players’ decision-making abilities.
Research Design: The system incorporated expert ratings, inter-group differences,
analysis of covariance, and test-retest reliability assessments to validate its effectiveness
and reliability.
Study Sample: A VR system with 100 task scenarios was used to assess decisionmaking performance and visual behavior. 30 high-level and 30 low-level players participated in two phases.
Data Collection and Analysis: In Phase 1, a panel of basketball experts (N = 3) rated
the decision-making scenarios. Kendall’s coefﬁcient of concordance (W) was used to
analyze expert ratings, conﬁrming content validity. Mann-Whitney U and independent
samples t-tests were employed to assess decision quality and decision time differences
between high- and low-level groups. Gender was included as a covariate in ANCOVA
to control for gender effects. Gaze patterns were analyzed to examine differences in
visual behavior. Phase 2 involved test-retest reliability analysis using Pearson’s correlation coefﬁcient (r).

Introduction
Decision-making is a crucial perceptual-cognitive skill in sports, deﬁned as the ability to
perceive and accurately interpret game-related information to select the appropriate motor
response. It involves perceiving, processing information, and taking action within a sports
context (Baker et al., 2003). Unlike general decision-making tasks, sports decisionmaking is characterized by limited available information, high time pressure, and uncertain outcomes (Ara´ujo et al., 2006). In basketball, decision-making is particularly
important on offense, as players must make quick and effective decisions to score against
various defensive strategies (Tsai et al., 2021). This requires players to analyze opponents’ positions and strategies and respond accurately and swiftly. Reading the defense
and making rapid decisions are essential skills for high-level players (Jia et al., 2024).
Elite athletes excel at decision-making because they can quickly identify meaningful
game patterns and use visual information to guide their choices. They know what to look
for and when, extracting meaning from the most information-rich areas of the visual ﬁeld
and taking appropriate actions (Jackson & Farrow, 2005). The duration and location of
gaze are key perceptual strategies for extracting relevant information (Natsuhara et al.,
2020). Visual search behavior is a critical component of the decision-making process,
enabling athletes to process information from various areas. Thus, the timing (when

Research indicates that experts exhibit more effective gaze behaviors in decisionmaking, as they leverage their existing expertise to identify and interpret relevant cues.
This behavior enables experts to make more effective decisions than non-experts, as
they can quickly extract meaningful, structured information from the visual scene.
However, these gaze patterns vary depending on different decision tasks and speciﬁc
contexts (Put et al., 2014). For instance, skilled athletes may rely on lower search rates
(i.e., fewer ﬁxations per second) in some decision tasks, while displaying higher search
rates in others (Ryu et al., 2013). When the same task (e.g., predicting a football pass) is
combined with changes in the number of players in the scene, the visual search patterns
are also notably altered (Ryu et al., 2016).Moreover, differences in research methods
can lead to varying visual search patterns (Kittel et al., 2021). For example, when
examining basketball decision-making tasks, researchers often use decision tasks
derived from game footage or videos taken from a broadcast perspective, presented in a
third-person viewpoint. The decisions and visual search patterns made by athletes in
response to these tasks, delivered via computer screens, may not accurately reﬂect their
decision-making responses in real-game situations. Therefore, to more effectively
assess athletes’ decision-making abilities and visual search behavior, it is essential to
maximize the similarity between decision tasks and real-game scenarios.
With the advancement of virtual reality (VR) technology, more researchers are applying
it to sports studies. VR uses computer-generated environments and interactive methods to
immerse users in a virtual world, closely replicating real game scenarios and providing
athletes with an immersive experience (Jia et al., 2024). In sports decision-making research,
VR technology allows athletes to make decisions and exhibit visual search behaviors that
closely align with real-game situations through realistic and immersive environments. This
immersion helps bridge the gap between laboratory settings and actual performance,
offering insights that are more reﬂective of athletes’ true capabilities (Le Noury et al., 2021).
Researchers developing virtual basketball decision-making assessment systems
typically use two formats: 360-degree VR and animated VR. Studies show no signiﬁcant differences in their effectiveness for assessing and training decision-making,
indicating that both approaches are effective (Tsai et al., 2021). VR-based decision
tests, tailored to speciﬁc sports tasks, can effectively differentiate between expert and
novice athletes and referees (Kittel et al., 2019). Presented from a ﬁrst-person perspective, these VR-based tests offer greater ecological validity than traditional video
methods and provide more experimental control than real-game situations. Therefore,
VR-based systems are considered valuable tools for assessing decision-making performance (Panchuk et al., 2018). However, despite their potential, studies using VR to
assess and enhance basketball players’ decision-making abilities have not explored the
reliability and validity of these assessment systems (Pag´e et al., 2019). Additionally, no
standardized assessment framework speciﬁcally for basketball players has been developed. Without a reliability assessment, it is difﬁcult to determine whether observed
differences in players’ decision-making performance result from genuine skill differences or from an unreliable testing system.

Therefore, further empirical research is necessary to establish the validity and reliability of VR-based basketball decision-making assessment systems before they can
be conﬁdently used in research settings. From a practical perspective, an effective and
reliable VR-based sports decision-making assessment system could be instrumental in
identifying promising basketball players and monitoring the improvement of decisionmaking skills over time. This study aims to develop a VR-based sports decision-making
assessment system by integrating VR equipment and eye-tracking devices as the
measurement tool. The system’s content validity, structural validity, and reliability will
be tested as follows: First, a basketball expert panel will screen and rate the decision
tasks, and expert rating consistency will be analyzed to validate the content validity of
the system. Second, assessments will be conducted with both novice and expert
basketball players to examine whether the system can differentiate between their
decision-making abilities, thus validating the system’s structural validity. Finally, a testretest procedure will be implemented with novice basketball players to analyze the
correlation between the test and retest results, verifying the reliability of the system. The
procedures for testing validity and reliability will be detailed in the following sections.
By combining external observational indicators of players’ eye movement characteristics with internal decision-making features in rapid-response scenarios, this
approach provides a comprehensive assessment of decision-making ability.
Development of a VR-Based Basketball Decision-Making
System
Setting up the Sports Decision-Making Trails
The virtual sports scenarios in this study are based on the NBA2K23 game, which uses
motion capture and artiﬁcial intelligence technologies to closely replicate real NBA
game situations. To ensure that player attributes do not inﬂuence the results, the players
in the study are represented as generic, unknown players, and factors such as game time
and score are excluded. Representative basketball decision-making trials are selected
by recording simulated full-court basketball game videos and employing a time occlusion paradigm (Larkin et al., 2014). This study used Adobe Premiere Pro 2023 to edit
simulated game videos from NBA2K23, initially selecting 100 decision-making clips,
each approximately 10 seconds long. For each decision trail, the video pauses on the
ﬁrst frame for 3 seconds to present information about the decision-making player. The
video then progresses to the moment when the player receives or dribbles the ball,
requiring the participant to make a quick decision based on the on-court situation (e.g.,
shooting, passing, or dribbling) and press the corresponding button on the controller:
button A for shooting, button B for passing, and button L for dribbling. Following
established research and expert recommendations (e.g., player size ratio, viewpoint
immersion), the size ratio of the players in the scenario is adjusted to match that of the
participant, and the video perspective is shifted to a viewpoint behind the decisionmaking player. This adjustment allows participants to view the court from a ﬁrst-person
perspective (see Figure 1).

Selection and Scoring of Sports Decision-Making Trails
To ensure the content validity of the basketball decision-making assessment system,
this study designed an expert panel evaluation process aimed at selecting and conﬁrming the authenticity, difﬁculty, and decision order of the tasks. The expert panel
consisted of two basketball professors and one professional Chinese national-level
basketball player, with an average of 25 years of basketball experience. To minimize
mutual inﬂuence and ensure independence among the experts, the evaluation process
was divided into two stages. In the initial phase, the experts independently viewed the
video clips for each decision trail and rated them based on their authenticity, difﬁculty,
and priority of choices. This process ensured the independence of expert ratings,
providing a reliable data foundation for subsequent consistency analysis. Afterward, the
expert panel held a roundtable meeting to discuss and review the ratings, conﬁrming
whether each decision trail accurately reﬂected real-game decision situations, whether
its difﬁculty was appropriate, and whether the decision choices were logically ordered.
During the meeting, the experts discussed according to a structured rating questionnaire, ensuring that each expert’s opinions were fully expressed and recorded. To
prevent any one expert from dominating the discussion and inﬂuencing the ratings, a
time limit was set for speaking, ensuring that each expert had an equal opportunity to
voice their opinion. This discussion mechanism effectively minimized potential biases
in the rating process and provided a scientiﬁc basis for optimizing the decision trail in
the future.
During the evaluation process, the expert panel used a 5-point Likert scale to rate
each decision trail. The expert evaluation dimensions included: (1) Authenticity –
assessing whether the trail realistically reﬂects decision-making situations in actual
games; (2) Decision Difﬁculty – evaluating whether the difﬁculty of the trail is appropriate for athletes of varying skill levels; (3) Decision Choice Priority – assessing
whether the decision points in the task are clear and whether the order of decisions is
reasonable and aligns with game scenarios. Each dimension was rated using the Likert
scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). After viewing each video trail, the

scored according to the expert panel’s priority ratings: three points for the ﬁrst choice,
two points for the second choice, and one point for the third choice. This scoring system
provides a more nuanced evaluation of decision-making quality compared to merely
recording decision accuracy, as it better reﬂects the reasonableness of athletes’ choices.
Construction of the Virtual Sports Decision-Making Assessment System
The virtual basketball decision-making assessment system was developed using the
Unity 2022.3.4 game engine and operates on a system with an Intel Core i7-6700K
CPU @ 4.00 GHz, 24 GB RAM, and an Nvidia GeForce GTX 1080 Ti GPU, running
on Windows 10. A virtual reality basketball arena was initially created in Unity
2022.3.4, with decision-making video clips rendered using proportional scaling
methods. Participants experience the virtual court through a head-mounted display
(HMD), which provides an immersive ﬁrst-person perspective, allowing them to rotate
their heads and view the decision-making scenarios. The HMD setup includes an HTC
Vive Cosmos headset and a Drooling F1 eye-tracking accessory. This equipment
presents the virtual scenarios and records participants’ visual gaze. At the end of each
decision task video, participants use the HTC Vive Cosmos Controller to make their
decisions: button A for shooting, button B for passing, and button L for dribbling.

ofﬁcial experiment began, we conducted a preliminary experiment by inviting

Validity Testing of the Assessment System
To test the validity of the VR-based basketball decision-making assessment system, this
study recruited basketball players of varying skill levels to determine if the system
could effectively differentiate between these levels. Eye-tracking equipment was used
to record participants’ visual behavior. A comparative visual analysis of the eyetracking data was conducted to examine the decision-making behaviors of high-level
versus low-level players, further validating the effectiveness of the assessment system.
Participants
Sixty university students from a sports college were assigned to two groups based on
their basketball experience and skill level. The high-level group consisted of 30 basketball team members (25 males and 5 females), all with a national basketball level of
second tier or above and experience in provincial or higher-level competitions. The
average age of this group was 23.96 ± 2.255 years, with an average of 8.92 ±
1.816 years of experience and a weekly training time of 6.58 ± 1.954 hours. The lowlevel group comprised 30 non-basketball major university students (15 males and
15 females), with an average age of 21.7 ± 2.344 years, an average experience of
0.957 ± 1.0103 years, and a weekly practice time of 0.587 ± 0.8614 hours. Both groups
had normal or corrected vision and were right-handed. All participants provided informed consent. The survey revealed that none of the participants had previous experience using the VR system.

Data Analysis. Data analysis for this study was conducted using SPSS 26.0. First, the
decision scores and decision times were subjected to the Shapiro-Wilk test to assess
their normality. The results indicated that the decision scores for the novice group did
not pass the normality test (p = 0.023), while the expert group data were normally
distributed (p = 0.564). Similarly, decision time data for both groups followed a normal
distribution (p > 0.05).
To address the non-normality of the decision score data, several common data
transformation methods were attempted, including logarithmic, square root, and reciprocal transformations. Despite multiple transformations, the data still did not meet
normality. Therefore, non-parametric tests were applied to the decision scores to avoid
using non-normally distributed data in parametric analyses.
For decision scores, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences
between the high-level and low-level groups. For decision time, since the data were
normally distributed, an independent samples t-test was used to compare the differences
between the two groups. Additionally, to control for the potential inﬂuence of gender on
the results, gender was included as a covariate in the analysis of covariance (ANCOVA)
to test the differences in decision times between the high-level and low-level groups
while eliminating the confounding effect of gender.

Reliability Testing of the Assessment System
To evaluate the reliability of the VR-based basketball decision-making assessment
system, this study evaluated the stability of individual responses through a retest
conducted within a short time interval (14 days). This time interval follows the recommendation of Pedhazur and Pedhazur-Schmelkin, which suggests that tests should
be conducted within a relatively short period (preferably 10-24 days) to assess random
variations in the test characteristics rather than changes in behavior or ability (Heck,
1994). To ensure consistency between the two tests, standardized procedures and
instructions were followed, and all participants were tested under the same environmental conditions. For example, all tests were conducted during the same time frame,
and physical environments and prior activities were kept as consistent as possible to
minimize the inﬂuence of external variables on the test results. Such environmental
control enhanced the replicability of the study’s ﬁndings.

Discussion
Previous research shows that VR is effective for assessing perceptual-cognitive skills,
such as sports decision-making, and has been successfully applied in basketball
decision-making studies (Pag´e et al., 2019; Panchuk et al., 2018; Tsai et al., 2021,
2022). Researchers typically develop virtual basketball decision-making assessment
systems using either 360-degree VR or animated VR formats (Le Noury et al., 2022).
Pag´e et al. found that while 2D video training improves basketball players’ decisionmaking abilities in real-game situations, it does not transfer to new, untrained scenarios.
In contrast, VR video training not only enhances decision-making in real games but also
provides generalized beneﬁts across different scenarios. This suggests that VR can
effectively improve decision-making skills and training outcomes (Pag´e et al., 2019).
Panchuk et al. reported no signiﬁcant difference in decision-making performance on the
real court before and after VR training, although signiﬁcant improvements were observed during immersive VR testing (Panchuk et al., 2018). Similarly, Tsai et al.
compared traditional training methods with immersive VR environments, including
360-degree and animated VR. The study found no signiﬁcant difference in decisionmaking knowledge acquisition between traditional and VR methods. However, VR
training led to improved decision-making times, highlighting its potential to enhance
the efﬁciency of decision-making processes (Tsai et al., 2021).
The inconsistent results across studies may stem from inadequate evaluation of the
effectiveness and reliability of VR-based basketball decision-making assessment
systems. Without assessing these systems’ effectiveness, it is challenging to determine
if VR-based tests accurately reﬂect athletes’ decision-making abilities. Furthermore, the
lack of reliability evaluation makes it difﬁcult to discern whether observed changes in
decision-making abilities result from the intervention itself or from an unreliable testing
method (Kittel et al., 2019). Therefore, developing an effective and reliable VR-based
assessment system is crucial for accurately evaluating basketball players’ decisionmaking skills.
Previous research shows that decision assessment systems have been validated for

However, creating detailed 360-degree VR decision tasks requires considerable time

This study makes a signiﬁcant contribution to the ﬁeld of perceptual-cognitive
decision-making by evaluating both the effectiveness and reliability of a VR-based
basketball decision-making assessment system. The research assesses effectiveness
in terms of content and structure and evaluates reliability through retesting. The
study began with the screening of sports decision-making task scenarios, followed
by a roundtable discussion with basketball experts to ﬁnalize the suitability and
sequence of the decision tasks, ensuring content validity. To validate the system’s
structural effectiveness, it needed to distinguish between high-level and low-level
athletes. The results demonstrate that the VR-based basketball decision-making
assessment system effectively differentiates between professional and novice
players, with the high-level group signiﬁcantly outperforming the low-level group in
decision-making performance. This distinction is largely attributed to the realism,
immersion, and high interactivity of the system’s decision-making tasks. Recently,
virtual reality environments with high ecological validity and psychological ﬁdelity
have become the preferred tools for decision-making research. To maximize the
effectiveness of decision-making training, it is crucial to design tasks that accurately
reﬂect real-world environments (Panchuk et al., 2018). Virtual sports environments
created with VR technology can closely replicate real game situations, enhancing the
sense of presence, attention, and engagement (Pag´e et al., 2019). Previous studies
have found that traditional computer videos limit athletes’ views to the camera’s
direction during ﬁlming, restricting task interactivity. HMDs overcome this limitation by allowing athletes to view more information through head movements at key
points in the task clips. This expanded view enables athletes to interact more
naturally with the virtual environment, enhancing their decision-making capabilities
(Brault et al., 2012).
This study not only compares decision-making performance between high-level and
low-level basketball players but also supports the validity of the evaluation system
through an analysis of visual behavior. The analysis of gaze trajectories shows that
high-level players cover a broader range of gaze points during decision-making. This
suggests that they gather more information and make decisions more quickly and
effectively. In contrast, the gaze points of the low-level group are more concentrated,
with less attention paid to critical information (Ryu et al., 2016). This behavior suggests
that the high-level group is able to rapidly gather overall scene information and dynamically adjust their attention distribution during the decision-making process. In
contrast, the gaze trajectories of the low-level group were primarily focused on noncritical areas, with a narrower attention span, often limited to the ball and the defending
opponent, without effectively integrating scene information. This further validates the
high-level group’s advantage in visual search and information processing strategies.

evolving over time. It is a continuous process rather than a single decision point
(Ashford et al., 2021). Decision-makers accumulate and process information gradually,
not instantaneously (Johnson, 2006). As the scene changes and key information becomes less clear, effective decision-makers must quickly gather and process relevant
information at the appropriate moments. High-level basketball players, with their
extensive training and numerous game experiences, accumulate a wealth of specialized
knowledge (Miller & Clapp, 2011). When engaging with decision-making task scenarios, they can draw on this expertise to compare scenes with their specialized
knowledge, analyze situations, gather relevant information swiftly, and make informed
decisions based on their understanding of their team’s defensive conditions and offensive tactics (Spering, 2022). In sports scenarios, due to the limitations of visual
attention, athletes cannot process all available information simultaneously. As a result,
attention is directed toward speciﬁc objects, and this selection is not random but
inﬂuenced by certain factors. Attention is characterized by both bottom-up and topdown processes, driven respectively by stimuli (visual factors) and goals (cognitive
factors) (Hu et al., 2024). Research has shown that the roles of visual factors (bottomup) and cognitive factors (top-down) in guiding attention are interdependent. The
inﬂuence of visual factors on bottom-up attention is revealed early on, when athletes
ﬁrst observe speciﬁc information, which is then matched with existing cognitive
knowledge in the brain, thereby exerting a top-down effect on attention. This leads
athletes to actively allocate more attention to critical information. Therefore, this also
explains why high-level athletes are able to actively extract key information in a short
period of time.
Previous studies have not only failed to validate the effectiveness of VR-based
assessment systems but also have not reported on their reliability (Pag´e et al., 2019;
Panchuk et al., 2018; Tsai et al., 2021). Without established reliability, it is difﬁcult to
determine whether differences in athletes’ decision-making test results are due to
random assessment errors or actual changes in decision-making ability. This study
addresses this gap by establishing the test-retest reliability of the decision assessment
system. Future research can use this validated system to reliably monitor changes in
basketball players’ decision-making performance. Empirical studies have shown that
VR-based training is effective for rapidly enhancing athletes’ decision-making skills,
especially during the off-season or when recovering from injuries. As VR technology
continues to advance, researchers have the opportunity to further explore its potential to
improve athletes’ performance. Future studies can build on the methods used in this
research to develop a range of assessment systems for evaluating and training decisionmaking skills.

Conclusion
In conclusion, this study is the ﬁrst to validate both the effectiveness and reliability of a
VR-based basketball decision-making assessment system. The results show that VR is a
reliable, effective, and ecologically valid tool for assessing basketball players’ decisionmaking abilities. VR-based scenarios offer greater realism, immersion, and interactivity. This study also integrates eye-tracking technology with VR, enabling a
comprehensive analysis of performance differences and changes in athletes’ decisionmaking abilities. By examining visual behavior, this approach provides deeper insights
into the cognitive processes behind decision-making, exploring the intrinsic relationship between cognition and action. At the same time, the system has signiﬁcant
advantages in talent selection and training, providing coaches with data support and
scientiﬁc evidence. Future research can use this assessment system as a standard for
evaluating and monitoring changes in sports decision-making performance during
training interventions, ensuring effectiveness, reliability, and ecological validity.
Additionally, this study provides a framework for developing new VR-based decision
assessment systems in other sports contexts, aiming to enhance the effectiveness,
reliability, and ecological validity of perceptual-cognitive performance research
methods. Since the sports decision-making assessment system developed in this study
is only applicable to basketball, its effectiveness in other sports has not yet been
explored. However, the system has the potential to be extended.
However, the study also has some limitations that may affect the generalizability and
external validity of the results. First, gender imbalance is a limitation of this study.
Although we included both high-level and low-level basketball players in the sample,
there was an imbalance in gender distribution. This gender difference may inﬂuence
decision-making performance, particularly in virtual reality environments, where
gender differences could affect comfort levels and performance. Future studies should
ensure gender balance in the sample to further validate the results of this study. Second,
this study used NBA-speciﬁc scenarios as the context for the decision-making tasks.
While these scenarios may be highly representative for high-level athletes, they may
lack generalizability for low-level or non-professional players, particularly for those
unfamiliar with NBA games. Therefore, future studies could consider using more
diverse and inclusive basketball scenarios to ensure that the results are applicable to a
broader range of athletes.
Despite these limitations, this study provides preliminary empirical support for the
development and application of a VR-based basketball decision-making assessment
system. Future research should further explore ways to overcome these limitations,
expand sample diversity, and assess the system’s effectiveness and applicability in
different contexts.
