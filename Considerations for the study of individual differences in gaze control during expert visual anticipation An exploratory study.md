Considerations for the study of individual differences in gaze control during expert visual 
1 
anticipation: An exploratory study 
2 
 
3 
Abstract 
4 
Recent perspectives for the study of perceptual-motor expertise have highlighted the 
5 
importance for considering variability in gaze behaviour. The present paper explores the 
6 
prevalence of variability in gaze behaviour in an anticipation task through examining 
7 
goalkeepers gaze behaviours when saving soccer penalty kicks, with a primary focus on 
8 
offering new considerations for the study of variability in gaze behaviour. A subset of data 
9 
from five goalkeepers in the previously published article of Dicks et al. (2010) were 
10 
reanalysed, with a focus on ten successful penalty saves for each goalkeeper. As the aim was 
11 
to conduct exploratory analyses of individual differences in goalkeeping performance, data 
12 
were not averaged across participants and instead intra- and inter-individual differences are 
13 
described using descriptive statistics. The main observation was that variation in the 
14 
goalkeepers’ gaze behaviours existed and were evident both between and within individuals, 
15 
specifically with regards to quiet eye duration but also for percentage viewing time and visual 
16 
search patterns. However, QE location appeared to represent the only invariant gaze measure 
17 
with the location being on the ball for the majority of trials. The current exploratory analysis 
18 
suggested that experienced goalkeepers did not converge on the same gaze patterns during 
19 
successful anticipation performance. The implications of these findings are discussed in 
20 
relation to extant gaze behaviour literature before considering implications for future 
21 
research.

Résumé 
3 
 
4 
De récentes perspectives ont souligné l’importance de tenir compte de la variabilité 
5 
du comportement visuel dans l’étude de l’expertise perceptivo-motrice. Cet article explore la 
6 
prévalence de la variabilité du comportement visuel dans une tâche d’anticipation en se 
7 
focalisant sur la proposition de nouvelles méthodes pour étudier la variabilité du 
8 
comportement visuel. Pour ce faire, nous avons examiné le comportement visuel de gardiens 
9 
de but lors d’arrêts de pénaltys. Des données provenant de 5 gardiens de but d’un précédent 
10 
article de Dicks et al. (2010) ont été réanalysées en se focalisant sur 10 arrêts réussis pour 
11 
chacun des gardiens de but. L’objectif de cette étude est de réaliser une analyse exploratoire 
12 
des différences interindividuelles. Les caractéristiques du comportement visuel de chaque 
13 
gardien de but ont été décrites à l’aide de statistiques descriptives plutôt que de moyenner les 
14 
données pour l’ensemble des participants. La principale observation a été que la variabilité du 
15 
comportement visuel des gardiens était présente à la fois entre les individus, mais aussi pour 
16 
un même individu entre ses essais. Plus précisément, ces variabilités apparaissaient pour la 
17 
durée du « quiet eye » (QE, dernière fixation visuelle avant l’amorce du mouvement) mais 
18 
aussi dans la répartition du temps de regard et du pattern de recherche visuel. Cependant, la 
19 
localisation du QE a été la seule mesure invariante, le regard étant dirigé vers le ballon sur la 
20 
plupart des essais. Cette analyse exploratoire suggère que les gardiens de but expérimentés ne 
21 
convergent pas vers un comportement visuel similaire pour réussir des tâches d’anticipation. 
22 
Ces résultats sont discutés au regard de la littérature existante avant de proposer des pistes de 
23 
recherches futures.

Introduction 
1 
In the domain of expertise research in sport, an extensive body of literature now exists 
2 
on the perceptual-motor processes that underpin elite performance (van der Kamp, Rivas, van 
3 
Doorn & Savelsbergh, 2008; Williams & Ericsson, 2005). One facet of this research has been 
4 
dedicated to the examination of gaze behaviours that are associated with the control of 
5 
highly-skilled perceptual-motor behaviours. Gaze behaviours have typically been studied in 
6 
three broadly conceptualised sporting contexts: aiming tasks (e.g., a basketball free throw), 
7 
anticipation tasks (e.g., a goalkeeper anticipating the direction of a penalty kick prior to the 
8 
penalty taker’s foot-ball contact), and decision-making tasks (e.g., deciding which teammate 
9 
to pass the ball to) (Vickers, 2007). Typically, authors have used experimental designs that 
10 
compare between a group of expert and non-expert performers to attain differences in gaze 
11 
behaviour (Williams & Ericsson, 2005). Several measures have been studied to enhance 
12 
understanding of differences in gaze behaviours, usually focussing on variations around the 
13 
location, duration, and number of fixations (McGuckian, Cole, & Pepping, 2018; Dicks, 
14 
Button, & Davids, 2010). 
15 
Research has demonstrated an experimental advantage for experts in perceptual-motor 
16 
skill and such differences tend to be reflected by differences in gaze patterns. For example, in 
17 
anticipation tasks, a common finding is that experts tend to use fewer fixations of a longer 
18 
duration than novices (Dicks, Davids, & Button, 2009). Moreover, in aiming tasks such as the 
19 
basketball free-throw, experts tend to utilise a longer final fixation compared with non20 
experts prior to final movement onset (Vickers, 1996). The tendency of researchers to analyse 
21 
gaze measures at the group-level by averaging data across participants and trials (e.g., Dicks 
22 
et al., 2010) has recently been questioned as this approach implies that the same gaze pattern, 
23 
when utilized by all participants in the same task or experiment, will lead to equivalent levels 
24 
of success (Dicks, Button, Davids, Chow, & van der Kamp, 2017). Implicit within this view 
25 

--- [Página 5] ---
is the implication that a consistent and repeatable gaze pattern may be replicated from trial to 
1 
trial with little deviation between or within individual participants. However, evidence 
2 
indicates that individual differences in gaze behaviour appear to exist between performers of 
3 
the same skill level when successfully completing the same task (e.g., Croft, Button, & 
4 
Dicks, 2010; Mann, Coombes, Mousseau, & Janelle, 2011). For instance, in a ten-pin 
5 
bowling study, when participant data were considered at the group level, Chia et al. (2017) 
6 
observed that experts utilised a longer QE duration than novices. However, the authors 
7 
identified large inter- and intra-individual variation in QE duration without consequence for 
8 
performance in the expert group. Thus, the implication is that research which does not 
9 
consider variations in perceptual capacities within a skill-group or during learning, may not 
10 
adequately reveal the gaze patterns used during perceptual-motor control (Dicks et al., 2017). 
11 
With the above concerns in mind, findings derived from comparative studies of gaze 
12 
behaviours have often been used as the basis for learning studies, within which novices are 
13 
trained to replicate the gaze patterns of experts. This approach has provided evidence of 
14 
performance improvements after gaze training in aiming tasks (e.g., Vine, Moore, & Wilson, 
15 
2011) although such findings have not been replicated across all studies, including 
16 
anticipation training interventions (Klostermann, Vater, Kredel, & Hossner, 2015). The 
17 
finding that training individuals to replicate the gaze patterns of more skilled performers does 
18 
not always lead to increased performance in anticipation tasks raises possible doubt over 
19 
whether purported optimal or universal gaze patterns exist (Dicks et al., 2017). 
20 
Representative of the approach to train novices to anticipate using the average of expert gaze 
21 
patterns, Savelsbergh and colleagues (2010) used evidence from prior research to create a 
22 
visual search pattern for recreational goalkeepers to learn to use when attempting to 
23 
anticipate the direction of penalty kicks (see Savelsbergh, Williams, van der Kamp, & Ward, 
24 
2002; Savelsbergh, van der Kamp, Williams, & Ward, 2005). From run-up initiation to ball 
25 

--- [Página 6] ---
contact, the visual search pattern aimed to guide the keeper to first look at the head of the 
1 
penalty taker, then hip region, and then to the lower leg regions, specifically the orientation 
2 
of the non-kicking leg. The authors found support for training on the basis of this pattern 
3 
because recreational goalkeepers that used this gaze behaviour, improved their performance 
4 
of predicting the direction of penalty kicks (see Figure 1). However, the authors found that 
5 
another visual search pattern correlated positively with anticipation performance, suggesting 
6 
that more than one gaze pattern could be used to be successful within a given task. 
7 
The finding that more than one gaze pattern can be used to successfully anticipate the 
8 
direction of penalty kicks (Savelsbergh et al., 2010), is supported by results from Navia and 
9 
colleagues (Navia, Dicks, van der Kamp, & Ruiz, 2017) who analysed expert futsal 
10 
goalkeepers gaze behaviours when saving penalty kicks under differing spatiotemporal 
11 
constraints (from 6 m and 10 m distances). The authors found that gaze behaviours in the first 
12 
phase of the run up differed markedly between participants in the location and timing of 
13 
fixations, whereas the gaze variation decreased in the second phase, which was interpreted as 
14 
the pick-up of more reliable visual information as the kickers’ actions unfolded (specifically 
15 
within 250 ms of ball contact) (Navia et al., 2017; Diaz, Fajen, & Phillips, 2012). These 
16 
findings therefore indicated that skilled anticipation may not be reliant on a consistent and 
17 
repeatable search pattern (e.g. Abernethy, Schorer, Jackson, & Hagemann, 2012; Savelsbergh 
18 
et al. 2005, 2010). Further, it was reported that gaze variables, such as the percentage viewing 
19 
time spent looking at different locations, were highly variable between participants, 
20 
suggesting that this measure does not necessarily capture expert gaze behaviour. Instead, it 
21 
was proposed that looking in the right time(s) at the right place(s) may, in fact, be particularly 
22 
critical for successful performance in penalty kick interceptive actions (e.g., Mann, Spratford 
23 
& Abernethy, 2013), and that the search pattern used to arrive at a particular gaze location 
24 
may not be a necessary prerequisite for successful performance. 
25 

--- [Página 7] ---
The findings of Navia and colleagues (2017) may be indicative of a quiet eye (QE) 
1 
gaze pattern. QE, which is the final fixation prior to final movement onset (Vickers, 1996; 
2 
Vickers, 2007), has been proposed to reflect the parameterization of the necessary movement 
3 
without the pick-up of further visual information during the control of action (Panchuk & 
4 
Vickers, 2009). For example, Panchuk and Vickers (2006) reported that skilled ice hockey 
5 
goaltenders utilised significantly longer QE durations for saved shots in comparison with 
6 
trials in which goals were conceded. However, in contrast to this finding, Piras and Vickers 
7 
(2011) reported an equivocal result with regard to the importance of QE and response 
8 
accuracy amongst skilled goalkeepers when facing instep penalty kicks. Specifically, it was 
9 
reported that QE duration associated with a fixation on the ball led to less successful 
10 
performance whereas a QE duration associated with a visual anchor location (Williams & 
11 
Davids, 1998) between the ball and penalty taker led to more successful performance. Thus, 
12 
evidence concerning the suitability of QE as a facet of expertise in anticipation appears to 
13 
warrant further investigation to understand whether the duration or location of this fixation 
14 
differentiates successful performance (see also, McPherson & Vickers, 2004; Rodrigues, 
15 
Vickers, & Williams, 2002). 
16 
With a primary focus on anticipation in penalty kicks, the aim of this paper is to offer 
17 
new considerations for the study of variability in gaze patterns. A subset of data from the 
18 
previously published article of Dicks et al. (2010) will be reanalysed in order to help achieve 
19 
this aim. In this original study, eight skilled goalkeepers had their gaze patterns and 
20 
anticipation performance measured across five experimental conditions. In one condition, 
21 
participants were required to attempt to save non-deception penalty kicks in real-time as is 
22 
required during competition. Five out of the eight goalkeepers tested in the original study of 
23 
Dicks et al. (2010) saved more than ten penalty kicks and subsequently, these goalkeepers 
24 
had their gaze data reanalysed to permit a focus on ten successful trials for each participant 
25 

--- [Página 8] ---
(e.g., Land & McLeod, 2000; Mann et al., 2013). Different gaze behaviour measures that are 
1 
commonplace in extant research will be examined in order to ascertain how the respective 
2 
measures might vary both between and within participants, despite the fact that they achieved 
3 
equivalent levels of success in the task. Specifically, QE (Vickers, 1996), visual search 
4 
patterns (Savelsbergh et al., 2010) and percentage viewing time on fixation locations (Dicks 
5 
et al., 2010) will be analysed. 
6 
Method 
7 
Participants 
8 
Five experienced association football goalkeepers participated in the experiment (M 
9 
age = 24.2 years, SD = 4.7). These five goalkeepers were selected as they saved at least 10 
10 
penalty kicks in the study of Dicks et al. (2010). Specifically, in the original study, 
11 
goalkeeping performance was analysed for 15 trials, with kicks directed towards each side of 
12 
the goal (eight to the right and seven to the left). Participants faced five additional trials 
13 
distributed to varying goal locations with the aim of masking awareness of the task 
14 
procedure. Goalkeepers had the following percentage success rates on the basis of the 15 
15 
kicks faced in the original study: P1 = 86.7%; P2 = 86.7%; P3 = 80.0%; P4 = 93.3%; and P5 
16 
= 93.3%. As recognised by Dicks et al. (2010), these response accuracies are substantially 
17 
higher than those in competition and reflective of the decision to include only non-deception 
18 
trials with the aim of regulating variability in the penalty taker’s kicking action (see Schorer 
19 
Baker, Fath, & Jaitner, 2017). Following previous literature (Piras & Vickers, 2011), gaze 
20 
data were calculated on the basis of the first 10 saves. All had played to at least the level of 
21 
the New Zealand Southern Premier League, with an average of 12 years (SD = 5.4) 
22 
competitive experience. One penalty taker aged 24 years, who was matched to the 
23 
goalkeepers by performance standard and length of experience (cf. Panchuk & Vickers, 
24 
2006), was recruited to execute all kicks. Prior to testing and contacting participants, ethical 
25 

--- [Página 9] ---
clearance was obtained from the local University ethics committee. All players provided 
1 
written consent prior to participation in the study. 
2 
 
3 
Procedure and Apparatus 
4 
The procedure and apparatus are as reported in the original study of Dicks et al. 
5 
(2010) from which the current data set is derived. Specifically, the penalty kick data are from 
6 
the in situ interception condition. In this condition, the penalty taker followed a script which 
7 
included information about which part of the goal to aim each kick. The player was instructed 
8 
to use a non-deception strategy in order to minimize any variability in his kicking action and 
9 
initiated the run-up at an approach angle of between 10 and 30°, 4.0m from ball contact for 
10 
each trial. Penalty kicks were executed using a regulation size 5 football in an indoor 
11 
Astroturf facility at a full-size goal (7.32 x 2.44m) represented by a white screen marked with 
12 
six target areas (0.81 x 1.50m). Movements were recorded using a high-speed 100 Hz digital 
13 
video camera (JVC GRDVL9800), placed 1.5m horizontal to the penalty spot facing the goal. 
14 
To enable assessment of QE, goalkeeper movements were subjected to frame-by-frame 
15 
analysis relative to illumination of the LED array triggered during the penalty taker’s 
16 
approach. 
17 
 
18 
Measurement of Gaze Behaviours 
19 
A mobile eye-tracking system (MobileEye™, ASL Ltd, Massachusetts, USA) was 
20 
used to record gaze behaviours. Gaze behaviour data were collected at a rate of 25 frames per 
21 
second and subjected to a frame-by-frame analysis following testing using Focus X2 (Elite 
22 
Sports Analysis, Fife, United Kingdom). The scene video was recorded and captured for 
23 
offline analysis.

Data Analysis 
1 
The analysis started at 2000 ms prior to foot-ball contact which included the run-up 
2 
and a portion of the penalty taker’s preparation time to provide sufficient duration before 
3 
penalty kick initiation (Panchuk & Vickers, 2006). As the aim of the study was to conduct 
4 
exploratory analysis of individual differences in goalkeeping performance across each 
5 
dependent measure, data were not averaged across participants and therefore differences 
6 
between participants are described using descriptive statistics (see Chia, Chow, Kawabata, 
7 
Dicks, & Lee, 2017). 
8 
Percentage viewing time. Ten fixation locations were used to categorize position of 
9 
gaze: the penalty taker’s head, upper body (including arms), upper kicking leg and hip, upper 
10 
non-kicking leg and hip, kicking leg (including foot), non-kicking leg (including foot), turf 
11 
between the player and ball, the ball, the turf in front of the ball, and “other”. The “other” 
12 
category was used when gaze could not be coded due to extraneous jarring movements by the 
13 
participant, or when gaze was directed outside of the fixation location categories. 
14 
Visual search patterns. Gaze behaviours were analysed following the procedures of 
15 
Savelsbergh, et al. (2010) in order to identify the visual search patterns utilised by each 
16 
participant for each trial in each condition. The gaze patterns in each individual trial were 
17 
qualitatively matched to one of seven different global categories (Figure 1), which were the 
18 
same as those developed by Savelsbergh et al. (2010). Code–recode reliability ranged 
19 
between r = .87 for an independent coder and r =.98 for the same experimenter.

Results 
17 
Percentage Viewing Time 
18 
The percentage viewing time results revealed that the most fixated location consistent 
19 
across all participants, with the exception of P2, was the ball (Figure 2). However, despite 
20 
this being the most fixated location, there were variations between goalkeepers P1, P3, P4, 
21 
and P5; P3 and P5 tended to fixate primarily on the head and ball above all other locations, 
22 
whereas P1 and P4 fixated the lower kicking leg after the ball as well as some fixations on 
23 
the head and other body locations. The distribution of fixations across different body 
24 
locations was more reflective of P2 who oriented gaze towards all body locations as well as 
25 

--- [Página 12] ---
the ball. Moreover, there was also the observation of relatively large standard deviations in 
1 
P2’s data suggesting that there was intra-individual variability between trials, something that 
2 
was also a general trend across the other participants. Finally, although a relatively small 
3 
duration, goalkeepers P1 and P5 spent a proportion of time looking at the visual anchor 
4 
location between the player and ball (Piras & Vickers, 2011), whilst P2, P3, and P4 spent 
5 
comparatively more time fixating the turf (in front of the ball), suggesting a pattern whereby 
6 
the fovea “lay in wait‟ for ball-flight (cf. Land & McLeod, 2000).

Discussion 
13 
The aim of the current analysis was to examine individual differences in gaze 
14 
behaviour of five experienced goalkeepers each of whom saved at least ten penalty kicks 
15 
during a previously published experiment (Dicks et al., 2010). The individual-level analyses 
16 
showed that, rather than participants converging on the same gaze behaviours, there were 
17 
some discrepancies between and within participants in the dependent variables measured, 
18 
most notably QE duration but also percentage viewing time and visual search patterns. This 
19 
analysis builds on findings from past work, which have also indicated variability between 
20 
equally-skilled participants and from to trial for the same participant during skilled 
21 
interceptive actions (Croft et al., 2010; Navia et al., 2017). In the following discussion, the 
22 
implications of these findings are discussed in relation to extant gaze behaviour literature 
23 
before considering implications for future research. 
24 

--- [Página 14] ---
The current analysis revealed that, perhaps above the other measures considered, the 
1 
visual search categories of Savelsbergh et al. (2010) best captured some of the invariant 
2 
features of gaze patterns between participants. In particular, there was a general indication 
3 
that participants tended to utilise variations on visual search patterns 5 and 6 (Figure 1), 
4 
during which, fixations began at the head/upper body followed by a fixation near the ball area 
5 
(pattern 5) or fixations were distributed at the head/upper body followed by a fixation near 
6 
the lower-leg/ball area then a return fixation on the head/upper body before a fixation near 
7 
the ball (pattern 6). Further to the current findings, this mode of analysis has permitted 
8 
evaluation of the efficacy of perceptual training methods in previous research (Savelsbergh et 
9 
al., 2010). However, the classification procedure remains subjective and arguably lacks the 
10 
finite precision that could accurately differentiate between critical timings of information 
11 
pick-up (Navia et al., 2017). For instance, the visual search categories do not presently 
12 
differentiate gaze patterns such as a visual anchor location between the player and ball (Piras 
13 
& Vickers, 2011) or a fixation ahead of the ball (Land & McLeod, 2000; Mann et al., 2013), 
14 
both of which have been suggested critical to the performance of interceptive actions. 
15 
Unlike the search pattern measure, the percentage viewing time data (Figure 2) 
16 
enabled the identification of critical gaze locations, however, as used in the current analysis, 
17 
this measure did not provide understanding on the timing of gaze patterns. Thus, although 
18 
this measure revealed that the ball was the most fixated location consistent across all 
19 
participants with the exception of P2, this method of analysis does not detail on when this 
20 
location was fixated. Beyond the observation that the ball was the most fixated location, there 
21 
was variation between and within goalkeepers for percentage viewing time in line with the 
22 
results of Navia et al. (2017) who revealed that there was variability between participants for 
23 
this dependent measure. That is, across the duration of the trials, participants attended to 
24 
different gaze locations when successfully saving penalty kicks. Furthermore, assuming that 
25 

--- [Página 15] ---
the penalty taker is not attempting to deceive during the run-up as was the case in this study, 
1 
it is recognised that the kinematics of the kicker may provide information on time to contact 
2 
(when the kicker will make contact with the ball) rather than on kick direction (Diaz et al., 
3 
2012; Lopes et al., 2014). Thus, it is possible that variation in fixation locations could be 
4 
present because multiple variables may provide useful information on time to contact (van 
5 
der Kamp, Savelsbergh, & Smeets, 1997). Overall, the results of this measure imply the 
6 
duration of fixations at certain locations may not be what differentiates between successful 
7 
and unsuccessful performance, rather, it might be that looking at the right time at the right 
8 
place is most critical for successful performance. 
9 
In addition to the observed between-participant variations, results were also 
10 
characterised by intra-individual differences. That is, goalkeepers varied in the amount of 
11 
time spent fixating different locations when successfully saving penalty kicks (see also, Croft 
12 
et al., 2010). The observation of intra-individual differences was particularly evident in QE 
13 
duration (see Figure 4). Previous research (e.g., Panchuk & Vickers, 2006; 2009; Piras & 
14 
Vickers, 2011) has suggested that longer QE durations are associated with more successful 
15 
performance during anticipation tasks. However, the current results revealed that each 
16 
participant utilised a range of QE durations during successful performance. For example, the 
17 
QE of Participant 4 during one successful trial was 1960 ms while a QE duration of 160 ms 
18 
was recorded on a separate successful trial for the same participant. Similarly, Participant 2 
19 
revealed QE durations ranging between 0 ms and 1520 ms. Thus, comparable to previous 
20 
research in aiming tasks including basketball (de Oliveira, Oudejans, & Beek, 2008) and ten21 
pin bowling (Chia et al., 2017), the present findings indicate that variation in QE durations 
22 
can occur without negative performance consequences. 
23 
Previous results of Piras and Vickers (2011) reported that the location of QE was 
24 
more important than the duration. Specifically, these authors reported that QE duration 
25 

--- [Página 16] ---
associated with a fixation on the ball led to less successful performance whereas a QE 
1 
duration associated with a visual anchor location (Williams & Davids, 1998) between the ball 
2 
and penalty taker led to more successful performance. In the current study, the location of QE 
3 
appeared to be more important than the duration but in contrast to Piras and Vickers (2011), 
4 
the majority of successful saves were characterised by a QE location on the ball and none 
5 
were characterised by use of the visual anchor. This finding is likely due to differences in the 
6 
kickers angle of approach with a narrower approach angle in the present study compared to 
7 
that of Piras and Vickers (2011). With a wider runup, there is space for a visual anchor 
8 
fixation between the kicker and ball right up until foot-ball contact. However, with a 
9 
straighter run up (< 30°), the horizontal distance between the kicker and ball is greatly 
10 
reduced early during the run up meaning there is no observable visual anchor location 
11 
between the ball and kicker. This explains why the QE location was almost exclusively 
12 
located on the ball during the present study. Future work might consider how differences in 
13 
angle of approach, and also run up duration, might affect the gaze patterns employed when 
14 
attempting to save penalty kicks. Such findings would provide insight into how goalkeepers 
15 
adapt gaze behaviour to different task constraints experienced when facing penalty kicks. 
16 
Interestingly, participant 2 tended to utilise the same two visual search strategies as 
17 
the other participants (patterns 5 and 6) but differed markedly on percentage viewing time. 
18 
This finding suggests that even within this same visual search pattern there, exists 
19 
considerable variation in fixation duration at each location and likely therefore, in the timing 
20 
of saccades from one location to the next. Furthermore, P2 appeared to explore different gaze 
21 
behaviours to a greater extent than the other participants as they utilized more QE locations 
22 
on successful trials than all other participants. This exploration did not negatively affect 
23 
performance suggesting this participant utilized a number of gaze behaviours to pick up the 
24 

--- [Página 17] ---
required information to successfully anticipate and intercept penalty kicks therefore 
1 
indicating the omission of a universal optimal gaze strategy (Dicks et al., 2017). 
2 
 
3 
Future directions 
4 
The current exploratory analysis suggested that experienced goalkeepers do not 
5 
converge on the same gaze patterns during successful anticipation performance (Navia et al., 
6 
2017). The data therefore indicated that multiple information-movement couplings can be 
7 
used by different performers when achieving successful performance outcomes during visual 
8 
anticipation (Dicks et al., 2017), although it is important to note further rigorous studies are 
9 
required to support this claim. Indeed, work is still required to establish why such differences 
10 
appear to have emerged. Specifically, whether the expressed variability characterises a better 
11 
ability to adapt to different situations or whether it is noise that limits performance (Dicks et 
12 
al., 2017). On the one hand, the observed results are comparable to observations in the 
13 
coordination literature, which demonstrate that there are different coordination solutions that 
14 
can be utilised by performers in order to achieve success within the same performance 
15 
context (e.g., Chow, Davids, Button, & Koh, 2008; Hong & Newell, 2006). Thus, variation in 
16 
gaze patterns may provide performers with the flexibility to utilise different information17 
movement couplings in order to adapt to the variable coordination patterns utilised by skilled 
18 
opponents during fast-ball sports (see Schorer et al., 2007). Furthermore, it is possible that 
19 
the differences in gaze behaviours may be a reflection of changes in movement patterns 
20 
between goalkeepers. To our knowledge, there have thus far been limited attempts to 
21 
integrate movement and gaze measures to fully understand how gaze and movement patterns 
22 
are coordinated together, and therefore, this remains a research priority in future work. 
23 
Whilst an in-depth study of the relationship between gaze behaviour and movement 
24 
variability will further current empirical understanding, such empirical endeavour would also 
25 

--- [Página 18] ---
require the development of novel measures. Adopting a more individualized analysis 
1 
approach, rather than conventional group based averaging methods, has the promise to 
2 
further understanding of expert gaze control, and comparable to perspectives in the 
3 
coordination literature over two decades ago (e.g., Bates, 1996), single-subject methodology 
4 
can provide important evidence for the development of theoretical and applied perspectives. 
5 
Thus, a fruitful avenue for future research would be to apply methods of analysis including 
6 
cluster approaches (Chow, Davids, Button, & Rein, 2008; Seifert, Leblanc, Herault, Komar, 
7 
Button, & Chollet, 2011) and neural networks (Memmert & Perl, 2009) in order to gain a 
8 
greater understanding of the high dimensionality of the gaze behaviour datasets. Future 
9 
research could also consider employing multiple regression analysis to identify which gaze 
10 
variable(s) best predict anticipation success (e.g. Le Runigo, Benguigui, & Bardy, 2010; 
11 
Mallek, Benguigui, Dicks, & Thouvarecq, 2017). As an example, using the penalty kick, a 
12 
regression model could be run with QE duration, QE location, the different search patterns 
13 
used, and percentage viewing time to identify a model for the gaze variables that best predicts 
14 
goalkeeper save success. However, researchers must be mindful of the guidelines on sample 
15 
size in order to run this analysis (Darlington & Hayes, 2016). The importance of developing 
16 
knowledge in this area will have implications for developing expertise in sport and 
17 
developmental contexts. 
18 
 
19 
Conclusion 
20 
This article has revealed evidence of variation in gaze behaviour both between and 
21 
within goalkeepers for the successful anticipation and interception of penalty kicks. This 
22 
finding is comparable to the variation in motor control observed in the coordination literature. 
23 
Yet, there are still significant grounds to be made in understanding to what extent variability 
24 
is a characteristic of successful expert performance and why such variation is evident. 
25 

--- [Página 19] ---
Notably, a fruitful endeavour for future work is to examine how vision is used during the 
1 
control of movement. That is, combining motion capture and eye tracking offers the ability to 
2 
measure participants gaze and movement coordination simultaneously in representative tasks 
3 
in order to determine a comprehensive understanding of experts successful anticipation. Such 
4 
advancements promise to bring implications for the understanding and development of 
5 
sporting expertise.
