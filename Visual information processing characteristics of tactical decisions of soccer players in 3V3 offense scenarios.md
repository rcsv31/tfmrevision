Abstract
Objective  To quantitatively access the visual search of soccer players in the 3V3 offensive tactical situation and 
explore the eye movement characteristics and information processing mechanisms of high-level athletes under 
multi-target tracking.
Methods  Using the expert-novice research paradigm, eight second-level soccer players were selected as the 
expert group, and eight university students majoring in soccer were selected as the novice group. Real match 3V3 
offensive tactical scenarios were used as stimulus materials. The Dikablis Professional head-mounted eye tracker with 
D-lab software was used to record and process the participants’ eye movement data and decision-making agility 
data. Experts were invited to independently score the reasonableness of offensive tactical decision-making data. 
Independent samples t-tests were conducted for intergroup comparison analysis on SPSS 25.0 software.
Results  The tactical decision-making agility and rationality of the expert group were significantly higher than those 
of the novice group (P < 0.01). The reason for this difference in behavior is that the expert group took more fixations 
and saccades to deeply visually process the position and size of the gap area. At the same time, to gain opportunities 
to score goals, they prioritized the extraction and processing of information from the far offensive player area, which is 
closer to the goal. They used a following fixation mode to search for both offensive and defensive players, repeatedly 
observing the positional characteristics and changes in the gaps created by both sides. They used fewer fixations 
for visual search of the goal area and defensive player area, forming a visual allocation principle of far offensive 
players > near offensive players > gap > marking players > pressing players > covering players > goal.
Conclusion  The expert group can effectively balance decision-making agility and tactical rationality, effectively 
identify key information, deeply process the extracted information, and adopt different search strategies to handle 
key and non-key information, forming an economical and effective visual allocation principle to complete efficient 
perceptual prediction.

Introduction
Vision is the most important sense for humans and the 
primary means of obtaining information from the out­
side world [1]. During movement, the body continuously 
receives external information, which is processed and 
analyzed by the brain, allowing for timely feedback and 
adjustment of one’s own movements [2]. Visual search 
refers to the process in which the brain selectively ana­
lyzes and processes the characteristic information of 
external objects and makes reasonable decisions [3, 4]. 
This process includes three stages: information input, 
information processing, and instruction output [5, 6]. 
Information input mainly refers to the quality of visual 
information recorded; the speed of information process­
ing is determined by the complexity and volume of the 
recorded information, as well as the body’s memory stor­
age capacity; instruction output is reflected in the agility 
and rationality of decision-making [5, 6].
The development of eye-tracking technology provides 
a methodological basis for the immediate measurement 
of athletes’ anticipatory decision-making. This technol­
ogy can more accurately, objectively, and scientifically 
reflect the information search, processing, and handling 
mechanisms of athletes in sports scenarios, which has 
facilitated the development of specialized cognitive the­
ories in the field of elite sports [7]. Sport psychologists 
have made numerous studies on the relationship between 
visual search characteristics and sport performance in 
athletes, and have concluded that high-level athletes have 
an efficient perceptual prediction advantage in differ­
ent decision-making task scenarios [8–10]. The sources, 
characteristics, and mechanisms of this decision-making 
advantage of high-level athletes have more particularly 
been a hot research topic.
In soccer matches, athletes must eliminate all distrac­
tions to focus on the competitive tasks, acquire effective 
information in constantly changing scenarios, process it, 
and make reasonable decisions [11]. Visual search tasks 
are divided into single-target (penalty kicks, corner kicks) 
and multi-target (offensive tactics, defensive tactics) 
based on the number of visual search targets for soccer 
players [12, 13]. Single-target tasks refer to sports scenar­
ios with fewer external stimuli, where athletes have a nar­
rower field of vision, making it easier to predict and make 
decisions [13]. In the decision-making phase of visual 
search, athletes often exhibit prolonged attention to a key 
piece of information [13]. Many studies on goalkeepers 
[14–19] have found that the expert group has a higher 
accuracy rate in judging the direction of penalty kicks. 
The main reason is that they adopt a more economical 
and effective visual search strategy, mainly tending to 
focus on the positions of the penalty taker’s feet and torso 
when kicking the ball. Research on single-target tasks in 
soccer is already very mature, and a set of eye movement 
theoretical mechanisms for visual information processing 
has been essentially formed.
Due to the higher proportion of continuous match 
scenarios in soccer, the demand for multi-target visual 
search is more prominent. In this context, it is reasonable 
to study the multi-target visual search strategies of soc­
cer players. Tactical decisions occupy a central position 
in soccer matches and directly affect the overall perfor­
mance and outcome of the game. In most decision-mak­
ing scenarios in soccer matches, athletes must process 
the identity characteristics, spatial positions, movement 
trajectories, and speeds of players from both sides, as 
well as information about the ball, in order to control the 
ball [11]. They also need to predict the changes in implicit 
information such as gaps, which is a typical example of 
multiple object tracking [11]. To make reasonable deci­
sions in the sports scenario, athletes must track, extract, 
and process multiple targets on the field. The high speed 
of modern soccer offensive is the most critical factor in 
gaining time and space advantages, and quickly complet­
ing various offensive technical and tactical movements is 
the basic guarantee for exerting offensive power, breaking 
through the defense line, and scoring goals [20].
Previous research on the visual search advantages of 
soccer players has mainly focused on local and global 
offensive and defensive confrontations. In terms of local 
confrontations, Williams [21] conducted eye-tracking 
experiments with experienced and inexperienced soccer 
players, finding that experienced players tend to focus on 
the ball, the player in control of the ball, and the positions 
of other players, while inexperienced players often over­
look the positions of other players. Shi et al. [22] explored 
the characteristics of visual information processing in 
expert soccer players during 3V2 offensive tactical deci­
sion-making scenarios, discovering that the expert group 
had fewer fixations on open spaces, nearby offensive 
players, and distant defensive players. In terms of global 
offensive and defensive confrontations, relevant studies 
[24, 25] found that players with higher creativity or tac­
tical efficiency have a wider range of fixation points and 
process key information with shorter fixation durations. 
Additionally, Dambroz et al. [26] compared the visual 
search strategies of soccer players in different positions, 
finding that forwards have significantly fewer fixations 
and shorter fixation durations on the ball and teammates 
than midfielders. However, a systematic review and 
meta-analysis of visual search in ball sports athletes by 
Silva et al. [23] suggests that there is a high degree of het­
erogeneity among included studies, leading to variability 
in research results. Therefore, the related studies are not 
consistent and require further exploration.
The concept of 3V3 offensive tactics in soccer refers to a 
scenario in a soccer match where three attacking players 
work together and move strategically to break through

the defense of three defenders, creating scoring oppor­
tunities. This tactic emphasizes quick passing, individual 
technique, teamwork, and rapid decision-making and 
execution within a small area [27]. It meets the demands 
of modern soccer for speed, agility, and creativity, help­
ing players adapt to more complex tactical systems [27]. 
Currently, there is no research specifically targeting the 
visual search strategies of soccer players in 3V3 offen­
sive tactical decision-making scenarios. The 3V3 tactic is 
extremely important in the entire soccer tactical system 
and serves as the foundation for overall soccer offensive 
and defensive confrontations. Therefore, this study con­
tinues the expert-novice paradigm, using 3V3 offensive 
tactical videos as stimuli, and combines tactical deci­
sion-making behavior indicators to quantify the athletes’ 
visual search. It aims to explore the eye movement char­
acteristics and processing mechanisms of expert athletes 
in offensive tactical multiple object tracking, in order to 
provide psychological guidance for specific cognitive 
training, tactical awareness cultivation, sports skill devel­
opment, and talent identification.
Methods
Participants
This study used G*Power 3.1.9.7 software to calculate the 
sample size. Based on the systematic review and metaanalysis by Zhang et al. [43], the effect sizes (ES) for 
reaction time (ES = 0.91) and accuracy (ES = 1.51) in the 
cross-sectional comparison between the expert group 
and the novice group were extracted. The sample size 
was calculated for a difference test comparing the means 
of two groups, with a significance level set at α = 0.05, a 
statistical power of 1-β = 0.80, and a ratio of sample sizes 
between groups set at 1:1. The calculation indicated that 
a total of 6 to 12 participants would be needed. A total 
of 16 participants were recruited in this study, which 
were divided into the expert group and the novice group 
based on their sports performance level and training 
experience.
The expert group consists of 8 male athletes who have 
participated in the U21 league. The teams these athletes 
belong to are registered in the Chinese University Foot­
ball Association (CUFA) Super League, have advanced to 
the top 16 of the national league, and won the champion­
ship of the high-level men’s group in the Liaoning Pro­
vincial University Football League. The average age of the 
expert group is 18.75 years old, all of whom are national 
first-level athletes with 8–11 years of training experience. 
The novice group was composed of male college students 
majoring in soccer from the School of Physical Educa­
tion at Liaoning Normal University, with an average age 
of 19.50 years and a training experience ranging from 1 to 
3 years. These players did not participate in provincial or 
national competitions, but only engaged in instructional 
and training-based intra-university matches. All partici­
pants were in good health, free from physiological and 
psychological diseases, not color blind or weak-sighted, 
and had vision or corrected vision of 5.0 or above. They 
participated in the experiment voluntarily and were in 
good mental condition on the day of the experiment. The 
study was conducted in accordance with the Declara­
tion of Helsinki and received approval from the Scientific 
Research Ethics Committee of Liaoning Normal Uni­
versity. In addition, all participants provided informed 
consent.
Experimental design
This study adopted the expert-novice paradigm, com­
pared the tactical decision-making performance and 
visual search quality of the expert group and the novice 
group of players, and summarized the visual informa­
tion processing characteristics of the expert group play­
ers. The 3V3 offensive scenario selected in this study 
includes the following organizational structures: Firstly, 
the offensive structure is composed of two forwards and 
a midfielder (i.e., 1–2 formation). The midfielder reflects 
the playmaking core since he often faces the defend­
ers. Based on the visual distance from the ball-holding 
player (usually the playmaking core), the offensive play­
ers can be divided into near and far players. Secondly, 
the defensive structure is composed of a pressing player, 
a man-marking player, and a covering player. The press­
ing player is located near the ball-holding player (that is, 
the playmaking core) and uses lateral movement to com­
press the offensive space. The marking player sticks to the 
offensive player (that is, the forward) and uses physical 
contact and position blocking to restrict him in receiving 
the ball. The covering player is responsible for filling the 
gaps left by the pressing and marking players. Moreover, 
the purpose of 3V3 offense is to create effective shots by 
constantly tearing apart the gaps with the players’ move­
ments, while the purpose of 3V3 defense is to prevent 
the offensive players from shooting effectively on target. 
Based on the 3V3 offensive scenario, this study divided 
the stimulus video into seven areas of interest (AOIs): 
the goal, the gap, the pressing player, the marking player, 
the covering player, the near offensive player, and the far 
offensive player.
This study takes tactical decision-making efficiency and 
eye movement indicators as dependent variables, among 
which tactical decision-making efficiency includes two 
indicators: decision agility and decision rationality. Both 
are indicators for assessing the speed and quality of deci­
sion-making, although decision agility is often related to 
the reaction time of decisions whereas decision ratio­
nality emphasizes the logic of the decision-making pro­
cess to ensure the selection of the best solution [22]. Eye 
movement indicators include five indicators: number of

fixations, fixation duration, number of saccades, fixation 
frequency, and fixation allocation. Specifically, number 
of fixations refers to the fixation counts within a certain 
time or within a specific AOI; fixation duration is the 
sum of the durations of all fixations within the area of 
interest, reflecting the time spent on visual information 
acquisition and processing in that area; number of sac­
cades refers to the number of saccades within a certain 
time; fixation frequency usually refers to the number of 
fixations per unit of time; fixation allocation involves the 
distribution of fixation points across different AOIs. In 
addition, the indicators representing temporal features 
of eye movements include number of fixations, fixation 
duration, and fixation frequency; the indicators repre­
senting spatial features of eye movements include num­
ber of saccades and fixation distribution.
Experimental instruments
This study uses the Dikablis Professional eye tracker pro­
duced by Ergoneers in Germany to record the eye move­
ment data of each participant, with a sampling rate of 
60 Hz and a tracking accuracy of 0.1°-0.3°. The Dikablis 
consisted of a light-weight head-unit and transmitter 
(weight: 69  g). The head-unit was double-sided taped 
to each participant’s forehead to prevent slippage. The 
dual-camera system consisted of a monocular infra-red 
eye-camera to track pupil blackness and a fish-eye fieldcamera to record the environment in front of the partici­
pant. The system was calibrated using the manufacturer’s 
four-point procedure for each participant. Calibration 
created a shared coordinate system relating the position 
of the pupil captured by the eye-camera with the gaze 
direction displayed on the field-of-view camera [28]. In 
addition, two Dell laptops were used for the experimen­
tal operations. One was installed with D-Lab software 
to connect to the eye tracker and record the partici­
pants’ viewing process, while the other was connected 
to a SHARP XG-D3080XA projector to play the stimulus 
materials and mark the decision routes.
The process of capturing the stimulus materials 
required to be conducted under good weather condi­
tions, with the soccer field free from large areas of build­
ings, tree shadows, and other interfering factors. The 
selected stimulus materials for the experiment were 3V3 
offensive tactical videos. All offensive tactical videos were 
taken from real scenes of the Chinese Super League. This 
study selected seven players participating in the Chinese 
College Football League to reproduce the 3V3 offensive 
scenario as described earlier with one goalkeeper pro­
tecting the defensive team’s goal. One of the offensive 
players held a Canon 6D camera with a 50 mm prime lens 
to shoot the offensive tactics video from a first-person 
perspective. The other six players, according to the roles 
they played, reproduced the scenario in combination 
with the positioning of the players in the original video. 
To ensure the accuracy of the research results and elimi­
nate potential learning effects, none of the above-men­
tioned seven players were included in the participants 
analyzed in this study. The original materials included 
nine video clips, with three offensive videos for the left, 
center, and right offensive pathways. This study shot a 
total of 27 reproduced videos, in addition to two videos 
used for the preliminary experiment. The scene of soccer 
offensive tactical decision-making is shown in Fig. 1.
Three experts (coaches of the Chinese College Football 
League) were invited to assess the degree of reproduction 
of the 27 videos and select the nine videos with the high­
est similarity to the original materials. In addition, Video 
Studio 12.0 software was used to edit the stimulus videos. 
Ultimately, the stimulus videos started from the initiation 
by the opposing defensive player or the offensive team 
and ended when all players had completed their move­
ments, with each video lasting 3 to 6 s. According to the 
results of the subsequent offensive tactical decision-mak­
ing performance (Table 1), it was found that the abovementioned stimulus videos could effectively distinguish 
between the expert group and the novice group, and thus 
had a high degree of differentiation.
Experimental procedures
(1) Experimental equipment connection and preparation. 
Connect the eye-camera responsible for tracking the eyes 
and the field-camera responsible for recording the scene 
to the main body of the eye tracker, and connect it to the 
computer equipped with D-Lab software to record the 
participants’ eye movement data and viewing process. 
Connect the projector to the computer responsible for 
playing the stimulus materials and project the materials 
onto a screen (approximately 2.5 m away from the par­
ticipant’s testing area) to record the participants’ decision 
routes. In addition, the D-lab software records the par­
ticipants’ decision reaction time in real time.
(2) Recording basic participant information. Partici­
pants were successively brought into the sports psychol­
ogy laboratory at Liaoning Normal University, and their 
basic information (age, level of sports, sports experience, 
and years of sports participation, etc.) was recorded.
(3) Inform the purpose and process of the experiment. 
To improve the accuracy of the experimental results and 
reduce experimental errors, inform the participants that 
the purpose of the experiment is to explore the visual 
search characteristics and information processing mech­
anisms of athletes, and also inform them that there is no 
right or wrong in the choices they make to alleviate con­
cerns. Explain the experimental process to the partici­
pants, focusing on explaining the operational tasks of the 
participants pressing buttons to play and pause the video

Data analysis
Drawing on previous studies [29, 30], this study invited 
five experts who have long been engaged in football 
teaching and training (coaches of the Chinese College 
Football League) to assess the decision-making path­
ways of the participants, thereby obtaining scores for the 
rationality of tactical decision-making. The experts were 
required to use a 5-point Likert scale to rate the threat 
level of the participants’ decision-making pathways to the 
goal, with higher scores indicating a higher threat level. 
The average score of all experts’ ratings was taken as the 
final score for the rationality of tactical decision-making. 
In addition, D-lab software was used to record the par­
ticipants’ data on indicators of tactical decision-making 
agility and visual search eye movement indicators, and 
the data were imported into Excel software for storage.
SPSS 25.0 software was used for data statistics and 
processing. This study employs the single-sample Kol­
mogorov-Smirnov test combined with P-P plots and Q-Q 
plots to assess the normality of the data, and the results 
indicate that the data are approximately normally distrib­
uted. Therefore, independent samples t-tests are used for 
subsequent intergroup comparison analyses. When con­
ducting independent samples t-tests, Levene’s test is used 
to assess the homogeneity of variances. If the variances 
are homogeneous, the analysis is conducted assuming 
equal variances; if the variances are not homogeneous, 
the analysis is conducted without assuming equal vari­
ances. In addition, with the help of the effect size calcu­

Cohen’s d = 0.514).

offensive tactical decision-making scenario (Table  4), 
it was found that the expert group had a significantly 
higher fixation frequency in the far offensive players area 
than the novice group (t = 2.930, P = 0.005 < 0.01, Cohen’s 
d = 0.780); whereas, there were no significant differences 
in fixation frequency in the other AOIs (P > 0.05).
Number of saccades
Comparing the number of saccades in the seven AOIs 
between the expert group and novice group in the 3V3 
offensive tactical decision-making scenario (Table  5), 
it was found that there were no significant differences 
in the number of saccades across all AOI between the 
expert group and novice group (P > 0.05).
Fixation allocation
From Fig. 2, it is evident that there are significant differ­
ences in fixation allocation between the expert group and 
novice group during the 3V3 offensive tactical decisionmaking task while watching videos. When ranking the 
different AOIs based on the concentration of fixation 
points, it is observed that the expert group’s fixation allo­
cation is as follows: far offensive players > near offensive 
players > gap > marking players > pressing players > cov­
ering players > goal; whereas the novice group’s fixation 
allocation is: near offensive players > far offensive play­
ers > covering players > marking players > pressing play­
ers > goal > gap. The proportion of fixation points from 
the expert group falling on the gap, marking players, and 
far offensive players areas is notably larger than that of 
the novice group, while the proportion on the goal, cov­
ering players, and near offensive players’ areas is signifi­
cantly smaller than that of the novice group.

performance of experts and their special visual search 
behaviors. Compared with the novice group, the expert 
group has a stronger ability to predict the competitive

The expert group demonstrates rapid, agile and ratio­
nally effective tactical decision-making, which mainly 
depends on the attributes of the expert group. In the 
field of competitive sports, experts and novices are dis­
tinguished based on their performance levels in competi­
tive matches [35]. Ericsson [36] suggests that only those 
individuals who have invested a significant amount of 
effort in preparation or training and can maintain a high 
level of performance in a particular field can be called 
experts. Williams et al. [37], Tashman [38], and Gobet 
[39] propose the hypothesis that experts’ excellent per­
formance is due to their solid athletic skills, rich reserves 
of declarative and theoretical knowledge in their sport, 
which makes the structure and sequence of information 
processing regular over time. Therefore, expert team 
players have more match experience and rich on-thespot experience, and a large amount of scenario informa­
tion and representational information are stored in their 
memory systems [38, 39]. Based on this, they construct 
several psychological models that match scene informa­
tion with tactical decisions, which greatly shorten the 
time for athletes to extract information during tacti­
cal decision-making tasks [40]. In addition, experts can 
instantly discern the effectiveness of information, focus­
ing on the search and extraction of key information and 
excluding the interference of non-key information [37, 
40]. In summary, experts establish a complete “effective 
visual search, information extraction and processing, tac­
tical decision-making response” information processing 
mechanism, which effectively shortens decision-making 
response time and improves the rationality of tactical 
decision-making. Novices, on the other hand, has incom­
plete information processing mechanism, with lower 
visual search effectiveness, slower depth and speed of 
information extraction and processing, leading to poorer 
agility and rationality in tactical decision-making.
In soccer, the speed of attack and defense transitions 
is fast, and the game environment is unpredictable and 
complex [41]. To make correct decisions in the constantly 
changing scenarios within a short time, athletes need to 
quickly and accurately judge and process through visual 
search, grasp effective information, and conduct in-depth 
processing [22, 29]. Some studies [22, 29] have shown 
that analyzing athletes’ visual search characteristics solely 
from eye movement indicators is unconvincing. It is nec­
essary to combine visual search tasks with sport-specific 
features that can reflect the cognitive processing advan­
tages of the expert group to comprehensively evaluate 
athletes’ visual search characteristics. Therefore, this 
study explores the visual search characteristics of out­
standing athletes from both temporal and spatial aspects 
of eye movements.

The temporal dimension of eye movements essentially 
represents the embodied characterization of visual infor­
mation processing efficiency [42], primarily reflected 
through the number of fixations, fixation frequency, and 
fixation duration. As the basic unit of visual information 
input, fixation behavior reflects the brain’s selective atten­
tion pattern to environmental stimuli [43]. The expert 
group invested more fixation counts in gaps and far 
offensive player, with relatively higher fixation frequency 
on distant attacking players. This result is similar to those 
of previous studies [24, 29]. These aforementioned stud­
ies all indicate that the continuously changing gaps torn 
by offensive and defensive players are the focus of atten­
tion for elite players. Higher fixation frequency means 
athletes acquire more information per second and pro­
cess it faster [22]. The expert group’s higher fixation fre­
quency on far offensive players reflects the informational 
complexity of this area. Far offensive players are relatively 
closer to the goal. To ensure the effectiveness of attacks, 
the expert group tends to pass the ball to offensive play­
ers closer to the goal. Therefore, they also pay close atten­
tion to the running positions of far offensive players. In 
summary, according to the Cognitive Resource Alloca­
tion Theory [44] and the Bounded Rationality DecisionMaking Model [45], the expert group can preferentially 
capture cues matching tactical schemas through auto­
matic processing, while the scattered fixations of the nov­
ice group reflect the inefficiency of controlled processing. 
In addition, it is generally believed that the longer the fix­
ation durations, the deeper the level of visual information 
processing [46]. The long-duration fixation of the expert 
group in key areas such as the gap and far offensive play­
ers is not due to difficulties in information extraction, but 
rather through pattern recognition for in-depth semantic 
encoding, converting visual signals into tactical rules. On 
the contrary, the novice group may process information 
through large-scale saccades for the disordered integra­
tion of multi-source cues. Their gaze points are scattered, 
and they are unable to effectively distinguish between key 
and non-key information, resulting in a lower level of tac­
tical decision-making.
The spatial distribution of eye movements reflects ath­
letes’ visual search strategies, which essentially represent 
adaptive choices for cognitive economy under time pres­
sure. As a bridge for gaze point shifts, saccadic behavior 
and its frequency reflect the density and strategy of infor­
mation sampling [47]. According to the environmental 
coupling theory [48], the expert group’s high-frequency 
saccades in the space and distant offensive player areas 
demonstrate real-time coupling with dynamic offensivedefensive relationships. Through a cyclic mechanism of 
“fixation-saccade-re-fixation”, they continuously update 
the input parameters for tactical decisions. In contrast, 
novice athletes mostly adopt a large-scale scanning visual 
search strategy. This strategy causes players to continu­
ously receive stimuli from the rich information gener­
ated by the movement of offensive and defensive players, 
resulting in no significant difference in the number of 
saccades compared with the expert group. However, due 
to gaps in on-site experience and specialized cognitive 
abilities, novices have insufficient information extrac­
tion and poor visual information integration capabilities, 
leading to lower rationality in tactical decision-making.
Fixation allocation refers to the percentage of the total 
time an athlete spends in a certain AOI in the total dura­
tion of watching a stimulus video material. To a certain 
extent, it reflects the time characteristics of athletes 
in spatial positions. This study results show that the 
fixation allocation order of expert group athletes is far 
offensive player > near offensive player > gap > marking 
player > pressing player > covering defender > goal; the fix­
ation allocation of novice group athletes is near offensive 
player > far offensive player > covering defender > marking 
player > pressing player > goal > gap. The gap area is the 
key information source that causes the visual search dif­
ference between the expert group and the novice group. 
The expert group uses more fixations and longer fixation 
duration in the gap area to extract effective information 
and deeply process this area to improve the agility and 
rationality of predictive tactical decisions. In addition, the 
expert group is more inclined to allocate more fixations 
to the far offensive player, while the novice group is more 
inclined to allocate more fixations to the near offensive 
player. To strive for an effective shooting opportunity, the 
expert group is more inclined to give priority to fixating 
on the offensive player closer to the goal. In the stimu­
lus material, from the start of video playback to the end, 
the body shape of the near offensive player is obviously 
larger than that of the far offensive player, and relatively 
larger objects are more likely to attract attention. There­
fore, they cannot effectively identify key information or 
identify key information at the wrong time, resulting in 
tactical decision-making deviations. In summary, the 
gaze sequence of the expert group conforms to the goaldirected selection mechanism, which is, constructing a 
visual priority map based on tactical goals (such as cre­
ating shooting opportunities). In contrast, the phenom­
enon of near-side player priority in the novice group is 
constrained by stimulus-driven attention, namely being 
captured by objects with high visual salience (such as 
larger-sized near-side players), which reflects the limita­
tions of bottom-up processing.

sive tactical decision-making among elite soccer play­
ers, which has guiding significance for the theory and 
practice of soccer tactical decision-making. Firstly, the

The limitations of this study and future prospects
Although this study has achieved positive results, there 
are still the following limitations. Firstly, the ecologi­
cal validity of the stimulus materials used in this study is 
relatively low. This study employed two-dimensional (2D) 
dynamic videos as stimulus materials, but 2D videos lack 
depth cues and specific contextual demands in real com­
petition scenarios, leading to a certain gap from real-life 
situations. It is recommended that future research select 
virtual reality scene materials with higher reliability and 
validity, or conduct research in real competition contexts, 
to obtain visual search behaviors with greater ecologi­
cal validity. Secondly, the study cannot present the gaze 
trajectory of dynamic stimuli, making it difficult to bet­
ter summarize the spatiotemporal trajectory patterns of 
athletes’ eye movements. It is suggested that follow-up 
studies introduce indicators to display gaze trajectories, 
so as to clearly demonstrate the spatiotemporal trajec­
tories of athletes’ visual search. Thirdly, the decisionmaking scenarios for 3V3 offensive tactics are diverse, 
and a most representative scenario is selected for inves­
tigation. In addition, the 3V3 offensive tactical scenario 
is a classic one in the 11V11 football attack and defense 
confrontation. Although there may still be some differ­
ences between the 3V3 scenario and the 11V11 attack 
and defense confrontation scenario in football, the visual 
search strategies of athletes in the 3V3 scenario still have 
reference significance for the visual search strategies in 
the 11V11 attack and defense confrontation. It is recom­
mended that follow-up studies enrich the decision-mak­
ing scenarios to gain a deeper understanding of athletes’ 
visual search and information processing processes in 
more decision-making contexts, thereby promoting the 
continuous development of research in this field.
Conclusion
The study of visual search characteristics in 3V3 offen­
sive tactical decision-making among soccer players of 
different skill levels revealed that the expert group dem­
onstrated agile and rational tactical decision-making 
behaviors. The reason is that the expert group can more 
effectively select and attend to relevant information and 
deeply process scene information to form efficient per­
ceptual predictions. Expert athletes engage in more 
visual processing of the position and size of gaps, and to 
gain opportunities to score goals, they prioritize attend­
ing to and processing information about the far offensive 
players who are closer to the goal. Additionally, they use 
a tracking fixation pattern to search for offensive and 
defensive players, repeatedly observing the positional 
characteristics and changes in the gaps created by the 
players, and use fewer fixations to visually search the goal 
area and defensive player areas, forming an economical 
and effective principle of visual allocation. By measuring 
the visual search characteristics of expert athletes in the 
3V3 offensive tactical decision-making context, the study 
provides theoretical guidance and practical significance 
for cultivating tactical awareness based on the visual 
search strategies and cognitive processing abilities of out­
standing athletes. However, due to the lack of research 
methods and tools, it is not possible to present the gaze 
trajectory of dynamic stimuli to better summarize the 
spatiotemporal trajectory patterns of eye movements. It 
is hoped that future research will delve deeper into this 
area.
