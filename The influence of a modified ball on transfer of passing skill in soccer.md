Abstract 
18 
Objectives: Equipment is frequently modified to promote skill learning in sport. However, it 
19 
is unclear whether skills learned using modified equipment transfer to the criterion task. This 
20 
study examined the transfer of passing skill from practicing with a futsal ball to performing 
21 
with a soccer ball, and the perceptual skill underlying the process. 
22 
Methods: 24 adult novices (n=18 females and n=6 males, 24 ± 4.8 years old) were divided 
23 
into an experimental (FUT) and a control group (SOC). The two groups practiced the same 
24 
passing skill in response to video stimuli across 3 sessions, the FUT group used a futsal ball 
25 
and SOC group used a soccer ball. Passing performance and gaze behaviour were assessed 
26 
pre- and post-intervention using a soccer ball in both groups to evaluate transfer. 
27 
Results: FUT showed greater pre- to post-test improvement (Effect Size (ES) = 2.06 ± 0.86) 
28 
in passing performance than SOC (ES = 1.03 ± 0.82), and higher passing performance in a 
29 
time-constrained scenario in the post-test (ES = 1.83 ± 1.07). The higher passing performance 
30 
in FUT was underpinned by changes in gaze behaviour. FUT increased the number of 
31 
fixation alternations between the ball and other locations and changed the cues learners 
32 
focused their attention on, while SOC only slightly modified their gaze behaviour. 
33 
Conclusions: This study showed that modified equipment – futsal ball – shaped the 
34 
development of a behavioural repertoire that positively transferred to other equipment – 
35 
soccer ball – improving learning of a perceptual-motor skill. Practitioners working in soccer 
36 
are encouraged to use a futsal ball in their training sessions to fast-track learning, particularly 
37 
in novices.

Introduction 
43 
The characteristics of equipment, such as ball compression and bat dimensions can be 
44 
modified to simplify the execution of sport skills in children or in novices, potentially fast45 
tracking the learning process (Araújo, Davids, Bennett, Button, & Chapman, 2004; Farrow, 
46 
Buszard, Reid, & Masters, 2016). Buszard, Reid, Masters, and Farrow (2016b) recently 
47 
illustrated in their systematic review the psychological, biomechanical, cognitive, and skill 
48 
performance factors that can be promoted using modified equipment, and also highlighted a 
49 
lack of transfer studies which limits the current understanding of how modified equipment 
50 
influences skill learning. Furthermore, previous research has mainly assessed the physical 
51 
aspects of performance, e.g., number of skill executions (Farrow & Reid, 2010) and fluency 
52 
of movement (Buszard, Reid, Masters, & Farrow, 2016a), while the perceptual side of 
53 
performance has remained relatively un-explored. As such, it is currently unclear whether 
54 
skills learned with modified equipment transfer to tasks that employ other equipment (e.g., 
55 
standard equipment) and how the underpinning perceptual skill is affected. 
56 
Skilled behaviour emerges from the coupling of perception and action under the 
57 
interaction of organismic (e.g., action capabilities and intentions), environmental (i.e., 
58 
features of the environment), and task (e.g., equipment) constraints (Araújo & Davids, 2011; 
59 
Kelso, 1995; Newell, 1986). Opportunities for action (i.e., affordances) emerge from the the 
60 
performer-environment interaction (Gibson, 1979; Newell, 1991). The perception of 
61 
information specifying affordances regulates decision-making and the self-organisation of 
62 
coordination patterns (Araújo, Davids, & Hristovski, 2006; Davids, Button, & Bennett, 
63 
2008b). In this sense, perception, cognition and action are intertwined processes that underpin 
64 
an individual’s skill (Araújo et al., 2006; Araújo, Hristovski, Seifert, Carvalho, & Davids, 
65 
2017). 
66 

--- [Página 6] ---
5 
 
Transfer of skill in this context, refers to how previous exposure to a particular set of 
67 
interacting constraints influences task performance under different constraints (Newell, 1996; 
68 
Rosalie & Müller, 2012; Seifert et al., 2016). Skill transfer is evaluated on performance 
69 
achievement (i.e., the degree of success when performing a task; Araújo & Davids, 2015), 
70 
and is considered positive when previous practice leads to a performance improvement under 
71 
a new set of interacting constraints (Carroll, Riek, & Carson, 2001). Positive transfer occurs 
72 
due to the due to the similarity between a practiced behaviour and the functional perception73 
action coupling required in a new task (transfer) to achieve the task goal (Newell, 1996; 
74 
Pacheco & Newell, 2015). 
75 
A modification to equipment, which is a task constraint, influences how perception, 
76 
cognition, and action emerge (Araújo et al., 2004). Practicing with a piece of equipment 
77 
shapes how individuals educate their attention towards the information that specifies 
78 
affordances, and how they self-organise coordination patterns (Davids et al., 2008b). The 
79 
skill learned with a specific piece of equipment can positively transfer if the developed 
80 
behaviour cooperates with the new task constraints (e.g., different equipment) (Kelso & 
81 
Zanone, 2002; Zanone & Kelso, 1997). Put simply, an equipment modification that facilitates 
82 
perception (cognition) and action coupling in a new task promotes transfer. Similarity of the 
83 
information that guides action between the learning and transfer tasks promotes the transfer 
84 
process (Pinder, Davids, Renshaw, & Araújo, 2011; Snapp-Childs, Wilson, & Bingham, 
85 
2015). The influence of equipment on the transfer of perception and action is relatively un86 
explored in the human movement field; the current study examined how modifications to 
87 
equipment influence the transfer of passing skill from a futsal ball to a soccer ball. 
88 
The passing skill in soccer is a complex perceptual-motor skill that involves making a 
89 
decision, on who to pass a ball to, and kicking the ball towards a teammate (Oppici, Panchuk, 
90 
Serpiello, & Farrow, 2017). Perception, decision-making and the passing action are 
91 

--- [Página 7] ---
6 
 
intertwined and emerge from the interaction of a passer with their environment and the 
92 
characteristics of the task. A successful pass entails perceiving information specifying 
93 
affordances (e.g., distances and angles between players; Travassos et al., 2012) and 
94 
organising a functional kicking action to successfully kick the ball to the intended player. The 
95 
analysis of eye movements can be used for examining attentional processes that underpin 
96 
passing, due to the partial-interdependence of attention and eye movements (Dicks, Button, & 
97 
Davids, 2010; Van Gompel, Fischer, Murray, & Hill, 2007). Gaze patterns provide 
98 
information on individuals’ attunement to environmental information, and provide insights 
99 
into changes in perceptual skills (Panchuk, Vine, & Vickers, 2015). For example, previous 
100 
research has showed that frequent switches of attention between the ball and players 
101 
underpinned successful passing performance in soccer (Vaeyens, Lenoir, Williams, & 
102 
Philippaerts, 2007). 
103 
Futsal (FB) and soccer (SB) balls are used in futsal (the 5-a-side form of football) and 
104 
soccer (the 11-a-side form of football) respectively, and they are likely to influence how the 
105 
passing skill emerges. Both balls (i.e., FB and SB) are spherical but differ in size, 
106 
circumference of 63 and 69 cm, weight, 420 and 430 gr (FIFA, 2010), and coefficient of 
107 
restitution, 0.51 and 0.60 (Peacock, Garofolini, Oppici, Serpiello, & Ball, 2017). While 
108 
previous research showed that, in a group of young athletes, practicing futsal or soccer 
109 
exclusively influenced the perceptual skill underpinning passing (Oppici et al., 2017), the 
110 
effect of the different balls alone on the passing skill is unclear. Futsal balls are thought to be 
111 
easier to handle due to a higher energy loss during foot-ball impact (due to the lower 
112 
coefficient of restitution) that prevents FB from bouncing off the foot uncontrollably (Araújo 
113 
et al., 2004; Peacock et al., 2017). 
114 
Equipment that simplifies the execution of a skill promotes skill automaticity (i.e., 
115 
execution with little or no involvement of attention) (Buszard, Farrow, Reid, & Masters, 
116 

--- [Página 8] ---
7 
 
2014), which, in turn, has been shown to facilitate the education of attention towards 
117 
information specifying affordances (Mackenzie & Harris, 2017). Therefore, practicing the 
118 
passing skill with a FB is expected to promote the development of more efficient perception119 
action coupling relative to a SB, and the behaviour developed is expected to transfer to 
120 
passing with SB due to task similarity. While previous research (Travassos, Araujo, & 
121 
Davids, 2017) and anecdotes from elite soccer players (UEFA, 2014) have suggested that 
122 
practicing with a FB fosters the development of passing skill in soccer, implying the transfer 
123 
of skill, this issue has not been investigated. 
124 
The aim of this study was to determine the transfer of passing skill from practicing 
125 
with a FB to performing with a SB, and the perceptual skill underpinning it. The passing skill 
126 
of adult novices, who trained with a FB for 3 sessions, was evaluated against a control group 
127 
who trained with a SB. Pre- and post-training assessments were performed using a SB in both 
128 
groups to evaluate transfer. It was hypothesised that positive transfer of passing skill from a 
129 
FB to a SB would be indicated by higher improvement in passing performance for 
130 
participants training with a FB relative to the SB group, due to the FB’s properties and task 
131 
similarity. The superior performance improvement was hypothesised to be underpinned by 
132 
development of an efficient perceptual attunement to task-relevant cues, encouraged by a 
133 
higher level of skill automaticity. FB was predicted to facilitate skill automaticity that, in 
134 
turn, would promote higher attention alternations between ball and players, lower attention 
135 
time on ball and higher attention time on players.

Methods 
137 
Participants 
138 
Twenty-four adult novices (n=18 females and n=6 males, 24 ± 4.8 years old) were recruited 
139 
for the study. The required sample size was calculated a-priori using G*Power (version 3.1), 
140 
with a repeated-measures test (within-between interaction), with α = 0.05, power (1 – β) = 
141

8 
 
0.95, and an effect size of f = 0.42 (derived from similar studies with a similar design; 
142 
Abernethy, Schorer, Jackson, & Hagemann, 2012; Broadbent, Causer, Ford, & Williams, 
143 
2015), resulting in a total sample size of 22 with an actual power of 0.95. Two extra 
144 
participants were recruited (9% of calculated sample size) to account for attrition. 
145 
Participants had no prior experience in organised soccer or futsal (i.e., in a sport club), 
146 
and their experience in recreational soccer (i.e., kicking with friends or at school) and in other 
147 
team sports was collected using a customised questionnaire (table 1). The questionnaire 
148 
included questions on the average hours per week, weeks per year, and number of years of 
149 
training experience in team-sports and recreational experience in soccer. The participants 
150 
were divided into two groups, a futsal-ball experimental group (FUT, n = 12) and a soccer151 
ball control group (SOC, n = 12), after the pre-intervention test using the minimisation 
152 
procedure, which randomises the allocation of participants minimising group differences 
153 
(Hopkins, 2010b). Following this procedure, the two groups were matched for their pre-test 
154 
performance outcome, previous experience in soccer and other team sports (table 1). 
155 
Participants were informed about the aim of the study but they were blinded with respect to 
156 
the specific hypothesis. 
157 
Prior to the study, participants were fully informed of the risks involved in 
158 
participating in the experiment and they provided written informed consent to participate. 
159 
The study was approved by the research team’s University Ethics Committee.

165 
(figure 1). The sessions were interspersed by 48 h, and the time of each session was kept

Test and training stimuli constructions 
180 
Eight male soccer players (24.4 ± 4.4 years old), who regularly played in regional soccer 
181 
competitions, were filmed while performing soccer-specific movements on an outdoor pitch 
182 
to create the experimental video stimuli. A video camera (Panasonic HC-V380K Full HD, 
183 
Osaka, Japan) was positioned 20 m away from the players, at a height of 1.75 m to 
184 
approximate a soccer player’s field of view during games. The players were divided into two 
185 
teams, a red-uniform attacking team and black-uniform defending team, and three different 
186 
scenarios were created, including 2v2, 3v3 and 4v4. The players, organised in red-black pairs, 
187 
were instructed to perform set movements, with the red players moving to receive the ball 
188 
from the investigator positioned behind the camera, and the black players tightly marking 
189 
their direct opponent. Each trial ended with one of two potential outcomes, either one of the 
190 
attacking players was unmarked (i.e., his direct opponent stopped following him) or all 
191 

--- [Página 11] ---
10 
 
attacking players were tightly marked. An investigator’s verbal signal started the trial, while a 
192 
second verbal signal indicated the type of outcome. 
193 
The footage was then edited using Windows Media Player (Microsoft, Washington, 
194 
USA) to create decision-making video clips lasting 2.5 s. In each scenario, three different 
195 
types of clips were created, namely early decision, late decision and no decision. The timing 
196 
of the attacking player becoming free to receive a pass was between 1.5-1.7 and 2.0-2.1 s for 
197 
the early- and late-decision clips, respectively, while no attacking player was free in the no198 
decision clips. The early-decision clips represented an easier challenge than late-condition 
199 
clips as the teammate was free for a longer period and participants had more time to organise 
200 
their passing action. Each video clip included, in the following order, a 2-s image of the first 
201 
frame, a 3-2-1 countdown, the video and then a black screen with red vertical lines 
202 
corresponding to the final position of the attacking players (figure 2). 
203 
Apparatus and procedure 
204 
The experimental task involved the participant making a direct pass of a moving ball in 
205 
response to the video stimuli using the inside part of the dominant foot. The video clips were 
206 
projected, using a roof-mounted front projector (Mitsubishi XD550U, Tokyo, Japan), onto a 
207 
screen (4 x 2.5 m). To ensure consistency, the ball was delivered to the participants along the 
208 
ground through a hole at the bottom of the screen, via a custom-made ramp, positioned 
209 
behind the screen, that allowed speed to be approximately 2 m.sec-1 (Button, Smith, & 
210 
Pepping, 2005). This task was designed to improve the representativeness of the passing skill 
211 
in a laboratory setting. While previous research did not consider it (e.g., Helsen & Starkes, 
212 
1999; Vaeyens et al., 2007), the reception phase of a pass (i.e., when the ball travels towards 
213 
the person making a pass) is critical as it challenges an individual’s perception of information 
214 
about ball’s and player’s behaviour (Oppici et al., 2017; Oppici, Panchuk, Serpiello, & 
215 
Farrow, 2018). 
216

Pilot trials, where ball speed was indirectly calculated by measuring (from video) the 
217 
time from the ball exiting the ramp to reaching the spot where participants stood, showed 
218 
consistency in ball speed in both balls, being 1.96 ± 0.04 and 1.95 ± 0.04 m.sec-1 in FB and 
219 
SB, respectively. The ball delivery and the start of the video were manually coupled, i.e., the 
220 
ball was released on the ‘1’ of the countdown, with the video starting when the ball passed 
221 
through the screen hole. The similarity of trial duration in the two groups (2342 ± 123 and 
222 
2366 ± 132 ms in FUT and SOC, respectively) indicated consistent video-ball coupling 
223 
across the two groups. 
224 
Participants were instructed to stand on a specific spot, 5 m in front of the screen, wait 
225 
for the ball and pass it directly (i.e., without controlling it) along the ground towards the free 
226 
teammate (i.e., red attacking player). The pass had to be directed to the teammate’s current 
227 
position, not to the end-run trajectory. However, participants had to hold the ball when they 
228 
thought that no teammate was free. After each trial, participants were asked to verbalise their 
229 
decision saying out loud the number (counting the red lines left to right) corresponding to the 
230 
teammate they intended to pass the ball to. This was included to assess participant’s decision

independently from the accuracy of the kick.

Pre- and post-test 
242 
The testing sessions comprised of 24 trials, divided into two blocks of 12, including twelve 
243 
2v2 and twelve 4v4 scenarios, in a sequence that was consistent in both sessions across 
244 
participants. Each scenario included 5 early, 5 late and 2 no decision conditions. The trials 
245 
were interspersed by approximately 30 s, and no feedback was provided throughout the 
246 
session. 
247 
The sessions started with 20 warm-up kicks towards vertical red lines projected onto 
248 
the screen. Participants were then fitted with the eye tracker and the system was calibrated 
249 
using a 9-point reference grid. The calibration was checked between the two trial blocks. 
250 
In the pre-test, before the experimental trials, participants were provided with 
251 
instructions projected onto the screen, that explained the task in detail, and then provided 
252 
with 10 practice trials to become familiar with the video stimuli and with wearing the Mobile 
253 
Eye unit. 
254 
In the post-test, participants also performed a dual-task kick assessment before the 
255 
decision-making trials. The dual-task condition involved 10 kicks towards red lines projected 
256 
onto the screen while simultaneously counting back-wards out loud in ‘threes’ as quickly as 
257 
possible from a number indicated by the researcher. In each trial, the researcher provided a 
258 
different number (e.g., 54 or 76) and, after a 3-2-1 countdown, on the ‘go’ signal the ball was 
259 
released and participants started counting until the ball was kicked. Counting back-wards is a 
260 
valid stimulus to overload individuals’ attention while performing movements (Buszard et al., 
261 
2014). Participants were instructed to perform accurate kicks while counting quickly and 
262 
accurately, prioritising kick accuracy. Before the dual-task condition, participants performed 
263 
10 single-task kicks (i.e., kicking only) and 5 single-task counting (i.e., counting only). The 
264 
red line positions and number order were consistent across participants.

Data analysis 
274 
Performance accuracy 
275 
Considering that all trials involved a decision but not all of them required a pass, a 
276 
performance variable was created to capture participant’s performance accuracy. 
277 
Performance accuracy was evaluated by combining decision accuracy and pass accuracy. 
278 
Performance accuracy provided a measure of performance that balanced potential correct 
279 
decisions ending with bad kicks, and passes that were accidentally accurate (i.e., participant 
280 
meant to kick to the wrong teammate but the ball hit the correct player). Decision accuracy 
281 
was evaluated by comparing the participant’s verbal response with the correct decision, while 
282 
pass accuracy was evaluated in terms of proximity of ball end-point and the free-teammates 
283 
(i.e., correct decision) final position. The distance between the ball when it hit the screen and 
284 
the free-teammate position was evaluated by superimposing a grid onto the external-camera 
285 
video using a free-to-use video-player software (Kinovea 0.8.15). Reference points on the 
286 
projected video were used to calibrate the grid, which contained 16 spaces, at the beginning 
287 
of each evaluation. One end of the grid was placed on the final ball position (in the middle of 
288 
the ball) and the spaces between the ball and free-teammate red line were counted. As such, 
289 
the lower the distance the more accurate the pass. Performance accuracy was calculated 
290 
multiplying the participant’s decision accuracy by the inverse of average pass accuracy: 
291 

--- [Página 15] ---
14 
 
performance accuracy (AU): decision accuracy * 1/average pass accuracy. 
292 
Dual-task performance 
293 
Kick accuracy in single- and dual-task conditions was evaluated by superimposing the grid 
294 
onto the external-camera video, as described in the previous paragraph. As such, the lower 
295 
the value the more accurate the kick. In both conditions, counting performance was evaluated 
296 
as quantity of counted numbers, and number of errors in counting, through the performance 
297 
video. Dual-task cost was calculated in kicking and counting: 
298 
dual-task cost (%) = (dual-task performance – single-task performance) / dual-task 
299 
performance.

Gaze data 
301 
The video from the eye tracker and the external camera, both recorded at 30hz, were 
302 
synchronised using a commercially-available coding software (Quiet Eye Solution, QES) to 
303 
couple gaze with specific phases during the task (Vickers, 1996). The first frame of the video 
304 
stimuli was the trial onset and the participant’s first contact with the ball (either passing or 
305 
holding the ball) was the trial offset. 
306 
Four gaze behaviours were then coded as fixation, saccade, blink and other. Fixation 
307 
was coded when the gaze was stable, within 3 degrees of visual angle, on a location for a 
308 
minimum duration of 100 ms (Panchuk & Vickers, 2006), which corresponds to 3 video 
309 
frames. Saccade was coded when the gaze shifted to a different area, moving for more than 3 
310 
degrees of visual angle, with a minimum duration of 66 ms, while blink was coded when gaze 
311 
cursor disappeared for a minimum of 100 ms. Lastly, gaze was coded other when vibration of 
312 
the eye tracking made coding impossible. 
313 
 
Six fixation locations were identified: teammate-opponent pair, ball, free space (area 
314 
between players, below players’ head), free teammate, non-marking opponent (free 
315 
teammate’s direct opponent) and other (area outside the screen or above players’ head). 
316

 Number of fixations, average fixation duration, fixation order (i.e., the number of 
317 
fixation alternations between ball and other areas) and relative viewing time (%) in each area 
318 
of interest were evaluated in each trial.

Percentage transfer 
320 
The percentage transfer, from FB training to SB, was calculated for performance accuracy 
321 
with the formula: 
322 
experimental group – control group/experimental group + control group * 100 
323 
(Magill, 2011), applied to this study: 
324

Discussion 
428 
The aim of this study was to investigate the transfer of passing skill from a FB to a SB, and the 
429 
perceptual skill underpinning the process. It was hypothesised that positive transfer of passing 
430 
skill from FB to SB would be indicated by greater improvements in passing performance of 
431 
FUT relative to SOC. The results confirmed this hypothesis as FUT showed higher pre- to post432 
test improvement (i.e., larger effect sizes) and higher post-test passing performance than SOC 
433 
in all conditions, except the early condition. Practicing with a FB promoted a functional 
434 
coupling of affordance perception and coordination patterns that, when adapted to a SB, 
435 
improved performance. Particularly, the large between-group difference in performance in the 
436 
late condition showed that FB fostered the development of participants’ ability to functionally 
437 
couple perception and action in a time-constrained situation, i.e., the teammate was free for a 
438 
shorter period of time and participants had less time to organise the passing action. 
439 

--- [Página 21] ---
20 
 
The superior performance in FUT was hypothesised to be underpinned by 
440 
development of an efficient perceptual attunement to task-relevant cues, i.e., higher fixation 
441 
alternations between ball and other areas, lower fixation time on ball and higher fixation time 
442 
on players. The results confirmed that higher passing improvement in FUT was underpinned 
443 
by significant changes in their gaze behaviour, while SOC only slightly modified their 
444 
perceptual skill. Despite minimal changes in fixation duration and count in both groups, the 
445 
results of relative viewing time and fixation order indicated that changes in perceptual 
446 
attunement started to appear in FUT but not in SOC. The changes in gaze behaviour in FUT 
447 
partially confirmed the hypothesis as they increased the number of fixation alternations 
448 
between the ball and other areas (i.e., fixation order). FUT also increased the fixation time on 
449 
ball (contrary to predictions), increased fixation time on non-marking opponent, and 
450 
decreased fixation time on teammate-opponent pairs. Changes in both groups’ viewing time 
451 
resulted in FUT fixating free-teammates for longer than SOC. In addition, changes in fixation 
452 
duration and count were correlated with passing improvements in FUT but not in SOC. 
453 
Despite not entirely confirming the hypothesis, the perceptual modifications coupled with a 
454 
larger improvement in FUT, indicate that FB facilitated the development of an efficient 
455 
perceptual attunement to task-relevant cues that supported passing performance. 
456 
It was hypothesised that changes in perceptual skill would be promoted by higher skill 
457 
automaticity in FUT. The results did not confirm this hypothesis as both groups showed 
458 
similar improvement in skill automaticity throughout the intervention. Despite being easier to 
459 
kick (Peacock et al., 2017), the FB did not fast-track the development of skill automaticity 
460 
compared to SB. These results seem to contradict previous research that showed that easy-to461 
handle equipment places fewer attentional demands on performer, in turn, facilitating skill 
462 
automaticity (Buszard et al., 2014). However, a higher skill automaticity in FUT might have 
463 
been masked by the design of the dual-task assessment. Both dual-task kicking and counting 
464 

--- [Página 22] ---
21 
 
cost did not improve throughout the study in both groups suggesting that, potentially, 
465 
counting backwards and kicking did not challenge participants’ allocation of attention. 
466 
Participants perhaps focused their attention on counting when the ball was rolling towards 
467 
them and then switched attentional focus on kicking once the ball was close to them. 
468 
Therefore, participants’ attention was slightly affected during the kicking action, and skill 
469 
automaticity was not evaluated properly in the two groups. Therefore, the changes in 
470 
perceptual skill in FUT might have actually been promoted by participants’ skill automaticity 
471 
that was not captured with the adopted dual-task assessment. A potential research direction 
472 
stemming from these results would be to use a probe dual-task (Abernethy, 1988; Abernethy, 
473 
Maxwell, Masters, van der Kamp, & Jackson, 2007), where participants respond to a 
474 
secondary task during the execution of the pass, instead of a continuous dual-task, as adopted 
475 
in this study. 
476 
Mechanisms other than skill automaticity might have encouraged the development of 
477 
the observed behaviours. Previous research highlighted that equipment scaled to the 
478 
participants’ characteristics facilitated the execution of a skill, skill accuracy and encouraged 
479 
more opportunities to execute a skill (Buszard et al., 2016b). This suggests that modified 
480 
equipment might reduce movement variability, which would explain skill accuracy and 
481 
easiness in executing a skill, and, in turn, might promote the repetitions of a small number of 
482 
movement solutions, which was suggested to fast-tack the development of functional 
483 
coordination patterns (Ranganathan & Newell, 2013). In addition, haptic information changes 
484 
when properties of equipment are modified, and these changes in information likely play a role 
485 
in the learning process. Kicking a futsal or a soccer ball provides different haptic information 
486 
to the person performing the kick due to differences in the balls’ coefficient of restitution. 
487 
While previous research focused on visual information, haptic information has been argued to 
488 
be important for coordinated movement as much as, if not more, than vision (Turvey, Burton, 
489 

--- [Página 23] ---
22 
 
Amazeen, Butwill, & Carello, 1998), and an enhanced sensitivity to haptic information has 
490 
been suggested to fast-track learning (Davids, Button, & Bennett, 2008a). Therefore, a futsal 
491 
ball might have reduced movement variability and/or improved participants’ sensitivity to ball 
492 
touch during practice, and, in turn, encouraged the development of the observed gaze 
493 
behaviour. A potential future direction would be to assess movement variability and 
494 
participants’ sensitivity to haptic information when equipment is modified. 
495 
Previous research showed that futsal task constraints, including the ball, influenced 
496 
the orientation of attention underpinning passing, i.e., higher attention alternations between 
497 
ball and other areas, and lower attention time on ball relative to soccer task constraints 
498 
(Oppici et al., 2017). These results were partially confirmed in the current study as FUT 
499 
showed higher alternations between ball and other locations than SOC but FUT also 
500 
increased fixation time on ball. However, participants in Oppici et al. (2017) were skilled 
501 
junior players, while participants in this study were novices and the different skill levels 
502 
might have influenced attentional focus on ball. It is possible that individuals’ perceptual 
503 
attunement to the FB, as a source of information, follow an inverted-U-shape along the 
504 
expertise continuum. Typically, novices mainly rely on visual information to guide action but 
505 
as they progress through the expertise continuum they increasingly learn to use haptic 
506 
information (e.g., foot-ball contact) (Misceo & Plankinton, 2009). In this context, FB might 
507 
promote the use of haptic information over visual information on ball only in experts, who 
508 
are able to exploit FB properties (e.g., regular ball bounce and trajectory), being at the skill 
509 
level (Handford, Davids, Bennett, & Button, 1997). Future research direction would be to 
510 
evaluate the influence of modified equipment on individuals’ ability to use haptic information 
511 
to guide action and how the learning process is affected. An individual’s level of expertise 
512 
likely influences how equipment modification shapes skill learning and transfer. Therefore, 
513 

--- [Página 24] ---
23 
 
future research could examine how equipment modification shapes the behaviour of 
514 
individuals at different expertise levels. 
515 
A constraints-led approach, where constraint modification guides learning, as opposed 
516 
to the traditional coach-led approach, where the coach guides learning, has been suggested to 
517 
facilitate functional movement adaptations (Davids et al., 2008b). Task constraints, including 
518 
equipment, have been the focus of this approach as they can be readily manipulated by 
519 
practitioners. For example, sport programs, such as Tennis Australia’s Hot Shots program, 
520 
have recently started to scale equipment and playing area to the children’s physical 
521 
characteristics to encourage their engagement, enjoyment and development of sport skills 
522 
(Tennis Australia, 2018). The results of this study provide new insights supporting this 
523 
approach. Although constraints- and coach-led approaches were not compared, this study 
524 
showed that practicing the passing skill with the same instructions but with a different ball 
525 
influenced the learning process. Practicing the passing skill with FB was more beneficial than 
526 
SB in improving passing skill with SB, as previous research (Travassos et al., 2017) and 
527 
anecdotes suggested (UEFA, 2014). Practitioners working in soccer are encouraged to use FB 
528 
in their training sessions to fast-track learning, particularly in novices. 
529 
Despite coupling the perception of information specifying affordances and the kicking 
530 
action, the representativeness of the passing task adopted in this study could be improved in 
531 
future research. Rather than projecting players on a video screen and kicking to a target 
532 
placed at a fixed distance, the passing task could be performed with live players moving to 
533 
receive the pass. The ecological validity of the task would improve, and participants would 
534 
perform passes to players positioned at different distances in each pass. Therefore, they 
535 
would need to appropriately change the speed of their pass to accurately reach the intended 
536 
teammate. Furthermore, future research could examine the transfer of passing skill using a 
537 
soccer game as transfer task, and improve the generalisation of the findings to the game. 
538 

--- [Página 25] ---
24 
 
This study investigated issues that were relatively un-explored in the human 
539 
movement field providing results that extend the current understanding of the impact of 
540 
modified equipment on skill learning. The results showed that practicing a passing skill with 
541 
a modified ball promoted positive transfer to performing passes with another ball. The 
542 
participants that practiced with the futsal ball showed greater improvement in passing 
543 
accuracy than participants who practiced with the soccer ball. Furthermore, the results 
544 
showed that the equipment participants trained with influenced the perceptual attunement to 
545 
environmental cues. Practicing passes with the futsal promoted the education of attention 
546 
towards information specifying affordances, i.e., teammate-opponent relationships. In 
547 
summary, this study confirmed that modified equipment influences the self-organisation of 
548 
perception-action coupling, which, in turn, shapes the development of a behavioural 
549 
repertoire that can positively transfer to another equipment improving learning of a 
550 
perceptual-motor skill (Araújo et al., 2004; Farrow et al., 2016).
