Abstract 
7 
Dynamic motor skills such as volleyball blocking rely on efficient perception-action coupling 
8 
and are influenced by individual, environmental, and task constraints. However, limited 
9 
research studies have assessed the effect of an individual constraint such as blocking skill on 
10 
visual attention during an in-situ volleyball blocking task. Therefore, this study used a cross11 
sectional, observational design to investigate the gaze behaviour of eighteen male volleyball 
12 
players (25.6 ± 4.9 years), of two different levels of blocking skill determined a priori 
13 
according to success during an on-court blocking task. When compared to less successful 
14 
players (RUS), the gaze of more successful players (RS) was observed to fixate more often 
15 
(RUS: 0.7±0.7n, RS: 1.3±0.3n) and dwell for longer (Total; RUS: 12.2± 18.4%, RS: 
16 
48.0±37.2%, Phase 4; RUS: 6.6±8.8%, RS: 16.9±12.4%) on the opposition spiker, 
17 
demonstrating that important perceptual information about an opposing team’s attack lies 
18 
within the behaviour of the opposition spiker. More successful blockers were also observed to 
19 
be taller (RUS: 181.8±6.6cm, RS: 192.6±3.9cm), longer in arm-span (RUS: 185.7±5.6cm, 
20 
RS: 195.2±5.6cm) and heavier (RUS: 78.6±11.4kg, RS: 90.5±8.5kg). These results can 
21 
consequently be used to develop a profile of the visual attention and physical attributes of 
22 
successful blockers for use in developing talented players.

Introduction 
26 
The sport of volleyball is characterised by multiple high-intensity bouts of repetitive 
27 
explosive efforts, each usually 5-20 seconds in duration (Sheppard, Gabbett, & Stanganelli, 
28 
2009). In addition, play requires the manipulation of a fast-moving, airborne ball which can 
29 
only be rebounded off limbs and not caught or thrown. Since originating in 1895, volleyball 
30 
has now developed to become a leading international sport (FIVB, 2011). Such popularity 
31 
has resulted in an increased focus on the factors related to volleyball expertise (Eom & 
32 
Schutz, 1992; Palao & Santos, 2004; Rodriguez-Ruiz et al., 2011). 
33 
Blocking has been identified as one of the most important skills in determining the 
34 
result of a match in high-level indoor volleyball (Eom & Schutz, 1992; Palao & Santos, 2004; 
35 
Rodriguez-Ruiz et al., 2011) and is commonly used by teams to score points or counter 
36 
opposition attacks (Afonso, Mesquita, & Palao, 2005; Amasay, 2008; Araújo, Mesquita, & 
37 
Marcelino, 2009). A tall stature and long arm span are definite advantages in increasing the 
38 
size and reach of a block, however not all big players are proficient blockers (Stamm et al., 
39 
2017). Successful performance in blocking requires the coordination of several complex 
40 
spatial and temporal parameters. Specifically, the preparation and execution of a blocking 
41 
movement must begin in advance of an opposition player’s spike, as the oppositions attacking 
42 
pattern emerges, with the eventual attempt to block the ball occurring mid-air through the 
43 
forward and sideways manipulation of the arms and hands, in varying locations and often 
44 
within metres of the spiker (Afonso et al., 2005; Amasay, 2008; Araújo et al., 2009). 
45 
Therefore, a proficient blocker should be able to recognise an opponent’s attack early in order 
46 
to execute an accurate and fast blocking movement, especially at higher levels of competition 
47 
where faster gameplay creates greater time constraints (Panfil & Superlak, 2012). 
48 
Successful decision-making when blocking requires exceptional levels of two 
49 
performance elements, perception-action coupling and perceptual-cognitive ability (Afonso et

Materials and Methods 
118 
Participants 
119 
Nine top division and nine second division male Australian state league volleyball players 
120 
(mean age 25.6 ± 4.9 years, weight 84.6 ± 11.5 kg, height 187.2 ± 7.6 cm, arm span 190.5 ± 
121 
7.3 cm) volunteered to participate in this study. The top state league division is two tiers 
122 
below the national team level and players train approximately 4 hours per week. All 
123 
participants had more than three years experience in their current playing level and played a 
124 
role that regularly required blocking in multiple positions. All participants gave written 
125 
consent before participating in this study. The study was approved by the University of 
126 
Technology Sydney (UTS), Human Research Ethics Committee (ETH16-0651). 
127 
Participants were assigned to a relatively successful (RS) or relatively unsuccessful 
128 
(RUS) group based on their within-task performance, a method previously used successfully 
129 
by Vaeyens et al. (2007). In-task performance was determined by taking the mean of all 
130 
accuracy scores per participant per trial. Accuracy scores consisted of a point score between 
131 
one and three (one = poor/error; three = optimal) for three performance measures (timing, 
132 
location and body form) during the blocking task, where the maximum score per trial was 
133 
nine and the minimum possible score was three. Location was defined as the lateral area of 
134 
the net the blocker’s arms and body were located in relation to the opposition spiker. Timing 
135 
was defined as the coordination of the blocker’s peak jump height in relation to the time of 
136 
the opposition spiker’s contact with the ball. Form was defined as the shape of the blocker’s 
137 
arms and body. The maximum score was given if a block was successful. In the case where 
138 
the attacker was able to spike higher than the test participant’s block due to a physical size 
139 
difference between the players, the maximum score was also given if all performance 
140 
measures were performed optimally but the block was missed. Scoring of each trial was 
141 
determined using the mean score given by three independent accredited Level 3 Volleyball

Design 
148 
This study used a cross-sectional design in which all participants participated in a series of 26 
149 
attack sequences, randomly distributed over four different attackers. Additional Australian 
150 
state league volleyball players were recruited to perform the in situ attack sequences in an 
151

152 
participants. Each sequence consisted of an under-arm serve, a reception from one of the 
153 
three receivers, a left, middle, right or back-court pass from the setter, and a spike from an 
154 
attacker (Figure 1). This volleyball attack sequence is commonly used throughout all 
155 
experience levels. 
156 
Prior to starting the assessment trials, five familiarisation attack sequences were 
157 
performed for each participant, with two passes to the left side spiker (LS), one pass to the 
158 
middle spiker (MS), one pass to the right side spiker (RS) and one pass to the back-court 
159 
spiker (BCS). The assessment trials consisted of 26 attack sequences in a randomised order 
160 
with 12 passes to LS, four passes to MS, seven passes to RS and three passes to BCS. The 
161 
quantities used are approximately reflective of the number of attacks which immediately 
162 
follow a serve during one set of volleyball. Sequences were repeated if the reception was not 
163

Skill level and visual attention in blocking 
Discussion 
311 
The aims of this study were to test the applicability of using head-mounted eye trackers 
312 
during an on-field volleyball blocking task and further, investigate the effect of volleyball 
313 
blocking skill level on visual search behaviour. The results on this study indicate that head314 
mounted eye trackers may be an applicable tool when investigating visual search patterns in a 
315 
real-world context. Additionally, the results revealed that the gaze of more skilled players 
316 
fixated significantly more often and dwelled for longer on the opposition spiker, particularly 
317 
in the final phase of the attacking sequence, as the opposition spiker is about to contact the 
318 
ball. 
319 
The present findings are the first to analyse visual attention during an in situ blocking 
320 
task. The visual attention of all blockers was shown to follow a common pattern, with 
321 
fixations moving from the ball, to the setter, the ball, and onto the spiker before the blocking 
322 
movement was executed. This fixation order follows the logical progression of an attacking 
323 
sequence, with the ball travelling from the receiver, to the setter and then to a spiker. This 
324 
order aligns well with previous laboratory based research measuring the visual attention of 
325 
volleyball players during a defensive task (Afonso et al., 2012, 2014; Cohen, 1988; 
326 
Vansteenkiste, Vaeyens, Zeuwts, Philippaerts, & Lenoir, 2014; Wright et al., 1990) and 
327 
appears to demonstrate that using head-mounted eye trackers like the SMI ETG2W may be 
328 
suitable to investigate visual search patterns in a real-world context. 
329 
The presence of a common visual attention pattern also indicates that volleyball 
330 
players specifically assign varying levels of attention to each AOI at specific times during an 
331 
attacking sequence, likely based on the current location of the ball. This behaviour may 
332 
reflect an attempt to extract the most relevant environmental information for making their 
333 
blocking decision as visual attention is considered to be directly related to information 
334 
extraction and therefore assigned importance (Mann et al., 2007). An examination of the

386

387

388

389

390 
still reach the same block height. Having a longer time to observe a stimulus before 
391 
committing to a final decision has been shown in research to be advantageous when 
392 
generating an accurate response (Borgeaud & Abernethy, 1987; Starkes et al., 1995). Such 
393 
alleviated time constraints could have contributed to the RS group achieving significantly 
394 
longer relative dwell times on the spiker, particularly during the fourth phase. Similarly, it is 
395 
plausible that RUS blockers allocated less visual attention on the spiker as they may have 
396 
needed to execute their block jump earlier, therefore reducing their opportunity during the 
397 
fourth phase to direct their gaze and gather information important for an accurate decision. 
398

399 
opportunity to extract visual information from their environment. Therefore, in addition to a 
400 
greater block size, greater success may also be associated with the anthropometry of taller 
401 
and longer volleyball blockers, who may benefit from alleviated time constraint demands 
402 
which allow greater observational opportunity. Future research could investigate this 
403

404

421 
Perceptual-cognitive ability undoubtedly contributes to expertise in sporting actions, such as 
422

423

424

425

426

427

429

430

432

433

434

435

436

437

438

439
