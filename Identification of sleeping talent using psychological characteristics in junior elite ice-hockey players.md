SPORT AND EXERCISE PSYCHOLOGY
Identification of “sleeping” talent using psychological characteristics in junior elite 
ice-hockey players
Daniel Fortin-Guichard
a,b, Émie Tétreaulta, David Paqueta, David L. Mannb and Simon Grondina
aSchool of Psychology, Université Laval. 2325 Allée des Bibliothèques, Québec, QC, Canada; bDepartment of Human Movement Sciences, Vrije 
Universiteit Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The Netherlands
ABSTRACT
Scouts search for “sleepers” who may be initially overlooked but ultimately exceed expectations. The 
psychological characteristics of those players are often neglected because they are difficult to observe, 
but hold promise to identify sleepers given for example the self-regulation and perceptual-cognitive skills 
that those developing players might need to flourish. The aim of this study was to examine whether 
sleepers could be retrospectively identified using psychological characteristics. Ninety-five junior elite 
ice-hockey players (aged 15–16) were assessed on self-regulation and perceptual-cognitive skills before 
the yearly draft. Seventy players were drafted after the second round (37th or later). Three years later, 
professional scouts identified 15/70 sleepers they would now pick if given the chance. Those identified by 
the scouts showed higher self-regulation planning, and had distinguishable gaze behaviour (fewer 
fixations on more AOIs) when performing a video-based decision-making task than other late-drafted 
players (84.3% correct classification; R2 = .40). In addition, two latent profiles differentiated by self- 
regulation were found, with the profile with higher scores including 14/15 players selected by the scouts. 
Psychological characteristics were successful in retrospectively predicting sleepers, and may in future 
help scouts to make better selections of talent.

In sports, some athletes currently under-perform relative to 
their peers, but end up surpassing them later in life, while 
others currently perform well, but end up under-performing. 
In other words, current performance is not necessarily 
a good predictor of potential in sports (Baker et al., 2018). 
This represents a major challenge for scouts when they try 
to identify which players will perform well in the future, as 
they cannot rely solely on the performance they observe. 
Scouts in sports are those who are responsible for identify­
ing the players who are most likely to succeed either within 
an existing team or at a more advanced level of competi­
tion at some time in the future (i.e., they are responsible for 
talent identification; Guenter et al., 2018; Vaeyens et al., 
2008). There are numerous examples of athletes who were 
not identified when they were younger but ended up sur­
passing expectations. NFL quarterback Tom Brady was 
selected 199th in the 2000 NFL draft and is now considered 
one of the best quarterbacks to have ever played the sport. 
Martin St-Louis was never drafted in the National Hockey 
League (NHL), but is now a renowned member of the 
Hockey Hall of Fame. This specific type of overlooked talent, 
sometimes anecdotally referred to as a “sleeper”, “whispering 
talent” or “hidden gem”, has attracted attention in the pop­
ular media and some theoretical work (Ankersen, 2013; 
Baker et al., 2018; National Hockey League, 2021b, 2021a). 
However, to date, sleepers have received surprisingly little 
attention in empirical investigations.
A study by Schorer et al. (2017) represents one of the few 
scientific demonstrations of sleeping talent, even though that 
might not have necessarily been the main focus of their study. 
Schorer et al. sought to compare the prognostic validity of 
talent selections made in handball by various groups (e.g., 
coaches, players, laypersons). They accessed data of 68 female 
handball players who attended a training camp 10 years earlier 
when they were 12–14 years old. At the end of the camp, seven 
coaches made selections of who they considered to be the 14 
best players. Ten years later, the authors examined which com­
petition level each player had achieved. It was found that 12.1% 
of the players who the coaches did not formerly select ended 
up being high achievers (i.e., sleepers). Even more markedly, of 
the two (out of the original 68) players who ended up playing 
for the National team, one of them was overlooked by five of 
the seven coaches. This provided perhaps the first empirical 
demonstration of a sleeper: someone largely “missed” who 
ended up being one of the best players in their country.
The search for sleeping talent
To better understand sleeping talent, it appears necessary to 
first define what a “talented athlete” or an “athlete with poten­
tial” are. Clear definitions of talent and potential in sports have 
yet to reach consensus in the scientific literature, and it is 
unlikely that it is ever going to happen given the subjective 
nature of what constitutes a “talented athlete” (Fortin-Guichard

Baker et al. (2018) rather refers to having the necessary char­

varies from current to future abilities, and “potential” is there­
fore more-or-less synonym of Baker and colleagues’ definition

In many professional team sports, organisations invest con­
siderable financial and human resources in their search for 
potential talent. Scouts represent the main investment clubs 
make with respect to talent identification. Indeed, in addition to 
their salary, clubs must pay for scouts’ expenses (travel, accom­
modation, food) because they have to travel often to watch 
potentially talented players. Scouts are hired based on the 
assumption that they have an extensive understanding of the 
game to identify players with potential. Depending on the 
sport, the scouts can select players into youth academies (e.g., 
in football), or can be required to rank players in advance of 
a draft1 (Aalto & Räihä, 2012; Guenter et al., 2018).
Player drafts provide fascinating insight into the ability of 
sport organisations to make selections of talent and into the 
identification of relevant information to guide selections. To 
rank players, scouts can use numerous sources of subjective 
and objective information. For examples, scout rankings are 
known to be influenced by objective measures such as 
a player’s in-match statistics (e.g., goals and assists in ice- 
hockey), and a player’s test results at a combine where players’ 
skills are quantified (Rowan et al., 2015), and also by subjective 
assessments such as opinions on that player’s skill level and for 
how well they might fit-in with a team’s ideals for playing style 
and philosophy (Guenter et al., 2018). The combination of these 
measures is assumed to provide a deeper and better under­
standing of the characteristics of each athlete (Sieghartsleitner 
et al., 2019) and to help scouts rank the players. Although this 
generally leads to agreement on who are the most outstanding 
players who should be selected early in a draft, there is typically 
considerable variability in the selections in the remaining 
rounds (Johnston et al., 2022). Highlighting what factors 
might help to identify sleepers would be of great help for 
scouts and for organisations seeking future talent.
What makes sleepers so hard to identify?
Many reasons explain why it is difficult to find sleepers during 
the talent identification process. First, for a scout to see every 
player they want to assess, and/or to see specific athletes 
multiple times, available time and scheduling demands may 
reduce the chances of marginal players receiving attention 
(Baker et al., 2018; Guenter et al., 2018). Second, performance 
in sports is often complex and multidimensional (Lund & 
Söderström, 2017). For example, ice-hockey players must 
acquire physical, technical, tactical, and psychological skills to 
adapt to the ever-changing play situations (Lemoyne et al., 
2022). This complexity makes it difficult to determine who is 
talented. Third, the scouts’ assessments have their own biases. 
For example, scouts may mistake players’ physical stature for 
talent, when in fact they only select players born closer to the 
yearly cut-off date (i.e., relative age effect; Grondin et al., 1984; 
Mann, 2020). Finally, the scouts’ methods for assessing talent 
are likely incomplete. A lot of effort is devoted to the physical 
and technical assessments (i.e., in-game statistics, combine, 
reports), but some determinants of performance such as psy­
chological capabilities remain under-evaluated (Lemoyne et al., 
2022). Psychological characteristics are traits and states of the 
mind that influence thoughts, emotions, and behaviours (e.g., 
personality, cognition, mental health; IGI Global, 2023). To 
obtain information about these psychological characteristics, 
scouts sometimes can talk with the players, their coaches, 
teachers, or family members (McNamara et al., (2010)). These 
interviews may be useful, but it has been anecdotally reported 
that athletes often come well prepared by their agent (National 
Hockey League, 2009) and respond according to what is 
expected to be a “right answer”, making it difficult to obtain 
a realistic overview of their actual psychological profile.
Identifying sleeping talent using psychological 
characteristics
To help scouts better capture players’ psychological character­
istics, valid and time-efficient tools are available. Indeed, psy­
chological characteristics such as self-regulatory skills (e.g., 
reflection, effort) and perceptual-cognitive skills (e.g., decision- 
making, eye movements) have been shown to be promising 
predictors of future performance that could discriminate ath­
letes with potential in invasive team sports such as football, 
basketball, and ice-hockey (Jonker et al., 2010; Lemoyne et al., 
2022; O’Connor et al., 2016). To our knowledge, no scientific 
work has yet examined the psychological characteristics of 
sleepers. However, given that players drafted in the later 
rounds are often considered relatively homogeneous in terms 
of observable skills (Johnston et al., 2022), their psychological 
characteristics could be one of the few ways to distinguish 
them from other late-drafted players.
Jonker et al. (2010) have demonstrated that some self- 
regulatory skills are associated with success in invasion team 
sports. They examined the self-regulatory skills (i.e., planning, 
self-monitoring, effort, self-efficacy, evaluation, and reflection) 
of 222 athletes of national or international level and showed 
that the international athletes had higher scores than national 
athletes on the reflection subscale (i.e., the ability to appraise 
their learnings and adapt past knowledge and experiences to 
improve themselves). This finding indicates that self-regulation 
skills (especially reflection) could prove useful for the identifica­
tion of talent among high-level athletes.
Perceptual-cognitive skills are clearly related to skill in inva­
sion team sports. For instance, O’Connor et al. (2016) examined 
127 elite football players who were selected (n = 22) or not (n = 
105) to receive a scholarship. Players responded to 80 offensive

The aim of this study was to determine whether “sleepers” 
could be retrospectively identified using psychological charac­
teristics. The psychological characteristics of promising junior 
ice-hockey players were assessed prior to entering a draft. 
Based on the weight of evidence available, we chose to exam­
ine self-regulation (Jonker et al., 2010) and perceptual- 
cognitive skills (Klostermann & Moeinirad, 2019; O’Connor 
et al., 2016). Three years later, the performance of the players 
was assessed using in-game statistics. In addition, scouts 
knowledgeable of the players identified “sleepers” who they, 
in hindsight, would have picked in their team. Based on existing 
literature, we expected sleepers to (1) have better scores in self- 
regulation abilities, more specifically in the reflection sub-scale; 
(2) have better decision-making skills; and (3) make fewer but 
longer fixations than other late-drafted players.
Method
Participants
Ninety-five 15–16 years old (Mage = 15.59, S.D. = 0.49) male 
junior elite ice-hockey players participated: eight goaltenders, 
28 defencemen, and 59 forwards. They practiced ice-hockey on 
average 11.05 hours per week (S.D. = 3.91).
All participants played in one of the three highest levels 
possible for their age in Canada and were eligible for selection 
for the 2019 draft of a Major Junior league. Participants were 
contacted for an interview in the month prior to the draft by 
the scouts of a team playing in that league who were collabor­
ating with our research group. The data were collected for that 
team, with participants giving their written informed consent 
for the data to be used for research. The ethics committee of 
the Université Laval in research with humans approved this 
study (approbation number: 2019–024).
Materials
The experiment took place in a room either in an arena, 
a hotel, or a school close to each player’s neighbourhood. 
The rooms were reasonably free from distraction (i.e., very 
few auditory or visual distractions were present), although it 
was not always possible to fully negate them all given that 
the scouting interviews in some instances took part in ice- 
hockey arenas. An Intel Core i-7 computer running Windows 
10 with a 22-inch computer screen (refresh rate 60 Hz) was 
used to present video clips to the participants. Participants’ 
eye movements were tracked at 120 Hz using a Tobii X3– 
120 device (0.4° accuracy and 0.24° precision; similar 
devices were used in sports research: Catteeuw et al., 
2009; Fortin-Guichard et al., 2020). The experiment was 
programmed using Tobii Pro Lab version 1.31 (Tobii Pro, 
Stockholm, SWE). An LG digital recorder was used to record 
audio during the experiment.
Video clips
Video clips were filmed at 30 Hz using a GoPro HERO7 Black 
camera during a regular training session from the team colla­
borating with this research. The clips were filmed either from 
the stands (Figure 1a) or from behind the goaltender 
(Figure 1b). Scenarios represented attacking situations with 
varying number of attackers and defenders (either 3v2, 2v2 or 
2v1). Only odd-men attacking situations were chosen to pre­
sent at least one opportunity for players to identify a good 
decision. The selection procedure for the clips is presented in 
Supplementary Material Section 1.1.
Variables
Sociodemographic questionnaire
A pen-and-paper questionnaire was used to collect players’ 
date-of-birth, injury history and an estimation of weekly train­
ing hours. Injury history and weekly training hours were col­
lected as part of the scouting process but are not included in 
the present study given that they were not relevant to the 
experimental question of interest.

Self-regulated learning questionnaire
Self-regulatory skills were measured using either a French or 
English pen-and-paper version of the Self-regulated learning 
questionnaire, depending on each participant’s preferred lan­
guage (Jonker et al., 2010, 2011). The French version was 
a double translation of the English version. The questionnaire 
included 50 items divided into six subscales (planning, self- 
monitoring, evaluation, reflection, effort, and self-efficacy). 
A definition and a sample item of each subscale are presented 
in Supplementary Material Section 1.2. Questions were 
answered either on a four (planning, self-monitoring, effort 
and self-efficacy) or five-point (evaluation and reflection) 
Likert scale ranging from never to always. The English version 
showed acceptable internal consistency among teenagers 
(Cronbach’s α between .73 and .85) and sufficient temporal 
stability (ICCs between .69 and .84; Toering et al., 2012). In the 
present study, when combining the English and French results, 
the Cronbach’s α varied between .55 (self-monitoring) and .84 
(evaluation), with all the other subscales above .70.
Anticipation
Athletes were asked to predict, as rapidly as possible, what an 
identified player in the video would do immediately after the 
video occluded. The task varied according to position (goalten­
der, defencemen, forward): goaltenders and forwards had to 
predict whether the puck carrier would shoot (press “s” on the 
keyboard) or pass (“p”) the puck whereas defencemen had to 
predict whether the defenceman closest to the puck would 
keep skating backwards (down arrow) or skate towards the 
puck carrier (left or right arrow depending on the position of 
the defencemen relative to the puck). A score out of eight was 
calculated where one point was attributed for each clip cor­
rectly anticipated.
Decision-making
Once participants gave their anticipation answer, the video clip 
remained frozen and participants were asked to verbalise what 
they would do if they were the player involved and why. 
Following Fortin-Guichard et al. (2021), the quality of their 
decision-making was evaluated using the amount of relevant 
information on which their decision was based on. Participants 
were not aware of how their decision-making was evaluated. 
The relevance of each piece of information was determined 
before the experiment by the coaches of the Major Junior team. 
Relevant information included whether the players were shoot­
ing from the left or right, skating speed before occlusion, and 
position relative to the puck, and their teammates or oppo­
nents. Verbal responses were recorded and transcribed verba­
tim. Each mention of a relevant cue resulted in one point. The 
first clip was a familiarisation trial and participants received 
feedback to be as precise as possible on what visual cue(s) 
they used to make their decision. The eight remaining clips 
were used to calculate the average score per clip.
Eye movements
Prior to the temporal occlusion of the video clips, participants’ 
eye movements were recorded. From these, the number of 
fixations per clip, the percentage of time spent fixating, the 
location (i.e., areas of interest; AOI) and the moment of fixation 
for each fixation were extracted. A fixation was defined as 
a moment when the angular velocity of the eyes relative to 
the stimuli was 30°/s or less for at least 100 ms (Schorer et al., 
2013). The defined AOIs were all the players involved in the clip. 
An “Elsewhere” AOI was assigned to fixations that did not fall 
within a player-AOI. The moment of fixation was transformed in 
percentage time elapsed since the beginning of the clip, as the 
clips had slightly different durations (M = 2.1s, S.D. = 0.4s), 
although key events occurred roughly at the same time. The 
number of fixations, number of AOIs fixated, and percentage of 
time spent fixating reported here only concern the two clips 
that were shown to every playing position to allow 
a comparison. However, with respect to the moment and the 
location of each fixation, all clips were kept, as these data were 
analysed for each position separately.
Ice-hockey performance
Publicly available ice-hockey performance data were extracted 
at the end of the season three years after the self-regulation 
and perceptual-cognitive data were collected. Data for the 
players that played at least one game in the Major Junior 
league (n = 63) were extracted from the league’s website. 
Eight players played at least one game in an equivalent league 
(e.g., NCAA) and their performance data were extracted from 
their respective websites. Twenty-four players did not play any 
games at the Major Junior level. Performance measures for all 
positions included the draft rank and the number of games 
played. In addition, the number of goals, assists and differential 
(i.e., number of times a player’s team scored when they were on 
the ice compared to the number of times the team was scored 
against when they were on the ice) were collated for the 
defencemen and forwards. In the case of goaltenders, perfor­
mance statistics were not extracted because of the low number 
of participants. Players who did not play a game were allocated 
missing values on goals, assists and differential.
In addition to the objective performance measures, the 
scouts from the Major Junior team also provided their evalua­
tion of each player when they were 15 years old. These evalua­
tions included three scores: skating ability, on-ice decision- 
making ability and competitiveness. Five scouts gave 
a subjective score for each of these three skills from 0 (worst) 
to 9 (best). The three scores were then averaged across the skills 
and the scouts to obtain a global appreciation of the player.
The scouts were also asked to discuss and identify which 
players they would now want in their team among the players 
drafted after the second round, because three years had passed 
since the initial data collection (i.e., the collection of the data for 
self-regulation and perceptual-cognitive skill). Since there are 
18 teams in the Major Junior league, 18 players were drafted 
per round. Therefore, the scouts were asked to identify the 
players they would now want among those drafted at rank 37 
or later (i.e., after seeing the players play for three years at the 
Major Junior level). They could choose as many players as they 
wanted.

For the data collection regarding self-regulation and percep­
tual-cognitive skills, participants arrived individually to take

part in a “typical” scouting interview with the Major Junior team 
collaborating to this research (e.g., being asked about their 
strengths and weaknesses). A research assistant informed par­

self-regulation and perceptual-cognitive skill. Participants were 
assured that their answers would not impact their selection. 
They signed the consent form and completed both pen-and-

the audio recorder and the scouts asked typical scouting ques­

the experiment, but all participants (within a position) viewed

experiment lasted approximately 45 minutes per player.
Data analyses
Analyses were conducted using R Studio Version 1.3 (RStudio, 
PBC, Boston,
MA) or the SAS/STAT software, version 9.4, for the eye- 
tracking data (i.e., “when” and “where” the ocular fixations 
were). To give a portrait of the players, percentages, means, 
and standard deviations were calculated for the position 
played, the quartile of birth and the number of games played. 
Pearson correlations were calculated to explore how all the 
psychological and performance variables were related. Draft 
ranks were used to determine whether a player was a late 
draftee (i.e., those drafted in the third round or later out of 14 
rounds of selection). To identify sleepers among all late draf­
tees, players identified by the scouts from the Major Junior 
team as those they would now want in their team were con­
sidered sleepers (i.e., after seeing them play for three seasons).
Two analyses were performed. First, a stepwise logistic 
regression was run using an AIC selection criterion (MASS 
R package) to identify sleepers. The independent variables 
were the draft rank, the scouts’ average appreciation score, 
the players’ quartile of birth, their average scores on the six self- 
regulation subscales, their anticipation scores, their decision- 
making scores, the mean percentage of time they spent fixat­
ing, the number of AOIs they fixated, and the number of fixa­
tions they performed. Nagelkerke R2 and percentage of correct 
classification are reported. To establish correct classification, 
logistic regressions use a .50 predicted probability cut-off by 
default (e.g., in this case, participants with a predicted prob­
ability higher than .50 would be classified in the sleeper group). 
However, from a talent identification perspective, we consider 
that it is more valuable to identify talent (i.e., true positives) 
than it is to ignore less talented players (i.e., true negatives), 
because actual talent is considered rare (Baker et al., 2018; 
Gagné, 2011). Therefore, an optimal cut-off classification point 
was determined manually by trial-and-error where at least 70% 
of sleepers were indeed classified as such, while not classifying 
more than 30% of the other late-draftees as sleepers. A cross- 
validation of the model was performed by running the stepwise 
logistic regression five more times, with a fifth of the sample 
removed each time. The removed players from each cross- 
validation were then considered a test sample to see if the 
predicted probabilities of being a sleeper applied to them 
when they were not included in the calculation of the cross- 
validation. Each participant was included in the calculation of 
four out of five cross-validations. The cross-validation was per­
formed to check whether the original model was over-fitted to 
the data.
Second, a latent profile analysis (tidyLPA R package) was run 
to identify latent sub-populations among late draftees. Here, 
the algorithm did not know a priori who was considered 
a sleeper. This analysis aimed to identify whether sleepers 
would share a similar psychological profile to each other, 
while being dissimilar to other late draftees. Variables included 
in the model were the same as the regression. The analysis 
provides fit indexes (AIC, BIC) to identify the optimal number of 
profiles, with lower indexes suggesting better fit (Spurk et al., 
2020).2
Results
Table 1 indicates how all the psychological and performance 
variables correlated with each other across the whole sample. 
All the self-regulation subscales correlated moderately to 
strongly with each other (rs ≥ .29, ps ≤ .001). This was also 
true for the eye-movement measures (rs ≥ .20, ps ≤ .05). Most 
ice-hockey performance measures correlated with each other 
(rs ≥ .32, ps ≤ .05), except for the differential not correlating 
with draft rank and the number of games played (r = .09, p 
= .48; r = −.16, p = .20, respectively). Only three significant cor­
relations were obtained between psychological measures and 
performance: between the self-regulation reflection score and 
the number of games played (r = .22, p = .032), and between 
decision-making and draft rank (r = −.24, p = .017) and scouts’ 
appreciation (r = .28, p = .007). Overall, results showed little 
relationship between the psychological profile at 15 years and 
performance at 18. The next sections detail this relationship 
with a focus on sleepers.

Identifying latent subpopulations
The latent profile analysis (Figure 2) of the late-drafted players 
revealed that a solution with two profiles better fit the data, 
showing lower (better) AIC and BIC indexes (AIC = 2723.48; BIC 
= 2845.68) than that when including only one (AIC = 2823.02; 
BIC = 2885.98) or three (AIC = 2748.99; BIC = 2853.89) profiles. 
Solutions with four (AIC = 2722.06; BIC = 2886.20) and five (AIC 
= 2704.95; BIC = 2902.82) profiles showed lower AIC, but higher 
BIC indexes than the solution with two. The first sub-population 
(red in Figure 2) contained 46 players, and the second (blue in 
Figure 2) contained 24 players. The two profiles were similar in all 
variables except the scores on the six self-regulation subscales, 
with one profile clearly showing higher scores on each subscale. 
Importantly, the profile with higher scores included 14 of the 15 
sleepers, representing 30.4% (14/46) of this subpopulation. The 
profile with the lower self-regulation scores therefore included 
only one sleeper, representing 4.2% of this subpopulation (1/24).
Discussion
The aim of this study was to determine whether sleeping talent 
could be identified when measuring psychological characteris­
tics. A short test battery comprising measures of self-regulation 
and perceptual-cognitive skills was administered to elite junior 
ice-hockey players when they were 15 or 16 years old. The 
relationship between the late-drafted players’ psychological 
characteristics at 15–16 years and their on-ice performance at 
18–19 years was tested, with a focus placed on characterising 
those players identified as sleepers (i.e., identified three years 
later by scouts). It is noteworthy that the present study is the 
first, to our knowledge, to put an empirical focus on sleepers, 
showing it was retrospectively possible to identify those slee­
pers with relatively high accuracy using the measures of self- 
regulation and perceptual-cognitive skill. These results provide 
insights into these highly sought-after “sleepers” who are often 
overlooked by scouts, but who defy the odds and provide value 
to teams able to identify them.
Based on previous literature, we hypothesised that sleepers 
would have (1) better reflection abilities (Jonker et al., 2010), (2) 
better decision-making (O’Connor et al., 2016), and (3) distinct 
gaze behaviours when watching video clips (Klostermann & 
Moeinirad, 2019). These hypotheses were partially confirmed.

while only identifying 8/55 other late-drafted players as slee­

differences in self-regulatory and perceptual-cognitive skills 
between groups of athletes (Jonker et al., 2010; O’Connor

findings within athletes with a very homogeneous skill level. 
Those findings suggest that athletes’ psychological profiles, 
even when measured within 45 minutes, may be a promising 
indicator of potential when identifying future talent.
Age of the sleepers
The personal characteristic of the sleepers that stands out the 
most in the present study is their birth quartile of the year. 
Indeed, odds ratios suggest no relative age effect within the 
sleepers, despite there being a strong effect across the whole 
sample. This suggests an over-representation of those born 
later in the selection year amongst the group of sleepers. The 
relative age effect is one of the most well-known issues in talent 
identification, where coaches and scouts tend to select players 
born soon after the yearly cut-off date used for junior competi­
tions, largely because they mistake physical stature for talent 
(Grondin et al., 1984; Mann, 2020). This result may be explained 
in light of the reverse relative age effect, where late-born 
players who are able to survive the talent pathway tend to 
catch-up (and maybe surpass) early-born players once 
a higher level of competition is attained. This could be due to 
late-born players having to show higher resilience and motiva­
tion to overcome challenges (Fumarco et al., 2017; Lemoyne 
et al., 2021) in addition to the likelihood that they learn to 
compete against others who are older and probably more 
mature than they are. Our findings suggests that if sleepers 
born later in the year would have been evaluated at the same 
age as the others, there is a chance that they would have been 
selected earlier in the draft. This result provides a useful insight 
for organisations to pay attention to players born later in 
the year when looking for sleeping talent.
Predicting sleeper status
Our predictive model was surprisingly successful in identifying 
sleepers. Better planning abilities and distinct eye movements 
(i.e., more AOIs fixated and fewer fixations) predicted 40% of 
the variance and correctly classified almost 85% of the late- 
selected players when sleepers were identified by experienced 
scouts. These results support the idea that psychological char­
acteristics hold promise for talent identification. This could be 
even more true when trying to identify sleepers, as players 
selected later in a draft are often considered to be physically 
and technically homogeneous (Johnston et al., 2022), leaving 
psychological characteristics as one of the few ways to differ­
entiate those players.
Although our prediction model included the planning sub- 
scale in the model, it might be that it acted as a proxy for the 
broader construct of self-regulation. The self-regulation sub­
scales correlated strongly with each other, and it could be 
that planning was the one that one was chosen in the model 
by chance. Further, this would have prevented the other sub- 
scales from contributing to the model (including the hypothe­
sised reflection subscale). This interpretation is further sup­
ported by the model that performed similarly when 
a composite score of self-regulation was included. The results 
suggest that sleepers have better self-regulation skills in gen­
eral than other late draftees. Learners with better self- 
regulation abilities are better able to comprehend knowledge 
and skills they acquire and to apply them to various situations 
(Peltier et al., 2006). Late-drafted players with better self- 
regulation abilities may be better able to assimilate what they 
learned and to develop better sport-specific characteristics, 
resulting in them becoming sleepers. They might have been 
less skilled to start with, but their self-regulation allowed them 
to develop their skills better than other late-drafted players 
with a comparable skill level and given comparable access to 
resources (e.g., better coaches). From a methodological stand­
point, these results highlight that separating self-regulation 
into six subscales may be unnecessary. From a talent identifica­
tion perspective, where time for mass testing is often scarce, 
scouts could reduce the number of questions used in the 
questionnaire. For instance, a modified questionnaire could 
focus on planning, effort and reflection (Jonker et al., 2010).
With respect to eye movements variable, those included in 
the model slightly differ from the original hypothesis (i.e., fewer 
but longer fixations). Indeed, the sleepers tended to fixate more 
AOIs with fewer fixations. The difference between the results 
and the hypothesis may come from the very strong correlation 
(r = .74) between the number of AOIs fixated (kept in the 
model) and time spent fixating (hypothesised). It could be 
that the number of AOIs fixated was selected by the algorithm 
merely above fixation duration. Performing fewer but longer 
ocular fixations when watching sport-specific video clips has 
often been linked with perceptual-cognitive expertise 
(Klostermann & Moeinirad, 2019; Mann et al., 2019; though 
see; Roca et al., 2013). Therefore, it is possible that the scouts 
had a retrospective preference for players with good percep­
tual-cognitive skills.
Interestingly, the decision-making performance scores were 
not significant contributors to the predictive model. Of course, 
it is possible that our sample was simply too homogeneous in 
terms of skills to find differences in their decision-making. 
However, it is also possible that the screen-based temporal 
occlusion approach we used failed to reflect the on-ice abilities 
of the players (Mann & Savelsbergh, 2015; Mann et al., 2010). 
We chose this approach because we had only a few minutes to 
test each player, and that similarly short tasks have yielded 
expertise differences (e.g., O’Connor et al., 2016). Two main 
reasons underlie this explanation. First, temporal occlusion 
has been questioned with respect to its ability to accurately 
capture in situ perceptual-cognitive skills in sports (Mann et al., 
2010; van der Kamp et al., 2008). These doubts are mainly raised 
in light of the two-visual-systems hypothesis, suggesting that 
actually performing an action changes the way humans look 
and decide upon a situation (Goodale & Milner, 1992; Mann 
et al., 2021). Second, it is possible that the final frame of the

Identifying latent subpopulations of players
In addition to the prediction approach, we also attempted to 
uncover latent sub-populations of athletes among all late draf­
tees. This was done to verify whether sleepers could be identi­
fied without informing the algorithm in advance who should be 
considered a sleeper. The results suggest that late-drafted 
players can be separated into two latent sub-populations that 
are largely distinguishable based on their self-regulation skills. 
This approach was surprisingly successful in identifying slee­
pers: the sub-population with higher self-regulatory skills 
included a much larger proportion of sleepers. This sub- 
population included 14/15 sleepers (30.4% of all the players 
in this subpopulation), whereas the one with lower self- 
regulation scores included only one sleeper (4.2% of the sub­
population). It may be that the overall construct of self- 
regulation could help detect talent, especially when it comes 
to sleepers, but that it may result in a considerable proportion 
of false positives when compared to a model that also includes 
other variables (e.g., gaze).
Notably, the two sub-populations were similar in terms of 
draft rank. This is important because the spread of draft rank 
from the third to the 14th round is large, with 18 players drafted 
each round. Clearly, even though we considered all those 
players as potential sleepers, players drafted in the third 
round are expected to have more potential than those drafted 
in the 14th. Results showing that the two subpopulations were 
similar in terms of average draft rank gives reassurance that the 
differences found in self-regulation are not a mere artefact of 
expected potential, but rather a demonstration of actual 
performance.
Implication for athletes’ development
Beyond informing scouts about the factors that characterise slee­
pers, the present results can also help to improve athlete devel­
opment. For instance, coaches and parents could focus on 
teaching self-regulation skills to young athletes given that those 
skills appear to be associated with future success. Similarly, teach­
ing young players to perform few ocular fixations on many AOIs 
while playing (or at least when watching ice-hockey video 
sequences) could improve the perceptual-cognitive skills of 
players. Importantly, although it was shown that three main pre­
dictors contributed to the identification of sleepers (i.e., self- 
regulation planning, number of AOIs, number of fixations), gen­
eralising these results to all future ice-hockey players may not be 
appropriate because they were derived from means calculated on 
groups. This could falsely lead to the idea that a putative optimal 
perceptual-cognitive pattern exists (Caballero et al., 2019). Indeed, 
some evidence in sports suggest that individual differences exist 
among experts as they reflect how each individual adapts to their 
performance environment (Dicks et al., 2010).
Limitations
The novel longitudinal approach presented herein is not with­
out limitations. First, the generalisability of the results to other 
contexts (e.g., women hockey, selections for the NHL at 18 
years old, other invasion team sports) is to be taken cau­
tiously. Indeed, the cognitive status of 15 year old teenagers 
is often in development (Côté et al., 2012), and psychological 
characteristics contributing to success at an adult level (e.g., in 
the NHL) may not be the same as the ones required to per­
form at the Major Junior level. While we have no reason to 
believe that our results would differ for other invasive sports, 
or even for other forms of ice-hockey (e.g., for women or 
other age groups), caution is of course required in overgener­
alizing the results given that the video sequences presented 
on screen depicted men hockey, and playing style and psy­
chological and perceptual-cognitive demands tend to differ 
according to age and gender in invasion team sports 
(Braakhuis et al., 2015; Scanlan et al., 2015). Second, the 
context of the experiment where the athletes thought they 
were coming to a typical scouting interview may have altered 
some of their answers. It is possible that the players provided 
answers that they thought would show themselves in 
a positive light. For example, even though athletes were 
assured that their answers would not impact their selection, 
it is possible that they over-rated their self-regulation abilities. 
However, this should have been true for both the sleepers 
and the other late-draftees, and sleepers still recorded higher 
scores. Third, the low number of tests may raise doubts about 
how well we captured the holistic psychological profile of the 
athletes. The self-regulation measures combined with the 
perceptual-cognitive task took approximately 45 minutes to 
complete, which was already longer than the time the scout­
ing team initially allowed us. This provided a somewhat 
biased snapshot of each player’s psychological characteristics, 
without being able to fully conclude that they are the only 
psychological skills that may help detect sleepers (e.g., grit, 
self-confidence, personality; Gee et al., 2010; Lemoyne et al., 
2022). Fourth, the low Cronbach’s alpha of the self-monitoring 
subscale warrants caution in the interpretation of the related 
results. Still, this subscale was weakly correlated with the 
performance variable (see Table 1). It seems unlikely that 
a measure with a stronger validity would have led to stronger 
correlations, given that the other self-regulation subscales 
also have weak links with performance, while being well 
related with each other. Importantly, self-monitoring was 
also not linked with performance in other studies (see 
Jonker et al., 2010, 2011) where this subscale had good 
enough Cronbach’s scores. Finally, modern advanced statistics 
in ice-hockey can better reflect performance compared to 
what might be possible by measuring the number of goals, 
assists or differential (e.g., the share of ranking points attrib­
uted to each player; Kubatko, 2023). However, these metrics

Conclusion
The present study investigated whether “sleeping talent” could be 
identified based on psychological characteristics. The main find­
ings were that psychological characteristics, especially gaze beha­
viour and self-regulation skills, were predictive of high 
achievement three years later among late-drafted ice-hockey 
players. These results indicate that the measurement of players’ 
psychological profiles could help scouts make selections, espe­
cially in the later rounds of a draft.
